# 15.05 — High-Risk AI Systems

## Part 1 — Foundations, Legal Scope and High-Risk Classification

## 1.01 Purpose of High-Risk AI Governance

High-risk AI systems represent one of the most significant compliance categories under the EU AI Act.

Unlike prohibited AI practices, high-risk AI systems are **permitted**, but they are subject to extensive mandatory requirements designed to address risks to:

* health;
* safety;
* fundamental rights.

For AI Security Governance and GRC, high-risk AI therefore represents a central regulatory control domain.

The governance objective is:

> **Identify high-risk AI systems accurately and ensure that the applicable legal obligations are implemented throughout their lifecycle.**

High-risk classification should not be treated merely as a label. It determines the regulatory control environment that applies to the AI system.

---

## 1.02 Legal Foundation

The primary legal foundation for high-risk classification is **Article 6 of the EU AI Act**, together with **Annex I** and **Annex III**.

Article 6 establishes two principal pathways through which an AI system may be considered high-risk:

1. AI systems that are safety components of products, or products themselves, covered by specified Union harmonisation legislation listed in Annex I and subject to third-party conformity assessment requirements.
2. AI systems falling within the use cases listed in Annex III, subject to the conditions and exceptions established by the Regulation.

The organization should therefore avoid treating "high-risk AI" as a generic internal risk category.

It is a **specific legal classification**.

---

## 1.03 High-Risk Does Not Mean Prohibited

A fundamental distinction is:

| Category                    | Regulatory Treatment                                |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Prohibited AI               | Use is prohibited within the applicable legal scope |
| High-risk AI                | Use is permitted subject to mandatory requirements  |
| Transparency-relevant AI    | Specific transparency obligations apply             |
| Minimal/no significant risk | Generally fewer AI Act-specific obligations         |

An organization should never classify a prohibited practice as high-risk merely because it believes additional controls could make the use acceptable.

Conversely, high-risk classification should not be interpreted as a prohibition.

---

## 1.04 The Two High-Risk Pathways

The high-risk framework can be represented as:

### Pathway 1 — Product-Related AI

**AI system**

→ safety component or product

→ covered by specified Annex I legislation

→ third-party conformity assessment requirement

→ **High-risk**

### Pathway 2 — Annex III AI

**AI system**

→ falls within an Annex III use case

→ Article 6 conditions assessed

→ applicable exception considered

→ **High-risk or not high-risk**

This two-pathway model should be embedded into the organization's classification methodology.

---

## 1.05 Annex I Product-Related Pathway

The first pathway concerns AI systems associated with products regulated under specified Union harmonisation legislation.

Examples of legislative areas referenced in Annex I include:

* machinery;
* toys;
* recreational craft;
* lifts;
* equipment and protective systems intended for use in potentially explosive atmospheres;
* radio equipment;
* pressure equipment;
* personal protective equipment;
* medical devices;
* in vitro diagnostic medical devices;
* other specified regulated products.

The exact legal assessment must be performed against the legislation and conditions identified in the current version of Annex I.

---

## 1.06 Third-Party Conformity Assessment

The product-related pathway depends in part on whether the applicable product legislation requires a **third-party conformity assessment**.

This is an important distinction.

The organization should not assume:

> "AI is used in a regulated product, therefore it is automatically high-risk."

The assessment must establish whether the conditions in Article 6 and the applicable Annex I legislation are satisfied.

This requires coordination between:

* Product Compliance;
* Legal;
* Engineering;
* Regulatory Affairs;
* AI Governance;
* Quality Assurance.

---

## 1.07 Annex III Pathway

The second major pathway is based on specified AI use cases listed in Annex III.

The areas include:

1. biometrics;
2. critical infrastructure;
3. education and vocational training;
4. employment, worker management and access to self-employment;
5. access to essential private services and essential public services and benefits;
6. law enforcement;
7. migration, asylum and border control management;
8. administration of justice and democratic processes.

The organization must assess the specific use case rather than relying solely on the industry in which the AI operates.

---

## 1.08 Sector Is Not the Same as High-Risk Classification

A common error is:

> "This is an HR system, therefore it is high-risk."

or:

> "This is a healthcare system, therefore it is high-risk."

The correct approach is to determine whether the **specific AI use case** satisfies the applicable legal criteria.

For example, an AI system used by a company may operate in the employment sector but perform a function that does not fall within the relevant Annex III category.

The classification must therefore be based on the actual:

* purpose;
* function;
* deployment;
* affected persons;
* decision;
* legal category.

---

## 1.09 Biometrics

Annex III includes certain biometric-related AI systems.

The assessment may involve systems used for:

* remote biometric identification;
* biometric categorization;
* emotion recognition;
* other specified biometric purposes.

However, the organization must distinguish:

* authentication;
* identification;
* verification;
* categorization;
* emotion recognition;
* other biometric processing.

These functions have different regulatory implications.

---

## 1.10 Critical Infrastructure

Annex III includes specified AI systems intended to be used as safety components in the management and operation of critical digital infrastructure, road traffic, or the supply of water, gas, heating or electricity.

The organization should therefore determine:

* infrastructure involved;
* AI function;
* safety role;
* operational impact;
* whether the system is actually covered by the relevant Annex III entry.

A generic statement such as "critical infrastructure AI" is insufficient.

---

## 1.11 Education and Vocational Training

Annex III covers specified AI systems used in education and vocational training.

Relevant examples may include systems used for:

* determining access or admission;
* evaluating learning outcomes;
* assessing educational level;
* directing learning;
* monitoring behavior in specified contexts;
* evaluating individuals for educational opportunities.

The assessment must use the precise legal wording and current Annex III conditions rather than treating every educational AI application as high-risk.

---

## 1.12 Employment and Worker Management

Employment is one of the most important high-risk areas for enterprise AI governance.

Annex III includes specified AI systems used in:

* recruitment;
* selection;
* analyzing or filtering job applications;
* evaluating candidates;
* making decisions affecting employment relationships;
* task allocation based on individual behavior or personal characteristics;
* monitoring and evaluating performance or behavior.

Organizations should therefore perform dedicated AI assessments across:

* Applicant Tracking Systems;
* recruitment platforms;
* HR analytics;
* workforce management;
* performance management;
* employee monitoring.

---

## 1.13 Employment AI Governance

HR departments should not be allowed to procure AI independently of enterprise AI governance.

The organization should maintain an inventory of AI used for:

* candidate screening;
* ranking;
* recruitment;
* promotion;
* performance evaluation;
* workforce allocation;
* termination-related decisions;
* employee monitoring.

Each system should undergo regulatory classification before the relevant use begins.

---

## 1.14 Essential Services and Benefits

Annex III includes specified AI systems used in relation to access to essential private services and essential public services and benefits.

Examples within the statutory categories include certain systems associated with:

* access to essential services;
* evaluation of eligibility;
* creditworthiness or credit scoring;
* insurance-related risk evaluation or pricing;
* emergency response prioritization.

The organization must determine whether the particular system satisfies the relevant Annex III description.

---

## 1.15 Law Enforcement

Annex III includes specified AI systems used by or on behalf of law-enforcement authorities.

Potential areas include:

* assessing risks relating to natural persons;
* evaluating evidence reliability;
* detecting or investigating offences;
* profiling;
* polygraph-like or similar inference systems;
* evaluating characteristics in specified law-enforcement contexts.

Law-enforcement use requires particularly careful legal analysis because the AI Act also contains prohibited practices and additional safeguards applicable to certain biometric and law-enforcement applications.

---

## 1.16 Migration, Asylum and Border Control

Annex III identifies specified AI systems used in:

* migration;
* asylum;
* border control management.

Potential systems may assist with:

* risk assessment;
* application evaluation;
* identity verification;
* document examination;
* eligibility-related processes.

Organizations should identify both the AI function and the institutional role of the deploying organization.

---

## 1.17 Justice and Democratic Processes

Annex III includes certain AI systems used in:

* assistance to judicial authorities;
* alternative dispute resolution;
* investigation and interpretation of facts and law;
* application of law to concrete facts;
* influencing elections or voting behavior, subject to the conditions in the Regulation.

This area requires heightened governance because AI may affect:

* access to justice;
* legal rights;
* democratic participation;
* political decision-making.

---

## 1.18 High-Risk Classification Is Context-Dependent

High-risk classification should consider the context in which the AI system operates.

The same underlying technology can have different regulatory classifications depending on:

* intended purpose;
* deployment environment;
* affected persons;
* decisions supported;
* level of influence;
* sector;
* safety function;
* applicable legal framework.

This is why the AI inventory should capture the **actual use case**, not merely the model name.

---

## 1.19 Intended Purpose

Intended purpose is a central concept in high-risk classification.

The organization should document:

* what the system is designed to accomplish;
* who will use it;
* who will be affected;
* what decisions it supports;
* what outputs it generates;
* how outputs are used;
* what actions may follow.

A vague description such as:

> "AI-powered HR analytics"

is insufficient.

A stronger description would explain the specific process and decision the system supports.

---

## 1.20 Provider and Deployer Roles

High-risk governance must also identify the organization's role.

Relevant roles may include:

* provider;
* deployer;
* importer;
* distributor;
* product manufacturer;
* authorized representative;
* affected downstream organization.

The same AI system may involve multiple actors with different legal obligations.

Therefore:

> **High-risk classification and organizational role determination should be performed together.**

---

## 1.21 High-Risk Classification and AI Lifecycle

High-risk classification should be established early.

A recommended lifecycle is:

**Concept**

→ classification screening

↓

**Design**

→ intended-purpose validation

↓

**Development**

→ regulatory requirements mapped

↓

**Testing**

→ compliance verification

↓

**Deployment**

→ conformity/governance gate

↓

**Production**

→ monitoring

↓

**Change**

→ reassessment

This prevents classification from becoming an after-the-fact compliance exercise.

---

## 1.22 Exceptions to Annex III High-Risk Classification

Article 6 establishes specific conditions under which certain AI systems falling within Annex III may not be considered high-risk.

The exception framework is not a general organizational exemption.

The relevant assessment considers the system's purpose and functionality, including circumstances in which the system:

* performs a narrow procedural task;
* improves the result of a previously completed human activity;
* detects decision-making patterns without replacing or influencing the human assessment;
* performs a preparatory task to an assessment relevant to an Annex III use case.

The Regulation also establishes limitations, including that the exception does not apply where the AI system performs profiling of natural persons.

---

## 1.23 Exception Documentation

An organization relying on an Article 6 exception should document:

* applicable Annex III category;
* exact system function;
* intended purpose;
* human role;
* decision influence;
* applicable exception;
* legal reasoning;
* evidence;
* approval.

A simple statement such as:

> "Not high-risk because human approval is required"

is insufficient.

Human involvement alone does not automatically remove a system from high-risk classification.

---

## 1.24 High-Risk and Human Decision-Making

The existence of a human decision-maker should be assessed carefully.

Relevant questions include:

* What information does the human receive?
* Does the human understand the AI output?
* Can the human challenge it?
* Can the human override it?
* Does the human actually exercise independent judgment?
* Does the AI materially influence the outcome?

The organization should distinguish meaningful human assessment from nominal approval.

---

## 1.25 Profiling

Profiling is particularly important in high-risk classification.

Where the relevant exception framework excludes profiling, organizations should determine whether the system:

* evaluates characteristics of a natural person;
* analyzes behavior;
* predicts characteristics;
* generates individual scores;
* creates individual risk profiles.

The technical architecture and actual data processing should be examined rather than relying on vendor terminology.

---

## 1.26 High-Risk Classification and Fundamental Rights

High-risk AI governance is closely connected to fundamental-rights protection.

Potential impacts include:

* discrimination;
* privacy;
* employment rights;
* access to services;
* education;
* legal rights;
* democratic participation;
* personal autonomy.

The AI Act requires specific controls for high-risk AI designed to address risks to health, safety and fundamental rights.

Organizations should therefore integrate high-risk classification with:

* fundamental-rights assessment;
* privacy;
* security;
* risk management;
* compliance.

---

## 1.27 High-Risk Classification and Security

Security should be considered as part of the high-risk control environment.

Relevant security concerns include:

* adversarial attacks;
* data poisoning;
* model manipulation;
* unauthorized access;
* model extraction;
* prompt injection;
* integrity failures;
* availability failures;
* cybersecurity incidents.

Security risk, however, should not be confused with the legal determination of whether a system is high-risk.

---

## 1.28 High-Risk Classification and Risk Management

A mature organization should maintain two related but distinct assessments:

### Regulatory Classification

**Is the system legally high-risk under the AI Act?**

### Enterprise Risk Assessment

**What risks does this AI system create for the organization?**

The answers may not be identical.

An AI system could be legally high-risk but have relatively low enterprise exposure in a particular deployment.

Conversely, an AI system outside the high-risk category may still create substantial cybersecurity, privacy, operational or reputational risk.

---

## 1.29 High-Risk Classification and Compliance Obligations

Once a system is classified as high-risk, the organization must determine the obligations applicable to its role.

Depending on the organization and system, these can include requirements concerning:

* risk management;
* data and data governance;
* technical documentation;
* record-keeping;
* transparency;
* human oversight;
* accuracy;
* robustness;
* cybersecurity;
* quality management;
* conformity assessment;
* registration;
* post-market monitoring;
* incident reporting.

The classification decision therefore becomes the entry point to a broader compliance-control framework.

---

## 1.30 Classification Decision

The final classification should be recorded as a formal governance decision.

The record should state:

> **High-risk — Article 6(1)**

or

> **High-risk — Article 6(2) / Annex III**

or

> **Not high-risk — documented Article 6 assessment and applicable rationale**

Where uncertainty remains, the decision should be escalated rather than silently resolved in favor of deployment.

---

## 1.31 Current Applicability and Transition Management

The organization must distinguish the legal classification of a system from the date on which particular high-risk obligations become applicable.

The 2026 AI Omnibus introduced changes to the application timeline for certain high-risk provisions. In particular, specified Annex III high-risk rules apply from **2 December 2027**, while certain high-risk AI systems embedded in products covered by Annex I legislation have an application date of **2 August 2028**.

This means GRC teams should maintain both:

* **classification status**; and
* **applicable-obligation date**.

A future compliance date should not be confused with absence of regulatory relevance.

---

## 1.32 High-Risk AI Register

Organizations should maintain a dedicated register containing:

* AI system ID;
* system name;
* owner;
* provider;
* deployer;
* intended purpose;
* legal pathway;
* Annex I/III reference;
* classification decision;
* applicable obligations;
* conformity-assessment status;
* risk-management status;
* human-oversight status;
* documentation status;
* monitoring status;
* next review.

This register should integrate with the broader AI inventory.

---

## 1.33 High-Risk Classification as a GRC Control

The control objective should be:

> **Ensure that every AI system potentially meeting the legal criteria for high-risk classification is accurately assessed against Article 6 and the applicable Annex I or Annex III requirements, with documented evidence, governance approval and lifecycle reassessment.**

This control should operate before deployment and whenever:

* intended purpose changes;
* functionality changes;
* deployment changes;
* regulatory requirements change;
* the model changes materially;
* affected persons change;
* organizational role changes.

---

## 1.34 High-Risk Governance Principle

The foundational governance principle is:

> **High-risk classification is a legal and contextual determination based on the AI system's actual purpose, function and regulatory pathway—not simply the technology, industry or perceived severity of risk.**

A mature AI Security Governance program should therefore treat high-risk classification as the gateway into the organization's enhanced regulatory control environment.

# Part 2 — High-Risk Assessment, Control Design and Implementation

## 2.35 High-Risk Assessment Methodology

High-risk assessment should use a standardized and evidence-based methodology.

The recommended process is:

**AI System Identified**

↓

**Regulatory Scope Confirmed**

↓

**Organizational Role Determined**

↓

**Intended Purpose Established**

↓

**Annex I Pathway Tested**

↓

**Annex III Pathway Tested**

↓

**Article 6 Conditions and Exceptions Assessed**

↓

**High-Risk Decision**

↓

**Applicable Obligations Mapped**

↓

**Governance Approval**

↓

**Evidence Retained**

The process should be performed before the relevant deployment or market activity and repeated when defined reassessment triggers occur.

---

## 2.36 High-Risk Assessment Intake

The assessment should begin with a standardized intake record.

Minimum fields should include:

* AI system name;
* unique AI system ID;
* business owner;
* technical owner;
* provider;
* deployer;
* manufacturer where applicable;
* model name;
* model version;
* intended purpose;
* actual use;
* deployment location;
* business process;
* affected persons;
* sector;
* AI outputs;
* decisions influenced;
* level of autonomy;
* biometric functionality;
* profiling functionality;
* regulated product relationship;
* data categories;
* third-party components.

Incomplete information should prevent the assessment from being treated as final.

---

## 2.37 Determine the Regulatory Role

Before assessing high-risk obligations, determine the organization's role.

The organization may be:

* provider;
* deployer;
* product manufacturer;
* importer;
* distributor;
* authorized representative;
* downstream provider;
* another actor defined by the Regulation.

Role determination is important because obligations differ according to the organization's position in the AI value chain.

A GRC system should therefore maintain both:

**AI Classification**

and

**Regulatory Role**.

---

## 2.38 Annex I Assessment

The Annex I pathway should be assessed first for AI systems associated with regulated products.

The assessment should determine:

1. Is the AI system itself a product or a safety component?
2. Is the product covered by legislation listed in Annex I?
3. Does the relevant product legislation apply to the product?
4. Does the applicable legislation require a third-party conformity assessment?
5. Does Article 6(1) therefore establish high-risk classification?

The assessment should cite the relevant legislation rather than simply recording "regulated product."

---

## 2.39 Product Relationship Analysis

The organization should document the relationship between the AI system and the regulated product.

For example:

**AI System**

→ operates independently

versus

**AI System**

→ safety component of a regulated product.

The distinction can materially affect classification.

The assessment should identify:

* product;
* AI component;
* safety function;
* integration;
* manufacturer;
* conformity process.

---

## 2.40 Annex III Assessment

Where Article 6(2) is potentially applicable, the organization should map the AI system to the relevant Annex III area.

The assessment should document:

* Annex III section;
* specific use case;
* system function;
* intended purpose;
* affected persons;
* decision influenced;
* organizational role.

The assessment should use the actual wording and scope of the applicable Annex III entry.

---

## 2.41 Annex III Decision Matrix

A practical matrix can be used:

| Assessment Question                  | Result           |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------- |
| AI system within AI Act scope?       | Yes/No           |
| Annex III area applicable?           | Yes/No           |
| Specific Annex III use case matched? | Yes/No           |
| Intended purpose matched?            | Yes/No           |
| Relevant exception?                  | Yes/No           |
| Profiling involved?                  | Yes/No           |
| High-risk conclusion                 | Yes/No/Uncertain |
| Legal review required?               | Yes/No           |

This creates a consistent audit trail.

---

## 2.42 Biometrics Assessment

For biometric systems, identify the precise functionality.

Questions should include:

* Is the system identifying a person?
* Is it verifying identity?
* Is it categorizing individuals?
* Is it inferring characteristics?
* Is it performing emotion recognition?
* Is it used remotely?
* Is it used in a publicly accessible space?
* Who deploys it?
* What is its purpose?

The organization should avoid using "biometric AI" as a sufficient classification description.

---

## 2.43 Critical Infrastructure Assessment

For critical infrastructure use cases, establish:

* infrastructure type;
* AI function;
* safety role;
* operational impact;
* affected services;
* deployment context.

The assessment should distinguish AI used directly as a relevant safety component from AI used for unrelated administrative or analytical activities.

---

## 2.44 Education Assessment

For education-related systems, document:

* educational process;
* learner population;
* AI function;
* decision or recommendation;
* effect on educational opportunity;
* human involvement;
* intended purpose.

Potential applications should be evaluated individually rather than assuming that every educational AI system is high-risk.

---

## 2.45 Employment Assessment

For employment systems, the assessment should identify whether AI supports:

* recruitment;
* candidate selection;
* application filtering;
* candidate evaluation;
* employment decisions;
* task allocation;
* employee monitoring;
* performance evaluation.

The organization should identify the exact employment decision influenced by the AI.

---

## 2.46 Essential Services Assessment

For AI used in access to essential services or benefits, identify:

* service;
* affected person;
* eligibility decision;
* risk assessment;
* score;
* recommendation;
* final decision;
* human involvement.

Particular attention should be given to systems that can materially affect an individual's access to important services.

---

## 2.47 Law-Enforcement Assessment

Law-enforcement AI should be mapped against the exact Annex III use case.

The assessment may include:

* criminal-risk assessment;
* evidence evaluation;
* investigation support;
* profiling;
* polygraph-related inference;
* victim-related assessment;
* crime-related analytical systems.

The organization should separately screen the same system against Article 5 because a system may present both high-risk and prohibited-practice considerations.

---

## 2.48 Migration and Border-Control Assessment

The assessment should identify:

* migration process;
* asylum process;
* border-control process;
* identity function;
* risk assessment;
* eligibility determination;
* affected persons;
* decision influence.

Because these systems may affect vulnerable individuals, governance should involve Legal, Privacy, Compliance and appropriate human-rights expertise.

---

## 2.49 Justice and Democratic-Process Assessment

For judicial or democratic-process AI, identify:

* legal process;
* decision supported;
* legal interpretation function;
* affected parties;
* human authority;
* democratic process affected.

Where AI influences judicial or democratic processes, governance should apply enhanced scrutiny.

---

## 2.50 Article 6 Exception Assessment

Where an Annex III system appears potentially high-risk, the organization should explicitly evaluate whether the Article 6 exception applies.

The analysis should document:

* exact Annex III use case;
* system purpose;
* system function;
* degree of influence;
* human decision-making;
* procedural nature;
* preparatory nature;
* whether the system improves a previously completed human activity;
* whether profiling occurs.

The exception should be supported by evidence rather than asserted.

---

## 2.51 Exception Decision Tree

A practical exception process is:

**Annex III Use Case Identified**

↓

**Does the system perform only a specified narrow function?**

→ Yes/No

↓

**Does it materially influence decision-making?**

→ Yes/No

↓

**Does it replace or influence human assessment?**

→ Yes/No

↓

**Does it perform profiling?**

→ Yes/No

↓

**Does an Article 6 exception remain legally available?**

→ Yes/No

↓

**Document Legal Rationale**

The final determination should be reviewed according to the organization's legal-governance model.

---

## 2.52 Human Influence Assessment

Human involvement should be documented using observable criteria.

Assess:

* human authority;
* information available;
* ability to override;
* ability to disregard AI output;
* time available;
* training;
* independent judgment;
* actual workflow.

The organization should distinguish:

**Human Review**

from

**Human Rubber-Stamping**.

A nominal human step should not automatically be treated as meaningful human decision-making.

---

## 2.53 Decision-Influence Scale

Organizations can use an internal scale to support consistent analysis:

### Level 0 — No Decision Influence

AI output is unrelated to a human decision.

### Level 1 — Informational

Human receives information but AI does not materially influence the decision.

### Level 2 — Advisory

AI recommends an action.

### Level 3 — Material Influence

Human decision is substantially influenced by AI output.

### Level 4 — Automated

AI determines the outcome with little or no meaningful human intervention.

This is an internal governance tool and does not replace the legal criteria of the AI Act.

---

## 2.54 Profiling Assessment

Where an Article 6 exception may be relevant, profiling should receive explicit assessment.

The organization should determine whether the AI:

* evaluates characteristics;
* analyzes behavior;
* predicts attributes;
* assigns scores;
* creates individual profiles.

The assessment should document both technical functionality and legal interpretation.

---

## 2.55 High-Risk Classification Decision Record

The final decision should contain:

* system identification;
* intended purpose;
* actual deployment;
* regulatory role;
* Annex I assessment;
* Annex III assessment;
* exception assessment;
* legal basis;
* conclusion;
* reviewer;
* approver;
* effective date;
* reassessment triggers.

A decision without supporting rationale should be treated as incomplete.

---

## 2.56 Obligation Mapping

Once high-risk classification is confirmed, the organization should map the system to applicable obligations.

The control framework should evaluate requirements relating to:

* risk management;
* data and data governance;
* technical documentation;
* record-keeping;
* transparency;
* human oversight;
* accuracy;
* robustness;
* cybersecurity;
* quality management;
* conformity assessment;
* registration;
* post-market monitoring;
* incident reporting.

The exact obligations depend on the organization's role and the applicable legal provisions.

---

## 2.57 Risk-Management System

High-risk AI providers are subject to requirements concerning a risk-management system.

Operationally, the organization should establish a lifecycle process for:

* identifying foreseeable risks;
* estimating and evaluating risks;
* implementing risk controls;
* testing controls;
* monitoring residual risk;
* updating the assessment.

Risk management should be connected to the AI system's intended purpose and reasonably foreseeable misuse.

---

## 2.58 Risk Identification

High-risk AI risk identification should consider:

### Technical Risks

* model errors;
* robustness failures;
* adversarial attacks;
* data quality;
* distribution shift.

### Human Risks

* misuse;
* overreliance;
* automation bias;
* inadequate oversight.

### Fundamental-Rights Risks

* discrimination;
* unequal treatment;
* privacy impacts;
* unjustified restrictions.

### Operational Risks

* availability;
* integration;
* workflow failure;
* dependency.

### Security Risks

* manipulation;
* unauthorized access;
* data poisoning;
* model theft.

---

## 2.59 Data and Data-Governance Controls

Where applicable, high-risk AI requires appropriate data and data-governance practices.

Organizations should assess:

* relevance;
* representativeness;
* quality;
* completeness;
* errors;
* bias;
* provenance;
* preprocessing;
* labeling;
* validation.

Data controls should be documented throughout the lifecycle.

---

## 2.60 Bias and Data Quality

Organizations should establish controls to identify and address harmful data-quality problems.

Testing can include:

* demographic performance analysis;
* missing-data analysis;
* label-quality review;
* sampling analysis;
* outlier analysis;
* subgroup performance;
* historical bias assessment.

The purpose is not simply to make the model statistically accurate.

The objective is to support compliant, reliable and appropriate AI operation.

---

## 2.61 Technical Documentation

High-risk AI providers must maintain appropriate technical documentation.

The documentation should describe relevant aspects of:

* system architecture;
* intended purpose;
* development process;
* data;
* model;
* validation;
* testing;
* performance;
* limitations;
* risk management;
* monitoring.

Organizations should establish document ownership and version control.

---

## 2.62 Automatic Record-Keeping

Where applicable, high-risk AI systems should support automatic logging.

Logs should allow relevant events to be reconstructed.

Depending on the system, this can include:

* inputs;
* outputs;
* decisions;
* timestamps;
* users;
* system version;
* significant events;
* overrides;
* errors.

Logging must be implemented consistently with applicable privacy and data-protection requirements.

---

## 2.63 Transparency to Deployers

High-risk AI providers have obligations concerning information supplied to deployers.

Organizations acting as providers should ensure that relevant instructions and information enable appropriate deployment.

Organizations acting as deployers should ensure that they understand:

* system purpose;
* capabilities;
* limitations;
* operating conditions;
* required human oversight;
* applicable instructions.

---

## 2.64 Human Oversight Controls

High-risk AI should be designed and operated with appropriate human oversight.

Controls may include:

* trained operators;
* escalation procedures;
* override capability;
* intervention mechanisms;
* decision review;
* monitoring for automation bias;
* authority to stop system operation.

Human oversight should be meaningful rather than purely procedural.

---

## 2.65 Accuracy, Robustness and Cybersecurity

High-risk AI must address:

* appropriate levels of accuracy;
* robustness;
* cybersecurity.

Security controls should include, as appropriate:

* secure development;
* access control;
* vulnerability management;
* adversarial testing;
* integrity protection;
* model protection;
* logging;
* incident response.

The security program should be integrated with AI-specific risk management.

---

## 2.66 Quality Management System

Providers of high-risk AI systems are subject to quality-management requirements.

A QMS should address relevant processes such as:

* regulatory compliance;
* design;
* development;
* testing;
* validation;
* data management;
* risk management;
* change management;
* post-market monitoring;
* corrective action.

The exact requirements should be mapped to the organization's role and applicable AI Act provisions.

---

## 2.67 Conformity-Assessment Planning

Where applicable, high-risk AI providers must determine the appropriate conformity-assessment pathway.

The organization should establish:

* applicable conformity route;
* responsible function;
* documentation;
* assessment body where required;
* evidence;
* declaration requirements;
* registration requirements.

The conformity process should be integrated into product release governance.

---

## 2.68 High-Risk Deployment Gate

A high-risk AI system should not enter production without confirming that applicable pre-deployment requirements have been addressed.

A deployment gate may require:

* classification approval;
* risk-management documentation;
* data controls;
* technical documentation;
* testing;
* human oversight;
* cybersecurity;
* transparency information;
* conformity assessment where applicable;
* registration where applicable.

The exact gate should reflect whether the organization is a provider, deployer or another regulated actor.

---

## 2.69 Change Management

Material changes should trigger regulatory reassessment.

Potential triggers include:

* new intended purpose;
* new user population;
* new decision;
* new data;
* model replacement;
* material retraining;
* new deployment location;
* increased autonomy;
* new integration;
* new regulatory requirements.

The organization should define what constitutes a material change.

---

## 2.70 High-Risk Classification Reassessment

Reassessment should occur when:

* system purpose changes;
* Annex I relationship changes;
* Annex III use changes;
* exceptions may no longer apply;
* profiling is introduced;
* decision influence increases;
* affected persons change;
* legal requirements change.

The classification record should maintain historical versions.

---

This creates a traceable compliance architecture.

## 2.73 High-Risk Evidence Requirements

Evidence should include, as applicable:

* classification assessment;
* legal analysis;
* risk-management records;
* datasets and data-governance records;
* technical documentation;
* testing;
* validation;
* logs;
* human-oversight records;
* cybersecurity testing;
* conformity documentation;
* monitoring records;
* incident records;
* change history.

Evidence should be version controlled.

---

## 2.74 High-Risk Compliance Status

The GRC system should distinguish:

* Classified;
* Under Assessment;
* High-Risk Confirmed;
* High-Risk Exception;
* Obligations In Progress;
* Deployment Approved;
* Deployment Blocked;
* Non-Compliant;
* Remediation;
* Retired.

This prevents a simple "high-risk = compliant" status from being used.

---

## 2.75 High-Risk Compliance Principle

The fundamental implementation principle is:

> **High-risk classification is the beginning of enhanced compliance, not the end of the assessment.**

Once classification is established, the organization must determine which obligations apply, assign accountable owners, implement controls, collect evidence and continuously monitor the system.

The classification decision therefore acts as a **gateway control** into the high-risk AI compliance lifecycle.
