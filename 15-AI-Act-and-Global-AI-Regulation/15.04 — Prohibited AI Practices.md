# 15.04 — Prohibited AI Practices

## Part 1 — Foundations, Legal Scope and Prohibited AI Categories

## 1.01 Purpose of Prohibited AI Governance

The EU AI Act establishes a category of AI practices that are prohibited because they are considered sufficiently harmful, abusive or incompatible with fundamental rights and Union values.

These practices are fundamentally different from **high-risk AI systems**.

A high-risk AI system is permitted subject to prescribed requirements.

A prohibited AI practice is generally **not permitted to be placed on the market, put into service or used** within the circumstances covered by Article 5.

Article 5 therefore represents the strongest regulatory intervention in the EU AI Act's risk-based framework.

For an AI Security Governance and GRC function, prohibited-practice screening should therefore operate as a **preventive deployment control**, rather than merely as a post-deployment compliance assessment.

---

## 1.02 Article 5 as a Regulatory Boundary

The EU AI Act establishes prohibited AI practices in Article 5.

The core governance principle is:

> **An organization must determine whether an AI use case falls within a prohibited practice before permitting the relevant activity to proceed.**

This creates a regulatory boundary:

**Prohibited**

→ activity must not proceed within the prohibited scope.

**High-Risk**

→ activity may proceed subject to applicable requirements.

**Transparency-Relevant**

→ activity may proceed subject to applicable transparency obligations.

**Minimal/No Significant Risk**

→ generally fewer AI Act-specific requirements.

The distinction is critical because organizations should not attempt to "control" a prohibited practice in the same way they would control an otherwise permissible high-risk system.

---

## 1.03 Prohibited Does Not Mean "Dangerous AI"

The term **prohibited AI practice** should not be interpreted as meaning that the AI technology itself is inherently dangerous.

The legal assessment concerns the **AI system and the specific practice in which it is used**.

The same underlying technology may therefore have different regulatory consequences depending on:

* purpose;
* deployment context;
* affected persons;
* functionality;
* method of use;
* decision being influenced;
* data involved;
* intended or actual effects.

For example, an AI capability capable of analyzing human behavior is not automatically prohibited.

The organization must determine whether its actual use falls within a specific Article 5 prohibition.

---

## 1.04 Prohibited-Practice Screening

Every AI system entering the organization's inventory should undergo an initial prohibited-practice screen.

A practical sequence is:

**AI System Identified**

↓

**Intended Purpose Established**

↓

**Deployment Context Established**

↓

**Affected Persons Identified**

↓

**Article 5 Screening**

↓

**Potential Prohibition?**

→ **No:** Continue classification.

→ **Yes/Uncertain:** Stop or hold deployment and escalate.

The screening should occur before production deployment and whenever a material change could alter the legal characterization.

---

## 1.05 Current Article 5 Structure

Article 5 covers several categories of prohibited AI practices.

The principal categories include:

1. manipulative, deceptive or subliminal techniques causing significant harm;
2. exploitation of vulnerabilities causing significant harm;
3. certain forms of social scoring;
4. certain criminal-risk assessments based solely on profiling or personality characteristics;
5. certain creation or expansion of facial-recognition databases through untargeted scraping;
6. emotion recognition in workplaces and educational institutions, subject to specified exceptions;
7. certain biometric categorization based on sensitive or protected characteristics;
8. certain real-time remote biometric identification uses by law enforcement, subject to the Act's specific framework and exceptions.

The 2026 amendments additionally prohibit specified AI-generated or manipulated **non-consensual intimate material** and certain **child sexual abuse material**, with those new prohibitions applying from the date specified in the amended Regulation.

The organization should therefore maintain a version-controlled Article 5 control matrix rather than relying indefinitely on an original 2024 checklist.

---

## 1.06 Manipulative and Deceptive AI

Article 5 prohibits certain AI systems that use subliminal techniques or purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques where the required legal conditions are met.

The prohibition is not simply:

> "AI that persuades people."

The legal test is more specific.

The relevant practice involves techniques that materially distort behavior by appreciably impairing the person's ability to make an informed decision, causing or being reasonably likely to cause significant harm.

Therefore, governance should examine:

* technique used;
* purpose;
* effect;
* ability to make an informed decision;
* behavioral distortion;
* causal relationship;
* actual or reasonably foreseeable harm.

---

## 1.07 Manipulation Versus Ordinary Personalization

Organizations should distinguish prohibited manipulation from ordinary personalization.

Personalization can include:

* recommending content;
* adjusting user interfaces;
* customizing product recommendations;
* selecting advertisements;
* adapting communications.

These activities are not automatically prohibited.

The governance question is whether the particular AI practice satisfies the legal elements of the Article 5 prohibition.

A classification methodology should therefore avoid broad rules such as:

> "Behavioral personalization = prohibited."

Instead, it should require assessment against the actual statutory criteria.

---

## 1.08 Subliminal Techniques

Article 5 specifically addresses AI systems using subliminal techniques that operate beyond a person's consciousness, when the remaining statutory conditions are satisfied.

This requires consideration of:

* the mechanism;
* the target;
* whether the technique operates beyond conscious awareness;
* behavioral effect;
* impact on informed decision-making;
* significant harm.

The technical assessment may require collaboration between:

* AI engineers;
* behavioral specialists;
* product teams;
* Legal;
* Compliance;
* Risk.

A simple statement that an AI system "influences users" is insufficient to establish the legal test.

---

## 1.09 Exploitation of Vulnerabilities

Article 5 also prohibits certain AI practices that exploit vulnerabilities of natural persons or specific groups arising from:

* age;
* disability;
* specific social situation;
* specific economic situation.

The prohibited practice requires the applicable behavioral-distortion and significant-harm conditions to be satisfied.

Governance should therefore identify whether the AI system specifically targets or exploits a vulnerability.

---

## 1.10 Vulnerability Is Contextual

Vulnerability should not be treated as a generic label.

The assessment should identify:

* who is affected;
* what vulnerability exists;
* why the vulnerability exists;
* whether the AI system uses that vulnerability;
* how behavior is altered;
* what harm may result.

For example, an AI system designed for a general population is not automatically prohibited merely because some users happen to be vulnerable.

The organization must examine the actual practice and the applicable legal criteria.

---

## 1.11 Social Scoring

Article 5 prohibits specified forms of social scoring.

The prohibited practice concerns AI systems used to evaluate or classify natural persons or groups over a period based on:

* social behavior;
* known personal characteristics;
* inferred characteristics;
* predicted personal or personality characteristics.

The prohibition applies where the resulting social score leads to specified detrimental or unfavorable treatment, including treatment in unrelated social contexts or treatment that is unjustified or disproportionate to the person's behavior or its seriousness.

---

## 1.12 Social Scoring Governance

Organizations should distinguish social scoring from ordinary risk scoring.

For example:

**Credit risk score**

may support a specific financial decision.

**Fraud risk score**

may support a particular fraud-prevention process.

**Social score**

can involve broader evaluation of a person's behavior or characteristics and subsequent treatment across contexts.

The regulatory assessment must therefore examine:

* what is being scored;
* why it is scored;
* what data is used;
* how long the behavior is evaluated;
* where the score is used;
* whether the score crosses contexts;
* what treatment results.

---

## 1.13 Criminal-Risk Assessment Prohibition

Article 5 also addresses certain AI systems used to assess or predict the risk that a natural person will commit a criminal offence when the assessment is based **solely** on profiling or evaluation of personality traits and characteristics.

The prohibition contains an important qualification for AI supporting human assessment where that assessment is already based on objective and verifiable facts directly linked to criminal activity.

This distinction is important for governance.

The organization should not classify every predictive law-enforcement technology as prohibited.

Instead, it should assess:

* purpose;
* data basis;
* profiling;
* personality characteristics;
* objective facts;
* human assessment;
* relationship to actual criminal activity.

---

## 1.14 Untargeted Facial-Recognition Database Scraping

Article 5 includes restrictions concerning AI systems used to create or expand facial-recognition databases through untargeted scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV footage.

This area should be assessed separately from ordinary facial-recognition functionality.

The governance assessment should identify:

* source of facial images;
* whether collection is targeted or untargeted;
* whether internet sources are used;
* whether CCTV material is used;
* purpose of database creation;
* legal basis for collection;
* geographic scope;
* affected persons.

---

## 1.15 Emotion Recognition

Article 5 prohibits certain AI systems used to infer emotions in:

* workplaces;
* educational institutions.

The prohibition is subject to specified exceptions, including certain medical or safety-related purposes.

Therefore, the governance question is not simply:

> "Does the system detect emotions?"

The assessment must consider:

* environment;
* purpose;
* persons affected;
* type of emotion inference;
* intended use;
* applicable exception.

---

## 1.16 Workplace Emotion Recognition

Workplace use requires particular attention because AI may influence:

* employee evaluation;
* productivity management;
* disciplinary processes;
* recruitment;
* promotion;
* access to opportunities;
* workplace monitoring.

Organizations should therefore identify emotion-recognition capabilities in:

* HR platforms;
* employee-monitoring systems;
* call-center analytics;
* productivity tools;
* recruitment technologies;
* security systems.

A capability hidden inside a third-party SaaS product should not escape Article 5 screening merely because it is not marketed as an "emotion AI system."

---

## 1.17 Education Emotion Recognition

Education environments similarly require careful screening.

Potentially relevant systems may include:

* student engagement analysis;
* classroom monitoring;
* examination monitoring;
* attention detection;
* emotional-state analysis;
* behavioral analytics.

The governance team should examine the actual functionality rather than relying on the vendor's marketing terminology.

---

## 1.18 Biometric Categorization

Article 5 also addresses certain biometric categorization systems that infer sensitive characteristics from biometric data.

The governance assessment should determine:

* what biometric data is processed;
* what category is being inferred;
* whether the inferred characteristic falls within the prohibited scope;
* the purpose;
* the affected population;
* whether a statutory exception applies.

This assessment should be coordinated with:

* privacy;
* data protection;
* security;
* legal;
* AI governance.

---

## 1.19 Real-Time Remote Biometric Identification

Article 5 contains specific restrictions concerning real-time remote biometric identification systems used in publicly accessible spaces for law-enforcement purposes.

This area requires careful legal analysis because the Regulation establishes a specific framework and narrowly defined exceptions.

The organization should not treat:

> "Law enforcement biometric identification"

as automatically equivalent to:

> "prohibited in every circumstance."

Instead, the assessment should determine:

* whether identification is real-time;
* whether it is remote;
* what biometric modality is involved;
* where it is deployed;
* who is deploying it;
* whether the purpose is law enforcement;
* whether an applicable exception exists;
* whether the procedural safeguards required by the Regulation are satisfied.

---

## 1.20 2026 Additional Prohibited Practices

The 2026 AI Omnibus amended Article 5 to add prohibitions concerning certain AI systems that generate or manipulate realistic images, video, audio or similar material involving an identifiable person's intimate parts or sexually explicit activities without the person's required consent.

It also added a prohibition concerning certain AI-generated or manipulated material falling within the EU framework addressing child sexual abuse material.

These provisions demonstrate an important governance principle:

> **The prohibited-practice register must be maintained as a living regulatory control.**

Organizations should not assume that the Article 5 list is permanently static.

---

## 1.21 Prohibited Practice Versus High-Risk Classification

A prohibited practice should not be converted into a high-risk system simply because the organization believes stronger controls could make it acceptable.

For example:

**Incorrect approach**

> "This is potentially prohibited, but we will classify it as high-risk and add additional controls."

**Correct approach**

> "This may fall within Article 5. We must determine whether the prohibition applies before considering other regulatory classifications."

This distinction is fundamental to EU AI Act governance.

---

## 1.22 Prohibited Practice Versus Internal Risk Acceptance

Enterprise risk acceptance does not automatically authorize prohibited AI use.

An organization should not reason:

> "The business accepts the risk."

Where applicable law prohibits the practice, internal management approval cannot override the legal prohibition.

Risk acceptance is relevant to residual organizational risk where legally permissible; it is not a mechanism for overriding mandatory law.

---

## 1.23 Prohibited-Practice Screening as a Preventive Control

The preferred GRC control objective is:

> **Prevent the organization from placing on the market, putting into service or using AI systems in prohibited circumstances.**

The control should operate at multiple points:

### Procurement

Screen proposed AI capabilities.

### Design

Review intended functionality.

### Development

Assess planned use cases.

### Deployment

Require regulatory approval.

### Change Management

Reassess material modifications.

### Monitoring

Identify prohibited-use indicators.

### Incident Management

Escalate suspected prohibited use.

---

## 1.24 Prohibited-Practice Screening Questions

A basic screening questionnaire should ask:

1. Does the system influence human behavior?
2. Does it use manipulative or deceptive techniques?
3. Does it operate through subliminal techniques?
4. Does it target vulnerable persons or groups?
5. Does it create or use social scoring?
6. Does it assess criminal risk from profiling or personality characteristics?
7. Does it create facial-recognition databases through untargeted scraping?
8. Does it infer emotions in a workplace or educational environment?
9. Does it perform prohibited biometric categorization?
10. Does it perform real-time remote biometric identification?
11. Does it generate or manipulate non-consensual intimate material?
12. Does it generate or manipulate prohibited child sexual abuse material?
13. Does an exception or special legal condition potentially apply?
14. Is Legal review required?

A "yes" or "uncertain" response should trigger enhanced review.

---

## 1.25 Legal Uncertainty

Not every ambiguous AI use case should immediately be declared prohibited.

However, uncertainty should not be treated as permission to deploy.

The appropriate governance response is:

**Potential Article 5 Issue**

↓

**Deployment Hold / Escalation**

↓

**Fact Gathering**

↓

**Legal and Compliance Analysis**

↓

**Exception Analysis Where Applicable**

↓

**Decision**

↓

**Documented Evidence**

This creates a controlled process for dealing with uncertainty.

---

## 1.26 Prohibited-Practice Evidence

Evidence should support both positive and negative conclusions.

For a conclusion that a practice is **not prohibited**, evidence might include:

* system documentation;
* intended-purpose statement;
* technical architecture;
* model documentation;
* user workflow;
* data-flow documentation;
* deployment environment;
* affected-person analysis;
* legal assessment;
* exception analysis where relevant.

The objective is not to prove a negative in the abstract.

The objective is to demonstrate that the organization performed a reasonable, evidence-based assessment against the applicable Article 5 criteria.

---

## 1.27 Regulatory Review of Article 5

The prohibited-practice framework is subject to regulatory review.

The European Commission's 2026 review report specifically assesses whether technological and societal developments justify changes to the prohibited-practice list and the high-risk use cases.

This reinforces the need for AI Governance to maintain:

* regulatory monitoring;
* Article 5 methodology updates;
* control-library updates;
* employee training updates;
* inventory reassessment;
* legal interpretation records.

---

## 1.28 Core GRC Principle

The central principle for prohibited AI governance is:

> **Identify the practice first, apply the legal test second, determine whether a prohibition applies third, and only then proceed to other AI risk classifications and controls.**

This prevents a common governance failure in which organizations treat every AI system as a conventional technology risk and attempt to manage potentially prohibited activities through ordinary security, privacy or operational controls.

# Part 2 — Prohibited-Practice Assessment and Control Framework

## 2.29 Prohibited-Practice Assessment Methodology

The assessment of potentially prohibited AI practices should be structured as a legal and operational decision process.

A recommended methodology is:

**Identify AI Capability**

↓

**Define Actual Use**

↓

**Identify Affected Persons**

↓

**Identify Relevant Article 5 Category**

↓

**Test Each Legal Element**

↓

**Assess Exceptions Where Applicable**

↓

**Determine Prohibited / Not Prohibited / Uncertain**

↓

**Escalate**

↓

**Document Decision**

The assessment should be based on the actual system and deployment rather than the vendor's description or the organization's preferred classification.

Article 5 prohibits specified practices, and the Commission has published practical guidance specifically intended to support consistent interpretation and implementation.

---

## 2.30 Legal-Element Testing

A prohibited-practice assessment should decompose the relevant Article 5 provision into individual legal elements.

For example, for manipulative or deceptive AI, the assessment should separately examine:

* technique;
* intentionality where relevant;
* behavioral effect;
* impairment of informed decision-making;
* resulting decision;
* significant harm;
* reasonable likelihood of harm.

A conclusion should not simply state:

> "This system is not manipulative."

It should demonstrate how the relevant legal elements were assessed.

---

## 2.31 Fact-Finding Before Legal Classification

Legal classification depends on accurate facts.

The assessment team should establish:

* what the system does;
* what users see;
* what data the system receives;
* what outputs it produces;
* how outputs are used;
* who receives the outputs;
* who is affected;
* what decisions follow;
* whether the system operates autonomously;
* whether the deployment differs from the original design.

A legally sophisticated assessment based on inaccurate system facts can still produce an incorrect classification.

---

## 2.32 Intended Purpose and Actual Use

The organization should document both:

**Intended Purpose**

What the system is designed and authorized to do.

**Actual Use**

How users actually use the system.

This distinction is important because prohibited-practice exposure may arise from actual deployment even when the original product design did not contemplate the use.

For example:

> A general-purpose behavioral analytics capability may be deployed internally for a purpose materially different from its original intended use.

The organization should therefore incorporate actual-use monitoring into its governance framework.

---

## 2.33 Prohibited-Practice Decision Tree

A standardized decision tree can be used:

### Question 1

Is the technology an AI system within the scope of the AI Act?

**No**

→ Article 5 assessment ends, subject to other applicable law.

**Yes**

→ Continue.

### Question 2

Does the system perform an activity corresponding to an Article 5 category?

**No**

→ Continue with other AI Act classification.

**Yes/Uncertain**

→ Continue legal assessment.

### Question 3

Are all required legal elements satisfied?

**No**

→ Document rationale and continue classification.

**Yes**

→ Treat as prohibited.

**Uncertain**

→ Escalate to Legal/Compliance.

---

## 2.34 Prohibited-Practice Assessment Statuses

The GRC platform should use controlled statuses.

Recommended values:

* **Not Assessed**
* **Under Assessment**
* **No Article 5 Match**
* **Potential Article 5 Match**
* **Legal Review Required**
* **Prohibited**
* **Not Prohibited — Documented Rationale**
* **Exception/Specific Condition Requires Review**

Avoid using only:

* Yes
* No

because these values do not adequately represent legal uncertainty.

---

## 2.35 Manipulation Assessment Framework

For Article 5(1)(a), the assessment should examine the full chain:

**Technique**

→ subliminal, manipulative or deceptive?

↓

**Behavioral Effect**

→ materially distorts behavior?

↓

**Decision-Making**

→ appreciably impairs informed decision-making?

↓

**Decision**

→ causes a decision that otherwise would not have been made?

↓

**Harm**

→ significant harm caused or reasonably likely?

Each stage should be supported by evidence.

Article 5 specifically connects the prohibited practice to material behavioral distortion, impaired informed decision-making and significant harm.

---

## 2.36 Deceptive AI Assessment

Deception should be assessed based on the system's actual behavior.

Potential evidence includes:

* interface design;
* system prompts;
* automated messaging;
* recommendation logic;
* disclosures;
* user testing;
* A/B testing;
* behavioral experiments;
* product requirements;
* marketing claims.

The assessment should distinguish:

**Ordinary persuasive communication**

from

**purposefully deceptive or manipulative conduct meeting the Article 5 legal threshold.**

---

## 2.37 Vulnerability Assessment

For Article 5(1)(b), the organization should document:

### Vulnerability

What vulnerability exists?

### Basis

Does it arise from:

* age;
* disability;
* specific social situation;
* specific economic situation?

### Targeting or Exploitation

How does the AI system exploit the vulnerability?

### Behavioral Effect

Does it materially distort behavior?

### Harm

Does the practice cause or reasonably risk significant harm?

The existence of a vulnerable user alone does not establish the prohibition.

---

## 2.38 Vulnerability Evidence

Evidence can include:

* target-user profiles;
* marketing segmentation;
* system configuration;
* behavioral models;
* personalization rules;
* user research;
* product documentation;
* sales strategies;
* targeting criteria;
* testing results.

Where an organization claims that a vulnerability is irrelevant, the rationale should be documented.

---

## 2.39 Social-Scoring Assessment

For Article 5(1)(c), the assessment should establish:

1. Are natural persons or groups evaluated?
2. Is the evaluation performed over a period?
3. Is social behavior considered?
4. Are personal or personality characteristics used?
5. Is a social score produced?
6. Does the score lead to detrimental or unfavorable treatment?
7. Is the treatment in an unrelated social context?
8. Is the treatment unjustified or disproportionate?

The social-scoring prohibition specifically addresses detrimental treatment across unrelated social contexts and unjustified or disproportionate treatment.

---

## 2.40 Social Scoring Data Lineage

Organizations should maintain data lineage for systems that evaluate individuals.

The assessment should identify:

**Source**

→ where behavioral information originates.

**Collection**

→ how information is gathered.

**Processing**

→ how characteristics are derived.

**Scoring**

→ how the score is generated.

**Decision**

→ how the score influences treatment.

**Outcome**

→ what happens to the individual.

This allows governance teams to identify cross-context scoring that might otherwise remain hidden.

---

## 2.41 Criminal-Risk Assessment

For AI used to assess criminal risk, the organization should determine:

* whether the system predicts criminal behavior;
* whether the person is a natural person;
* whether profiling is involved;
* whether personality characteristics are used;
* whether the assessment is based solely on such profiling;
* whether objective and verifiable facts directly linked to criminal activity are also considered;
* whether a human assessment is involved.

The legal analysis should be performed by appropriately qualified personnel.

---

## 2.42 Facial-Recognition Database Assessment

For systems potentially involving untargeted scraping, the assessment should identify:

* source websites;
* source platforms;
* CCTV sources;
* image collection mechanism;
* collection scale;
* targeting methodology;
* purpose of the database;
* whether facial recognition is subsequently performed.

The organization should specifically investigate systems described as:

* face search;
* facial intelligence;
* face indexing;
* identity search;
* biometric search;
* image-to-identity.

Marketing terminology should not determine the legal conclusion.

---

## 2.43 Emotion-Recognition Assessment

For workplace or education deployments, assess:

* whether emotions are inferred;
* which biometric or behavioral signals are used;
* whether the output represents an emotion or emotional state;
* purpose;
* affected persons;
* decision influenced;
* environment;
* applicable statutory exception.

Potential data sources include:

* facial expressions;
* voice;
* speech;
* physiological signals;
* body movements;
* behavioral patterns.

A vendor's claim that a system measures "engagement" rather than "emotion" should be technically validated.

---

## 2.44 Technical Validation of Emotion Claims

AI Governance should involve technical specialists where the legal characterization depends on what the system actually infers.

For example:

**Vendor description**

> "Employee engagement analytics."

**Technical investigation**

→ facial landmarks
→ voice characteristics
→ behavioral signals
→ inferred emotional states.

The governance team should classify the actual capability rather than the commercial label.

---

## 2.45 Biometric Categorization Assessment

For biometric categorization, determine:

* biometric modality;
* input data;
* inferred category;
* intended purpose;
* target population;
* output;
* downstream use;
* whether the characteristic falls within the prohibited scope;
* whether an applicable legal condition applies.

The assessment should distinguish categorization from:

* authentication;
* identification;
* verification;
* general biometric analysis.

These are not necessarily legally equivalent activities.

---

## 2.46 Real-Time Remote Biometric Identification Assessment

For law-enforcement use, establish:

* location;
* accessibility of the space;
* biometric modality;
* whether identification occurs remotely;
* whether identification occurs in real time;
* purpose;
* authority requesting deployment;
* legal basis;
* applicable exception;
* required procedural safeguards.

Because the legal framework contains specific conditions and exceptions, the organization should not use a simplistic "biometric = prohibited" rule.

The European Commission currently lists real-time remote biometric identification for law-enforcement purposes in publicly accessible spaces among the Article 5 prohibited practices.

---

## 2.47 Non-Consensual Intimate Material

The 2026 amendment introduced an additional prohibited category concerning AI systems that generate or manipulate specified non-consensual intimate material.

The prohibition responds to the increasing availability of AI systems capable of creating realistic intimate imagery and similar material without the required consent.

Governance controls should therefore identify:

* image-generation capabilities;
* image-editing capabilities;
* identity replacement;
* "nudification" functionality;
* synthetic sexual imagery;
* consent controls;
* user reporting;
* abuse-prevention mechanisms.

The relevant Article 5 provisions apply from **2 December 2026** under the amended Regulation.

---

## 2.48 Child Sexual Abuse Material

The amended Article 5 framework also addresses certain AI-generated or manipulated child sexual abuse material.

Organizations operating generative AI systems should therefore implement controls capable of identifying and preventing prohibited use.

Relevant controls include:

* content safety filtering;
* model-level safeguards;
* prompt controls;
* output moderation;
* abuse detection;
* account controls;
* incident escalation;
* evidence preservation;
* law-enforcement reporting where legally required.

This category should be treated as a **zero-tolerance prohibited-use control**, rather than as a conventional model-risk category.

---

## 2.50 Preventive Deployment Control

No system identified as potentially prohibited should proceed through normal deployment approval without resolution.

A recommended control is:

> **AI-PRH-01 — Prohibited Practice Deployment Gate**

**Control Objective**

Prevent prohibited AI practices from being deployed or used within the organization's operations.

**Trigger**

New AI system, material change, new use case or regulatory change.

**Control Owner**

AI Governance / Compliance.

**Required Evidence**

* Article 5 assessment;
* supporting technical evidence;
* legal analysis where required;
* approval decision.

**Failure Condition**

Production deployment without completed assessment.

**Escalation**

AI Governance + Legal + Compliance.

---

## 2.51 Procurement Control

Procurement contracts should include appropriate provisions requiring vendors to disclose relevant AI capabilities.

Questions should include:

* Does the product perform emotion recognition?
* Does it perform biometric categorization?
* Does it perform facial recognition?
* Does it profile individuals?
* Does it generate or manipulate images, audio or video?
* Does it use behavioral targeting?
* Does it support automated decisions?
* What safeguards prevent prohibited use?
* What changes to functionality will be communicated?

Vendor questionnaires should support—not replace—the organization's own legal assessment.

---

## 2.52 Contractual Prohibited-Use Controls

AI contracts should consider provisions addressing:

* prohibited use restrictions;
* notification of material functionality changes;
* regulatory cooperation;
* documentation;
* audit rights;
* security controls;
* incident notification;
* data handling;
* subcontractors;
* model changes;
* content-safety mechanisms.

The organization should ensure contractual rights are consistent with its regulatory obligations.

---

## 2.53 Product Governance

Organizations developing AI products should embed Article 5 screening into product lifecycle management.

Required checkpoints can include:

**Concept**

→ prohibited-practice screening

**Design**

→ technical/legal assessment

**Development**

→ control implementation

**Testing**

→ abuse-case testing

**Release**

→ regulatory approval

**Production**

→ monitoring

**Change**

→ reassessment

This is especially important for AI products capable of being repurposed by customers.

---

## 2.54 Abuse-Case Testing

Security and AI governance teams should test whether a system can be configured or manipulated to facilitate prohibited practices.

Testing should include:

* adversarial prompts;
* unsafe configurations;
* unauthorized feature activation;
* API misuse;
* excessive personalization;
* biometric misuse;
* inappropriate profiling;
* prohibited content generation.

The objective is to identify whether technical safeguards adequately prevent foreseeable prohibited use.

---

## 2.55 Red-Team Testing for Prohibited Practices

AI red teams can test scenarios such as:

> Can the system be induced to manipulate a vulnerable user?

> Can the system generate prohibited intimate material?

> Can the system infer protected characteristics from biometric data?

> Can the system be configured to perform prohibited emotion recognition?

> Can the system create or expand a facial-recognition database through unauthorized collection?

Red-team results should feed directly into governance and product-risk decisions.

---

## 2.56 Monitoring for Prohibited Use

Preventive screening alone is insufficient.

Organizations should monitor for indicators such as:

* prohibited prompts;
* unusual user behavior;
* attempts to bypass safeguards;
* unauthorized biometric functions;
* prohibited targeting;
* unexpected profiling;
* abuse reports;
* policy violations;
* configuration changes.

Monitoring should be proportional to the system and its deployment environment.

---

## 2.57 Incident Classification

A suspected prohibited AI practice should be treated as a potentially significant compliance incident.

The incident process should include:

1. Detect.
2. Contain.
3. Preserve evidence.
4. Determine actual use.
5. Assess Article 5 applicability.
6. Escalate to Legal/Compliance.
7. Determine required corrective action.
8. Assess reporting obligations where applicable.
9. Remediate.
10. Document closure.

The organization should not wait for a confirmed legal conclusion before applying appropriate containment measures to an apparently serious prohibited-use scenario.

---

## 2.58 Deployment Hold

A deployment hold should be available as a formal governance mechanism.

A hold may be triggered by:

* potential Article 5 violation;
* unresolved legal interpretation;
* missing evidence;
* material uncertainty;
* inadequate technical safeguards;
* unexplained vendor capability;
* unauthorized change.

The hold should remain until the designated authority records a decision.

---

## 2.59 Exception Governance

Exceptions should be handled carefully because some Article 5 provisions contain specific conditions or exceptions.

The organization should document:

* exact legal provision;
* exception relied upon;
* facts supporting the exception;
* responsible legal reviewer;
* technical evidence;
* conditions;
* limitations;
* approval;
* reassessment trigger.

A generic "business exception" should never be sufficient.

---

## 2.60 No Business-Override Principle

Business urgency should not override prohibited-practice controls.

A request such as:

> "We need this deployed today for a major customer."

should not bypass Article 5 assessment.

The organization should instead have an expedited legal and governance escalation process.

This maintains commercial agility without turning regulatory controls into optional procedures.

---

## 2.61 Prohibited-Practice Control Testing

Control testing should evaluate whether:

* all new AI systems receive Article 5 screening;
* screening occurs before deployment;
* high-risk or prohibited indicators trigger escalation;
* legal reviews are documented;
* deployment holds are enforced;
* material changes trigger reassessment;
* vendor capabilities are independently validated;
* monitoring detects prohibited-use indicators.

Testing should include both normal and exceptional scenarios.

---

## 2.62 Key Evidence

A mature prohibited-practice governance program should retain:

* AI inventory;
* Article 5 screening;
* technical documentation;
* intended-purpose record;
* affected-person assessment;
* data-flow diagrams;
* legal analysis;
* exception analysis;
* approval;
* deployment-gate evidence;
* testing results;
* monitoring evidence;
* incidents;
* remediation;
* reassessments.

The evidence should be linked to the AI system's unique identifier.

---

## 2.63 Prohibited-Practice Control Lifecycle

The operational lifecycle is:

**Discover**

→ AI capability identified.

**Screen**

→ Article 5 categories evaluated.

**Assess**

→ legal elements tested.

**Escalate**

→ uncertainty or potential prohibition reviewed.

**Decide**

→ documented legal classification.

**Prevent**

→ deployment controls applied.

**Monitor**

→ actual use observed.

**Reassess**

→ material changes reviewed.

**Assure**

→ control effectiveness independently tested.

This lifecycle should be integrated into the organization's broader AI governance operating model.

---

## 2.64 Control Objective

The overall control objective is:

> **Ensure that AI systems are not placed on the market, put into service or used in circumstances prohibited by Article 5 of the EU AI Act, and ensure that potential prohibited practices are identified, escalated, documented and prevented through effective governance controls.**

The control must operate across the entire AI lifecycle rather than only at procurement or deployment.
