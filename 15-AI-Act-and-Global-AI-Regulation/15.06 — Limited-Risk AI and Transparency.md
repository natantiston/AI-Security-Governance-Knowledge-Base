# 15.06 — Limited-Risk AI and Transparency

## Part 1 — Regulatory Foundations, Scope and Governance Context

### 1. Introduction

Not all AI systems require the extensive compliance framework applicable to high-risk AI.

The EU AI Act establishes a risk-based regulatory model in which certain AI systems are subject primarily to **specific transparency obligations**, while many other AI applications remain outside the Act's substantive obligations because they present minimal or no identified regulatory risk.

The expression **"limited-risk AI"** is commonly used operationally, but the AI Act does not establish a formal legal category called "limited-risk AI." A more precise regulatory description is **AI systems subject to specific transparency obligations**, particularly those addressed by Article 50.

The European Commission identifies transparency risks as situations where people need to know that they are interacting with AI or that content has been generated or manipulated by AI. The Article 50 transparency obligations became applicable on **2 August 2026**.

From a GRC perspective, this distinction is important because an organization should not assume:

> **"The system is not high-risk, therefore there are no AI Act obligations."**

A system can fall outside the high-risk category while still being subject to legally binding transparency requirements.

---

### 2. The Regulatory Position of Transparency-Risk AI

The AI Act's risk-based framework can be understood operationally as including:

1. prohibited AI practices;
2. high-risk AI systems;
3. AI systems subject to specific transparency obligations;
4. minimal- or no-risk AI systems.

Transparency-risk obligations therefore occupy a position between prohibited/high-risk regulation and minimal-risk AI.

The distinction is important because the control philosophy differs.

**Prohibited AI**

The organization must prevent prohibited practices.

**High-risk AI**

The organization must satisfy a broad lifecycle compliance framework.

**Transparency-risk AI**

The organization must provide specified information, disclosures, markings or labels when the relevant legal conditions apply.

**Minimal/no-risk AI**

The AI Act generally does not impose additional substantive obligations solely because the system is AI, although other laws and voluntary governance controls may still apply.

The European Commission identifies examples of minimal- or no-risk applications such as AI-enabled video games and spam filters, while identifying transparency obligations for systems such as chatbots and generative AI.

---

### 3. Article 50 as the Core Transparency Provision

Article 50 of the AI Act establishes transparency obligations for providers and deployers of certain AI systems.

The current consolidated provision addresses several distinct scenarios:

* AI systems directly interacting with natural persons;
* AI systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text;
* emotion-recognition systems;
* biometric-categorisation systems;
* deepfake content;
* certain AI-generated or manipulated text concerning matters of public interest.

These obligations should not be treated as one generic "AI disclosure" requirement.

Each has different:

* responsible party;
* triggering condition;
* required action;
* timing;
* exception;
* technical implementation;
* evidence requirement.

A mature AI GRC program should therefore map each Article 50 obligation independently.

---

### 4. Provider and Deployer Responsibilities

The first governance question should be:

> **Who is responsible for the relevant transparency obligation?**

The AI Act distinguishes between **providers** and **deployers**.

A provider generally develops an AI system, or has it developed, and places it on the EU market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark.

A deployer uses an AI system under its authority, excluding personal, non-professional activity.

Article 50 contains obligations that apply differently to providers and deployers.

This distinction has major GRC implications.

For example:

**AI chatbot provider**

→ responsible for designing the system so users are informed that they are interacting with AI where Article 50(1) applies.

**Company deploying an emotion-recognition system**

→ responsible for informing persons exposed to the system under Article 50(3).

**Organization publishing an AI-generated deepfake**

→ may have a deployer disclosure obligation under Article 50(4).

Therefore, transparency compliance cannot be assigned exclusively to the AI development team.

---

### 5. Direct Interaction with Natural Persons

Article 50(1) requires providers of AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons to design and develop those systems so that the individuals concerned are informed that they are interacting with an AI system, unless this is obvious to a reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect person considering the circumstances and context.

Examples can include:

* customer-service chatbots;
* AI assistants;
* conversational agents;
* AI avatars;
* interactive virtual agents;
* certain AI-powered support interfaces.

The relevant assessment should focus on whether the system is genuinely designed for direct interaction with natural persons and whether the circumstances make the AI nature of that interaction obvious.

---

### 6. The "Obvious AI" Exception

The obligation to inform users does not apply where the AI nature of the interaction is already obvious from the perspective of the relevant reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect person.

This exception should not be interpreted casually.

A GRC program should not record:

> **"Users know this is AI."**

as sufficient evidence.

Instead, the organization should document why the AI nature of the interaction is objectively obvious in the particular context.

Relevant considerations may include:

* interface design;
* system name;
* disclosure already displayed;
* avatar design;
* conversational context;
* surrounding documentation;
* user expectations;
* deployment environment.

The exception should be applied carefully because it removes a transparency safeguard.

---

### 7. Timing of Transparency

Article 50 requires the relevant information to be provided clearly and distinguishably **at the latest at the time of the first interaction or exposure**, subject to the specific obligation and applicable conditions.

The information must also take account of applicable accessibility requirements.

Transparency should therefore not normally be implemented as:

* a hidden privacy-policy statement;
* a disclosure available only through a secondary menu;
* information presented after the interaction;
* an obscure legal disclaimer;
* information that an ordinary user cannot reasonably perceive.

The GRC control objective is to ensure that the affected person receives the required information at the legally relevant point in the interaction.

---

### 8. Generative AI and Machine-Readable Marking

Article 50(2) introduces a separate obligation for providers of AI systems, including GPAI systems, that generate synthetic audio, image, video or text content.

Providers must ensure that outputs are marked in a **machine-readable format** and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated, subject to the conditions and exceptions in the regulation.

The technical solution should be effective, interoperable, robust and reliable as far as technically feasible, taking account of content-specific limitations, implementation costs and the generally acknowledged state of the art.

This is fundamentally different from simply displaying:

> **"Generated by AI."**

on a user interface.

The provider-level requirement concerns machine-readable marking and technical detectability.

Consequently, AI governance must address both:

* **human-readable transparency**, and
* **machine-readable provenance or detection mechanisms**.

---

### 9. Human-Readable Disclosure Versus Machine-Readable Marking

These concepts should be separated in the control framework.

**Human-readable disclosure**

Designed for people.

Examples:

* "This image was generated by AI."
* "You are interacting with an AI assistant."
* "This video has been artificially generated."

**Machine-readable marking**

Designed for technical detection.

Examples can include:

* metadata;
* embedded provenance information;
* standardized technical markers;
* other interoperable mechanisms capable of supporting detection.

The two mechanisms serve different objectives.

A machine-readable marker does not automatically satisfy a requirement for visible disclosure to a person.

The Commission specifically explains that deployers cannot simply rely on provider-side machine-readable marking to fulfil their own applicable deepfake disclosure obligation under Article 50(4).

---

### 10. Assistive Editing Exception

Article 50(2) does not apply to the extent that an AI system performs an assistive function for standard editing or does not substantially alter the input data supplied by the deployer or the semantics of that input, subject to the statutory conditions.

This creates an important boundary for organizations using AI-powered:

* image enhancement;
* grammar correction;
* noise reduction;
* video editing;
* transcription;
* formatting;
* background processing;
* other content-editing functions.

The organization should not automatically classify every AI-assisted modification as requiring the same marking regime.

Instead, it should determine whether the system falls within the statutory exception and document the reasoning.

---

### 11. Emotion Recognition and Biometric Categorisation

Article 50(3) places transparency obligations on **deployers** of emotion-recognition systems and biometric-categorisation systems.

Persons exposed to such systems must be informed of the operation of the system, subject to the applicable legal conditions and exceptions.

The processing of personal data must also comply with the applicable data-protection framework.

This creates an important GRC intersection between:

* AI governance;
* privacy;
* data protection;
* biometric governance;
* employment law;
* cybersecurity;
* fundamental rights.

Transparency under Article 50 does not replace GDPR or other applicable privacy obligations.

It operates alongside them.

---

### 12. Deepfakes

Article 50(4) requires deployers of AI systems that generate or manipulate image, audio or video content constituting a deepfake to disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated, subject to the statutory conditions and exceptions.

This is a **deployer disclosure obligation**, not merely a provider marking obligation.

The distinction is operationally significant.

A provider may technically mark an output.

The deployer may nevertheless have an independent obligation to ensure that people exposed to the deepfake receive an appropriate disclosure.

The disclosure should be:

* clear;
* distinguishable;
* understandable;
* perceivable;
* provided at the legally relevant point of exposure.

---

### 13. Artistic, Creative, Satirical and Fictional Content

Article 50(4) contains specific treatment for content forming part of an evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous work or programme.

In these circumstances, the transparency obligation is limited to disclosure of the existence of the generated or manipulated content in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work.

This is relevant to organizations involved in:

* film;
* television;
* advertising;
* gaming;
* entertainment;
* digital media;
* creative production.

Organizations should therefore establish contextual rules rather than applying one generic label to every AI-generated element.

The relevant GRC question becomes:

> **What disclosure is appropriate for this type of audience and context while still satisfying the applicable legal requirement?**

---

### 14. AI-Generated Text on Matters of Public Interest

Article 50(4) also addresses AI-generated or manipulated text published for the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest.

The deployer must disclose that the text was artificially generated or manipulated unless a statutory exception applies, including circumstances involving human review or editorial control under the conditions specified in the regulation.

This is particularly relevant to:

* news organizations;
* public-information websites;
* public authorities;
* corporate communications;
* crisis communications;
* financial or economic information;
* public-interest publications.

Organizations should distinguish between:

**AI-assisted drafting with meaningful human editorial responsibility**

and

**AI-generated or manipulated text published without the qualifying human review or editorial control.**

That distinction should be documented in the organization's publishing workflow.

---

### 15. Transparency Is Not the Same as Accuracy

An important governance principle is:

> **Disclosure does not make an inaccurate AI output acceptable.**

A system may comply with Article 50 transparency requirements and still violate:

* privacy law;
* consumer-protection law;
* discrimination law;
* sector-specific regulation;
* intellectual-property requirements;
* cybersecurity requirements;
* contractual obligations;
* other provisions of the AI Act.

For example, telling a customer that a chatbot is AI does not make deceptive advice acceptable.

Similarly, labelling a deepfake does not eliminate other legal or ethical restrictions applicable to its creation or distribution.

Transparency is therefore a **control layer**, not a substitute for substantive AI governance.

---

### 16. Transparency Is Not Limited to Consumer-Facing AI

Organizations should avoid assuming that Article 50 is relevant only to public consumer applications.

Potentially relevant environments include:

* workplaces;
* education;
* healthcare;
* financial services;
* public administration;
* customer service;
* recruitment;
* marketing;
* media;
* security operations;
* enterprise collaboration;
* professional services.

Applicability depends on the particular Article 50 trigger, system characteristics and deployment context.

An internal enterprise chatbot may therefore require a different analysis from a public website chatbot.

---

### 17. Enterprise AI Transparency Inventory

Organizations should maintain a transparency inventory separate from, or integrated into, the broader AI inventory.

Useful fields include:

| Field                    | Example                              |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| AI System ID             | AI-TR-0012                           |
| System                   | Customer AI Assistant                |
| Provider/Deployer        | Provider                             |
| AI interaction           | Yes                                  |

| Biometric categorisation | No                                   |
| Deepfake capability      | No                                   |
| Public-interest text     | No                                   |

| User disclosure          | Required                             |
| Machine-readable marking | Required                             |
| Disclosure timing        | First interaction                    |
| Accessibility review     | Completed                            |
| Owner                    | Product Governance                   |
| Evidence                 | Disclosure test + technical evidence |
| Status                   | Compliant                            |

This inventory enables the organization to distinguish:

* systems requiring transparency controls;
* systems not subject to Article 50;
* systems requiring further legal assessment;
* systems with applicable exceptions;
* systems requiring technical marking;
* systems requiring human-facing disclosure.

---

### 18. Transparency-by-Design

Transparency should be incorporated during system design rather than added immediately before deployment.

Design reviews should ask:

* Does the user know whether they are interacting with AI?
* Is the disclosure visible at the correct point?
* Is the disclosure understandable?
* Is it accessible?
* Is AI-generated content technically marked where required?
* Can the marking survive relevant transformations?
* Can the content be detected reliably?
* Does the deployment require visible disclosure?
* Is the relevant exception properly documented?
* Does the system generate deepfakes?
* Does it generate public-interest text?
* Does it perform emotion recognition or biometric categorisation?

This creates a **transparency-by-design** control analogous to privacy-by-design and security-by-design.

---

### 19. Transparency Risk Assessment

Every potentially applicable Article 50 system should undergo a structured assessment.

The assessment should determine:

1. Is the technology an AI system?
2. Is it intended to interact directly with natural persons?
3. Is the interaction direct?
4. Is the AI nature obvious?
5. Does it generate synthetic audio, image, video or text?
6. Does Article 50(2) apply?
7. Is the system performing standard assistive editing?
8. Does it perform emotion recognition?
9. Does it perform biometric categorisation?
10. Does it generate or manipulate deepfakes?
11. Does it generate or manipulate text intended to inform the public on matters of public interest?
12. Is there qualifying human review or editorial control?
13. Does a statutory exception apply?
14. Is the organization acting as provider, deployer or another regulated actor?
15. What evidence demonstrates compliance?

The output should be a controlled **Transparency Applicability Decision**.

---

### 20. Transparency as a GRC Control Domain

A mature AI governance framework should establish transparency as a dedicated control domain.

The control objectives should include:

* correct applicability determination;
* correct provider/deployer responsibility;
* timely disclosure;
* technically effective marking;
* appropriate labeling;
* accessibility;
* exception governance;
* monitoring;
* testing;
* evidence retention;
* regulatory change management.

This prevents transparency from becoming an informal user-interface feature controlled solely by product designers.

---

### 21. Current Regulatory Status

Article 50 transparency obligations are applicable from **2 August 2026**. The European Commission published guidance on Article 50 transparency obligations in 2026 to support consistent implementation by providers, deployers and competent authorities.

The Commission also approved a **Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content** in July 2026. The code is intended to facilitate compliance with the relevant transparency obligations, but following a voluntary code does not by itself constitute conclusive proof of compliance with the AI Act.

For certain AI systems already placed on the market before 2 August 2026, the Act provides a limited transition concerning the Article 50(2) marking obligation, with the relevant obligation applying by **2 December 2026** under the applicable transitional provision.

For GRC purposes, organizations should therefore treat Article 50 as an **active compliance requirement**, not merely a future regulatory planning item.

---

### 22. Governance Principle

The central governance principle for transparency-risk AI is:

> **An AI system does not need to be high-risk for transparency to become a legal control requirement.**

Organizations should identify the specific transparency trigger, determine the responsible regulatory actor, document applicable exceptions, implement the required technical or human-facing control, and retain evidence demonstrating that transparency occurred at the legally required point in the AI interaction or content lifecycle.
