# 15.06 — Limited-Risk AI and Transparency

## Part 1 — Regulatory Foundations, Scope and Governance Context

### 1. Introduction

Not all AI systems require the extensive compliance framework applicable to high-risk AI.

The EU AI Act establishes a risk-based regulatory model in which certain AI systems are subject primarily to **specific transparency obligations**, while many other AI applications remain outside the Act's substantive obligations because they present minimal or no identified regulatory risk.

The expression **"limited-risk AI"** is commonly used operationally, but the AI Act does not establish a formal legal category called "limited-risk AI." A more precise regulatory description is **AI systems subject to specific transparency obligations**, particularly those addressed by Article 50.

The European Commission identifies transparency risks as situations where people need to know that they are interacting with AI or that content has been generated or manipulated by AI. The Article 50 transparency obligations became applicable on **2 August 2026**.

From a GRC perspective, this distinction is important because an organization should not assume:

> **"The system is not high-risk, therefore there are no AI Act obligations."**

A system can fall outside the high-risk category while still being subject to legally binding transparency requirements.

---

### 2. The Regulatory Position of Transparency-Risk AI

The AI Act's risk-based framework can be understood operationally as including:

1. prohibited AI practices;
2. high-risk AI systems;
3. AI systems subject to specific transparency obligations;
4. minimal- or no-risk AI systems.

Transparency-risk obligations therefore occupy a position between prohibited/high-risk regulation and minimal-risk AI.

The distinction is important because the control philosophy differs.

**Prohibited AI**

The organization must prevent prohibited practices.

**High-risk AI**

The organization must satisfy a broad lifecycle compliance framework.

**Transparency-risk AI**

The organization must provide specified information, disclosures, markings or labels when the relevant legal conditions apply.

**Minimal/no-risk AI**

The AI Act generally does not impose additional substantive obligations solely because the system is AI, although other laws and voluntary governance controls may still apply.

The European Commission identifies examples of minimal- or no-risk applications such as AI-enabled video games and spam filters, while identifying transparency obligations for systems such as chatbots and generative AI.

---

### 3. Article 50 as the Core Transparency Provision

Article 50 of the AI Act establishes transparency obligations for providers and deployers of certain AI systems.

The current consolidated provision addresses several distinct scenarios:

* AI systems directly interacting with natural persons;
* AI systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text;
* emotion-recognition systems;
* biometric-categorisation systems;
* deepfake content;
* certain AI-generated or manipulated text concerning matters of public interest.

These obligations should not be treated as one generic "AI disclosure" requirement.

Each has different:

* responsible party;
* triggering condition;
* required action;
* timing;
* exception;
* technical implementation;
* evidence requirement.

A mature AI GRC program should therefore map each Article 50 obligation independently.

---

### 4. Provider and Deployer Responsibilities

The first governance question should be:

> **Who is responsible for the relevant transparency obligation?**

The AI Act distinguishes between **providers** and **deployers**.

A provider generally develops an AI system, or has it developed, and places it on the EU market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark.

A deployer uses an AI system under its authority, excluding personal, non-professional activity.

Article 50 contains obligations that apply differently to providers and deployers.

This distinction has major GRC implications.

For example:

**AI chatbot provider**

→ responsible for designing the system so users are informed that they are interacting with AI where Article 50(1) applies.

**Company deploying an emotion-recognition system**

→ responsible for informing persons exposed to the system under Article 50(3).

**Organization publishing an AI-generated deepfake**

→ may have a deployer disclosure obligation under Article 50(4).

Therefore, transparency compliance cannot be assigned exclusively to the AI development team.

---

### 5. Direct Interaction with Natural Persons

Article 50(1) requires providers of AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons to design and develop those systems so that the individuals concerned are informed that they are interacting with an AI system, unless this is obvious to a reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect person considering the circumstances and context.

Examples can include:

* customer-service chatbots;
* AI assistants;
* conversational agents;
* AI avatars;
* interactive virtual agents;
* certain AI-powered support interfaces.

The relevant assessment should focus on whether the system is genuinely designed for direct interaction with natural persons and whether the circumstances make the AI nature of that interaction obvious.

---

### 6. The "Obvious AI" Exception

The obligation to inform users does not apply where the AI nature of the interaction is already obvious from the perspective of the relevant reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect person.

This exception should not be interpreted casually.

A GRC program should not record:

> **"Users know this is AI."**

as sufficient evidence.

Instead, the organization should document why the AI nature of the interaction is objectively obvious in the particular context.

Relevant considerations may include:

* interface design;
* system name;
* disclosure already displayed;
* avatar design;
* conversational context;
* surrounding documentation;
* user expectations;
* deployment environment.

The exception should be applied carefully because it removes a transparency safeguard.

---

### 7. Timing of Transparency

Article 50 requires the relevant information to be provided clearly and distinguishably **at the latest at the time of the first interaction or exposure**, subject to the specific obligation and applicable conditions.

The information must also take account of applicable accessibility requirements.

Transparency should therefore not normally be implemented as:

* a hidden privacy-policy statement;
* a disclosure available only through a secondary menu;
* information presented after the interaction;
* an obscure legal disclaimer;
* information that an ordinary user cannot reasonably perceive.

The GRC control objective is to ensure that the affected person receives the required information at the legally relevant point in the interaction.

---

### 8. Generative AI and Machine-Readable Marking

Article 50(2) introduces a separate obligation for providers of AI systems, including GPAI systems, that generate synthetic audio, image, video or text content.

Providers must ensure that outputs are marked in a **machine-readable format** and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated, subject to the conditions and exceptions in the regulation.

The technical solution should be effective, interoperable, robust and reliable as far as technically feasible, taking account of content-specific limitations, implementation costs and the generally acknowledged state of the art.

This is fundamentally different from simply displaying:

> **"Generated by AI."**

on a user interface.

The provider-level requirement concerns machine-readable marking and technical detectability.

Consequently, AI governance must address both:

* **human-readable transparency**, and
* **machine-readable provenance or detection mechanisms**.

---

### 9. Human-Readable Disclosure Versus Machine-Readable Marking

These concepts should be separated in the control framework.

**Human-readable disclosure**

Designed for people.

Examples:

* "This image was generated by AI."
* "You are interacting with an AI assistant."
* "This video has been artificially generated."

**Machine-readable marking**

Designed for technical detection.

Examples can include:

* metadata;
* embedded provenance information;
* standardized technical markers;
* other interoperable mechanisms capable of supporting detection.

The two mechanisms serve different objectives.

A machine-readable marker does not automatically satisfy a requirement for visible disclosure to a person.

The Commission specifically explains that deployers cannot simply rely on provider-side machine-readable marking to fulfil their own applicable deepfake disclosure obligation under Article 50(4).

---

### 10. Assistive Editing Exception

Article 50(2) does not apply to the extent that an AI system performs an assistive function for standard editing or does not substantially alter the input data supplied by the deployer or the semantics of that input, subject to the statutory conditions.

This creates an important boundary for organizations using AI-powered:

* image enhancement;
* grammar correction;
* noise reduction;
* video editing;
* transcription;
* formatting;
* background processing;
* other content-editing functions.

The organization should not automatically classify every AI-assisted modification as requiring the same marking regime.

Instead, it should determine whether the system falls within the statutory exception and document the reasoning.

---

### 11. Emotion Recognition and Biometric Categorisation

Article 50(3) places transparency obligations on **deployers** of emotion-recognition systems and biometric-categorisation systems.

Persons exposed to such systems must be informed of the operation of the system, subject to the applicable legal conditions and exceptions.

The processing of personal data must also comply with the applicable data-protection framework.

This creates an important GRC intersection between:

* AI governance;
* privacy;
* data protection;
* biometric governance;
* employment law;
* cybersecurity;
* fundamental rights.

Transparency under Article 50 does not replace GDPR or other applicable privacy obligations.

It operates alongside them.

---

### 12. Deepfakes

Article 50(4) requires deployers of AI systems that generate or manipulate image, audio or video content constituting a deepfake to disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated, subject to the statutory conditions and exceptions.

This is a **deployer disclosure obligation**, not merely a provider marking obligation.

The distinction is operationally significant.

A provider may technically mark an output.

The deployer may nevertheless have an independent obligation to ensure that people exposed to the deepfake receive an appropriate disclosure.

The disclosure should be:

* clear;
* distinguishable;
* understandable;
* perceivable;
* provided at the legally relevant point of exposure.

---

### 13. Artistic, Creative, Satirical and Fictional Content

Article 50(4) contains specific treatment for content forming part of an evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous work or programme.

In these circumstances, the transparency obligation is limited to disclosure of the existence of the generated or manipulated content in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work.

This is relevant to organizations involved in:

* film;
* television;
* advertising;
* gaming;
* entertainment;
* digital media;
* creative production.

Organizations should therefore establish contextual rules rather than applying one generic label to every AI-generated element.

The relevant GRC question becomes:

> **What disclosure is appropriate for this type of audience and context while still satisfying the applicable legal requirement?**

---

### 14. AI-Generated Text on Matters of Public Interest

Article 50(4) also addresses AI-generated or manipulated text published for the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest.

The deployer must disclose that the text was artificially generated or manipulated unless a statutory exception applies, including circumstances involving human review or editorial control under the conditions specified in the regulation.

This is particularly relevant to:

* news organizations;
* public-information websites;
* public authorities;
* corporate communications;
* crisis communications;
* financial or economic information;
* public-interest publications.

Organizations should distinguish between:

**AI-assisted drafting with meaningful human editorial responsibility**

and

**AI-generated or manipulated text published without the qualifying human review or editorial control.**

That distinction should be documented in the organization's publishing workflow.

---

### 15. Transparency Is Not the Same as Accuracy

An important governance principle is:

> **Disclosure does not make an inaccurate AI output acceptable.**

A system may comply with Article 50 transparency requirements and still violate:

* privacy law;
* consumer-protection law;
* discrimination law;
* sector-specific regulation;
* intellectual-property requirements;
* cybersecurity requirements;
* contractual obligations;
* other provisions of the AI Act.

For example, telling a customer that a chatbot is AI does not make deceptive advice acceptable.

Similarly, labelling a deepfake does not eliminate other legal or ethical restrictions applicable to its creation or distribution.

Transparency is therefore a **control layer**, not a substitute for substantive AI governance.

---

### 16. Transparency Is Not Limited to Consumer-Facing AI

Organizations should avoid assuming that Article 50 is relevant only to public consumer applications.

Potentially relevant environments include:

* workplaces;
* education;
* healthcare;
* financial services;
* public administration;
* customer service;
* recruitment;
* marketing;
* media;
* security operations;
* enterprise collaboration;
* professional services.

Applicability depends on the particular Article 50 trigger, system characteristics and deployment context.

An internal enterprise chatbot may therefore require a different analysis from a public website chatbot.

---

---

### 18. Transparency-by-Design

Transparency should be incorporated during system design rather than added immediately before deployment.

Design reviews should ask:

* Does the user know whether they are interacting with AI?
* Is the disclosure visible at the correct point?
* Is the disclosure understandable?
* Is it accessible?
* Is AI-generated content technically marked where required?
* Can the marking survive relevant transformations?
* Can the content be detected reliably?
* Does the deployment require visible disclosure?
* Is the relevant exception properly documented?
* Does the system generate deepfakes?
* Does it generate public-interest text?
* Does it perform emotion recognition or biometric categorisation?

This creates a **transparency-by-design** control analogous to privacy-by-design and security-by-design.

---

### 19. Transparency Risk Assessment

Every potentially applicable Article 50 system should undergo a structured assessment.

The assessment should determine:

1. Is the technology an AI system?
2. Is it intended to interact directly with natural persons?
3. Is the interaction direct?
4. Is the AI nature obvious?
5. Does it generate synthetic audio, image, video or text?
6. Does Article 50(2) apply?
7. Is the system performing standard assistive editing?
8. Does it perform emotion recognition?
9. Does it perform biometric categorisation?
10. Does it generate or manipulate deepfakes?
11. Does it generate or manipulate text intended to inform the public on matters of public interest?
12. Is there qualifying human review or editorial control?
13. Does a statutory exception apply?
14. Is the organization acting as provider, deployer or another regulated actor?
15. What evidence demonstrates compliance?

The output should be a controlled **Transparency Applicability Decision**.

---

### 20. Transparency as a GRC Control Domain

A mature AI governance framework should establish transparency as a dedicated control domain.

The control objectives should include:

* correct applicability determination;
* correct provider/deployer responsibility;
* timely disclosure;
* technically effective marking;
* appropriate labeling;
* accessibility;
* exception governance;
* monitoring;
* testing;
* evidence retention;
* regulatory change management.

This prevents transparency from becoming an informal user-interface feature controlled solely by product designers.

---

### 21. Current Regulatory Status

Article 50 transparency obligations are applicable from **2 August 2026**. The European Commission published guidance on Article 50 transparency obligations in 2026 to support consistent implementation by providers, deployers and competent authorities.

The Commission also approved a **Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content** in July 2026. The code is intended to facilitate compliance with the relevant transparency obligations, but following a voluntary code does not by itself constitute conclusive proof of compliance with the AI Act.

For certain AI systems already placed on the market before 2 August 2026, the Act provides a limited transition concerning the Article 50(2) marking obligation, with the relevant obligation applying by **2 December 2026** under the applicable transitional provision.

For GRC purposes, organizations should therefore treat Article 50 as an **active compliance requirement**, not merely a future regulatory planning item.

---

### 22. Governance Principle

The central governance principle for transparency-risk AI is:

> **An AI system does not need to be high-risk for transparency to become a legal control requirement.**

Organizations should identify the specific transparency trigger, determine the responsible regulatory actor, document applicable exceptions, implement the required technical or human-facing control, and retain evidence demonstrating that transparency occurred at the legally required point in the AI interaction or content lifecycle.

# Part 2 — Transparency Applicability Assessment and Control Implementation

### 23. Transparency Applicability Assessment

An organization should not determine Article 50 applicability based solely on the general description of an AI product.

The assessment should examine the **specific system, function, output, actor and deployment context**.

A practical assessment sequence is:

**AI System Identified → Regulatory Actor Identified → Article 50 Scenario Tested → Exception Tested → Obligation Determined → Control Designed → Evidence Defined → Compliance Approved**

The assessment should be documented before production deployment where practicable.

The assessment record should contain:

* AI system identifier;
* provider;
* deployer;
* intended purpose;
* actual use;
* affected persons;
* relevant Article 50 provision;
* triggering facts;
* applicable exception;
* required transparency measure;
* responsible owner;
* implementation date;
* evidence requirements;
* legal/compliance review;
* final determination.

---

### 24. Step 1 — Identify the AI System

The first control is to establish whether the technology falls within the AI Act's definition of an AI system.

The organization should document:

* system name;
* supplier;
* model;
* model version;
* functionality;
* inputs;
* outputs;
* deployment environment;
* intended purpose;
* users;
* affected persons;
* interfaces;
* connected systems.

This information should come from the AI inventory rather than being reconstructed separately for each transparency review.

---

### 25. Step 2 — Determine the Regulatory Actor

The organization should determine whether it is acting as:

* provider;
* deployer;
* importer;
* distributor;
* product manufacturer;
* authorized representative;
* another actor covered by the AI Act.

Article 50 contains obligations that apply differently to providers and deployers.

For example:

| Scenario                                                  | Primary Article 50 focus |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| AI chatbot interacting with customers                     | Provider disclosure      |
| AI system generating synthetic content                    | Provider marking         |
| Employer using emotion recognition                        | Deployer disclosure      |
| Organization publishing a deepfake                        | Deployer disclosure      |
| Organization publishing AI-generated public-interest text | Deployer disclosure      |

Role determination should be documented because responsibility cannot reliably be inferred from who operates the software.

---

### 26. Step 3 — Determine the Applicable Article 50 Scenario

The assessment should test each relevant transparency scenario separately.

#### Scenario A — Direct AI Interaction

Determine whether:

* the system interacts directly with natural persons;
* the interaction is intended by design;
* the person is aware of the AI nature of the interaction;
* the AI nature is already obvious;
* the provider has implemented an appropriate disclosure.

#### Scenario B — Synthetic Content

Determine whether the system generates:

* audio;
* images;
* video;
* text.

Then determine whether Article 50(2) applies and whether a statutory exception applies.

#### Scenario C — Emotion Recognition

Determine whether the system performs emotion recognition.

If applicable, determine:

* who is exposed;
* where the system operates;
* who is the deployer;
* what disclosure is required;
* whether another AI Act prohibition or high-risk provision is independently relevant.

#### Scenario D — Biometric Categorisation

Determine whether the system performs biometric categorisation and whether Article 50(3) applies.

#### Scenario E — Deepfake

Determine whether AI-generated or manipulated:

* image;
* audio; or
* video

constitutes a deepfake.

Then determine whether the artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous-work provision applies.

#### Scenario F — Public-Interest Text

Determine whether AI-generated or manipulated text is published for the purpose of informing the public about matters of public interest.

Then assess whether the human-review/editorial-control exception applies.

---

### 27. Article 50 Decision Matrix

A GRC team can use a decision matrix such as:

| Question                                   | Yes                | No                            |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| Is the technology an AI system?            | Continue           | Record out of scope           |
| Direct interaction with natural persons?   | Assess Art. 50(1)  | Continue                      |
| AI nature already obvious?                 | Document rationale | Disclosure required           |
| Generates synthetic content?               | Assess Art. 50(2)  | Continue                      |
| Standard assistive editing exception?      | Document exception | Marking assessment required   |
| Emotion recognition?                       | Assess Art. 50(3)  | Continue                      |
| Biometric categorisation?                  | Assess Art. 50(3)  | Continue                      |
| Deepfake generation/manipulation?          | Assess Art. 50(4)  | Continue                      |
| Public-interest AI-generated text?         | Assess Art. 50(4)  | No Article 50(4) text trigger |
| Qualifying human review/editorial control? | Assess exception   | Disclosure required           |

The matrix is an internal governance tool.

It should not replace legal interpretation where applicability is uncertain.

---

### 28. Direct AI Interaction Control

For systems subject to Article 50(1), the organization should implement an explicit disclosure control.

The disclosure should be:

* clear;
* distinguishable;
* understandable;
* presented at the appropriate time;
* accessible;
* appropriate to the interaction.

Possible implementation mechanisms include:

* persistent interface label;
* opening message;
* visible assistant identity;
* voice disclosure;
* conversational notification;
* equivalent interface mechanism.

The organization should test the disclosure using the actual production interface.

A policy stating that the chatbot is "AI-powered" is not sufficient evidence if users never see that information.

---

### 29. First-Interaction Testing

A specific test should verify that disclosure occurs no later than the first interaction or exposure where required.

Test cases should include:

* new user;
* returning user;
* anonymous user;
* authenticated user;
* mobile interface;
* desktop interface;
* voice interface;
* accessibility interface;
* API-driven interface where relevant;
* multilingual interface;
* embedded third-party interface.

The test should record:

* timestamp;
* interface;
* user journey;
* disclosure content;
* disclosure position;
* screenshot or equivalent evidence;
* expected result;
* actual result;
* tester;
* test date.

---

### 30. Accessibility of Transparency Disclosures

Transparency information should be accessible to the relevant users.

Testing should consider:

* screen readers;
* keyboard navigation;
* sufficient text clarity;
* audio alternatives;
* language accessibility;
* mobile display;
* visual accessibility;
* cognitive accessibility where relevant.

A disclosure that technically exists but cannot reasonably be perceived by the affected user may create a compliance weakness.

Accessibility testing should therefore form part of the transparency control rather than being treated exclusively as a general UX requirement.

---

### 31. Synthetic Content Marking

For applicable systems generating synthetic content, providers should establish technical controls capable of supporting the required machine-readable marking.

The control should define:

* marking format;
* insertion point;
* metadata/provenance mechanism;
* persistence requirements;
* interoperability;
* detection method;
* integrity protection;
* failure handling;
* downstream transformation behavior.

The AI Act requires applicable markings to be effective, interoperable, robust and reliable as far as technically feasible, taking account of content-specific limitations and the state of the art.

The technical design should therefore be documented as part of the system's compliance architecture.

---

### 32. Marking Integrity

Machine-readable marking can be compromised through:

* metadata stripping;
* format conversion;
* screenshotting;
* transcoding;
* editing;
* compression;
* content reposting;
* platform transformation.

The organization should therefore assess:

* whether markings survive normal processing;
* what happens when they are removed;
* whether removal can be detected;
* whether alternative technical mechanisms are required;
* whether downstream systems preserve provenance information.

This is particularly important for AI-generated media distributed through third-party platforms.

---

### 33. Content Provenance Governance

Organizations should distinguish between:

**AI generation**

and

**provenance of AI-generated content**.

A mature provenance architecture can record:

* originating AI system;
* model;
* model version;
* generation timestamp;
* content type;
* relevant generation event;
* subsequent modifications;
* responsible organizational unit;
* provenance metadata;
* transformation history.

Where applicable, provenance information should be protected against unauthorized alteration.

The organization should also establish who owns provenance management:

* AI engineering;
* platform engineering;
* information security;
* content governance;
* legal/compliance;
* records management.

---

### 34. Human-Readable AI Content Disclosure

Machine-readable marking does not automatically satisfy every human-facing disclosure requirement.

Where deployer disclosure is required, the organization should provide a clear notice to the audience.

Examples include:

* "AI-generated image";
* "AI-manipulated video";
* "This audio has been artificially generated";
* "This content has been altered using AI."

The exact wording should be appropriate to:

* content type;
* audience;
* medium;
* legal requirement;
* accessibility requirements;
* communication context.

The disclosure should not be deliberately obscured through:

* tiny text;
* low-visibility placement;
* misleading wording;
* hidden menus;
* unnecessary legal jargon.

---

### 35. Deepfake Assessment

The organization should create a dedicated deepfake assessment.

The assessment should establish:

1. What content was generated or manipulated?
2. Is it image, audio or video?
3. Was AI used?
4. Was the content substantially generated or manipulated?
5. Does it constitute a deepfake under the applicable legal definition?
6. Who is the deployer?
7. Is the content publicly distributed?
8. Does the artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous-work exception apply?
9. What disclosure is required?
10. Where will the disclosure appear?
11. Who approves publication?
12. What evidence will be retained?

This assessment should be integrated into content-publishing workflows.

---

### 36. Public-Interest Text Assessment

Organizations that publish information to the public should create a separate assessment for AI-generated or manipulated text.

Relevant questions include:

* Is the text generated or manipulated by AI?
* Is it intended to inform the public?
* Does it concern a matter of public interest?
* Is a natural or legal person editorially responsible?
* Was human review performed?
* Was the content subject to editorial control?
* Does the statutory exception apply?
* Is disclosure required?
* Who approved publication?

Potentially relevant business functions include:

* communications;
* marketing;
* public affairs;
* media;
* investor relations;
* government relations;
* customer communications;
* emergency communications.

---

### 37. Human Review and Editorial Control

Where Article 50 contains an exception based on human review or editorial control, the organization should not simply mark a workflow as "human reviewed."

Evidence should demonstrate:

* reviewer identity;
* reviewer competence;
* review date;
* content reviewed;
* material changes made;
* approval decision;
* editorial authority;
* publication authorization.

A human merely clicking "approve" without meaningful review should not automatically be treated as effective human oversight or editorial control.

The organization should define what constitutes sufficient review for the relevant process.

---

### 38. Emotion Recognition Control

Organizations deploying emotion-recognition systems should establish a dedicated transparency control.

The control should identify:

* system capability;
* persons exposed;
* deployment location;
* purpose;
* data processed;
* disclosure mechanism;
* legal basis for processing where applicable;
* access restrictions;
* retention;
* monitoring;
* complaints mechanism.

The AI Act's transparency requirement operates alongside applicable data-protection law. Article 50 does not replace GDPR obligations.

Where the use also falls within a prohibited or high-risk category, those requirements must be assessed independently.

---

### 39. Biometric Categorisation Control

For biometric-categorisation systems, the organization should document:

* biometric modality;
* categories inferred;
* purpose;
* affected persons;
* deployment environment;
* regulatory classification;
* transparency obligation;
* privacy implications;
* security controls;
* access restrictions;
* retention;
* human oversight.

The system should also be screened against Article 5 because some biometric categorisation practices may be prohibited rather than merely subject to transparency requirements.

This is a critical GRC principle:

> **Transparency assessment must never replace prohibited-practice screening.**

---

### 40. Separation of AI Act Transparency From Privacy Notices

An organization should maintain a distinction between:

**AI transparency**

Information required because a person is:

* interacting with AI;
* exposed to AI-generated or manipulated content;
* exposed to emotion recognition;
* exposed to biometric categorisation;
* exposed to certain AI-generated public-interest text.

**Privacy transparency**

Information concerning:

* personal-data processing;
* legal basis;
* purposes;
* recipients;
* retention;
* rights;
* automated decision-making;
* international transfers;
* other GDPR requirements.

A privacy notice should not automatically be assumed to satisfy an Article 50 transparency obligation.

Likewise, an Article 50 disclosure does not automatically satisfy GDPR transparency requirements.

---

### 41. Transparency Control Testing

Transparency controls should be tested throughout the AI lifecycle.

**Design testing**

Determine whether the transparency mechanism is correctly designed.

**Implementation testing**

Determine whether it has actually been implemented.

**Operating-effectiveness testing**

Determine whether it operates consistently.

**User-experience testing**

Determine whether an ordinary affected person can perceive and understand it.

**Technical testing**

For machine-readable marking, test:

* metadata;
* provenance;
* integrity;
* detectability;
* persistence.

**Change testing**

Determine whether model, interface or deployment changes could remove or weaken transparency.

---

### 42. Negative Testing

Transparency controls should include negative test cases.

Examples:

* disclosure missing;
* disclosure delayed;
* disclosure displayed only after interaction;
* disclosure hidden on mobile;
* disclosure removed during interface redesign;
* metadata stripped;
* generated content converted to another format;
* third-party platform removes provenance;
* multilingual interface omits disclosure;
* accessibility mode fails to expose disclosure;
* model upgrade changes output-marking behavior.

The system should fail the compliance test when the legally required transparency mechanism is absent.

---

### 43. Change Management

Transparency obligations should be included in AI change-management workflows.

Changes requiring review may include:

* model replacement;
* model upgrade;
* interface redesign;
* new content modality;
* new user population;
* new deployment country;
* new language;
* new output type;
* new downstream platform;
* new vendor;
* new content workflow;
* new biometric capability;
* new emotion-recognition capability.

A change should trigger a transparency reassessment where it could affect:

* the responsible actor;
* the applicable exception;

* disclosure content;
* marking;
* detectability;
* accessibility.

Contracts should clearly allocate responsibilities.

### 50. Exception Management

* relevant facts;

* date;

### 51. Transparency Incident Management

* chatbot disclosure absent;
* incorrect disclosure;
* deepfake published without required disclosure;

* machine-readable marking malfunction;
* public-interest AI content incorrectly classified;
* emotion-recognition disclosure missing;
* biometric-categorisation disclosure missing.

The response process should include:

**Detect → Assess → Contain → Correct → Notify if Required → Preserve Evidence → Identify Root Cause → Improve Control**

Corrective actions may include:

* interface correction;
* content withdrawal;
* disclosure correction;
* technical remediation;
* vendor escalation;
* additional testing;
* staff training;
* process redesign.

### 54. Compliance Status Classification

The organization should avoid using only "compliant/non-compliant."

A more useful internal status model is:

**Not Assessed**
No applicability assessment completed.

**Not Applicable**
Documented assessment demonstrates that the relevant Article 50 requirement does not apply.

**Applicable — In Design**
Requirement identified but control not yet implemented.

**Applicable — Implemented**
Control implemented but operational effectiveness not yet validated.

**Compliant — Tested**
Control implemented and successfully tested.

**Exception Approved**
Requirement applicable but an authorized and documented exception applies.

**Deficient**
Required control is missing or ineffective.

**Under Remediation**
Deficiency identified and corrective action underway.

This status model provides better management visibility than a binary compliance indicator.

# Part 3 — Governance, Accountability, Risk and Assurance

### 56. Transparency Governance Framework

AI transparency should be governed as an enterprise compliance capability rather than delegated exclusively to product, engineering or communications teams.

The governance framework should establish:

* policy ownership;
* regulatory accountability;
* business ownership;
* technical ownership;
* legal interpretation;
* compliance monitoring;
* privacy coordination;
* security coordination;
* content governance;
* control ownership;
* exception authority;
* assurance responsibilities.

The governance objective is to ensure that transparency obligations remain effective when AI systems, models, interfaces, vendors, users or regulatory requirements change.

---

### 57. Board and Executive Oversight

Senior management should understand that AI transparency can create regulatory, operational, reputational and fundamental-rights exposure.

Board or executive reporting should address:

* material Article 50 exposure;
* significant transparency failures;
* recurring disclosure failures;
* major deepfake incidents;
* failures involving public-interest content;
* significant third-party transparency weaknesses;
* regulatory developments;
* overdue remediation;
* material exceptions.

The board does not need to approve every transparency label, but it should have sufficient visibility into material regulatory exposure and systemic control weaknesses.

---

### 58. AI Governance Committee

Organizations with significant AI deployment should consider assigning transparency oversight to an AI Governance Committee or equivalent governance body.

Responsibilities may include:

* approving the AI transparency policy;
* reviewing material applicability decisions;
* approving significant exceptions;
* reviewing transparency incidents;
* monitoring compliance metrics;
* reviewing regulatory developments;
* resolving cross-functional disputes;
* ensuring accountability between providers and deployers.

The committee should not replace operational control owners.

Its role is governance, challenge and escalation.

---

### 59. Three Lines Model

The Three Lines Model can be applied to AI transparency.

**First Line — Business and Technology**

Responsible for:

* implementing disclosures;
* maintaining technical marking;
* operating publishing controls;
* monitoring systems;
* preserving evidence.

**Second Line — AI Governance, Legal, Compliance, Privacy and Risk**

Responsible for:

* interpreting requirements;
* establishing methodology;
* challenging first-line decisions;
* monitoring compliance;
* reviewing exceptions;
* providing regulatory guidance.

**Third Line — Internal Audit**

Responsible for:

* independent assurance;
* control-effectiveness testing;
* governance assessment;
* evidence review;
* reporting deficiencies.

This separation reduces the risk that the team responsible for deploying AI becomes the sole judge of its own compliance.

---

Every Article 50 obligation should have a named accountable owner.

A practical accountability model includes:

| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |

| Internal Audit       | Independent assurance                        |

Accountability should be documented in the organization's AI governance framework.

### 61. Regulatory Interpretation Authority

Organizations should establish who has authority to interpret ambiguous Article 50 requirements.

Potentially disputed questions include:

* whether an interaction is direct;
* whether AI use is obvious;
* whether editing is merely assistive;
* whether content qualifies as a deepfake;
* whether a matter is of public interest;
* whether human review is sufficient;
* whether editorial control exists;
* whether an exception applies.

These decisions should not be made solely by developers.

Material ambiguity should be escalated to Legal and, where appropriate, AI Governance and Compliance.

---

### 62. Legal Uncertainty and Escalation

Transparency requirements may involve facts that are difficult to classify.

The organization should establish escalation thresholds.

For example:

**Low uncertainty**

→ Business and AI Governance determine applicability using approved methodology.

**Moderate uncertainty**

→ Compliance or Legal review required.

**High uncertainty**

→ Legal opinion required before deployment or publication.

**Material regulatory exposure**

→ Executive or AI Governance Committee escalation.

**Potential prohibited practice or fundamental-rights concern**

→ Immediate deployment hold pending legal and governance assessment.

The objective is to prevent uncertain legal interpretations from becoming uncontrolled production decisions.

---

### 63. Risk Appetite for Transparency

Organizations should define their tolerance for transparency failures.

A typical risk appetite framework could classify:

**Zero tolerance**

* intentional concealment of AI interaction;
* deliberate removal of required disclosures;
* deliberate misrepresentation of AI-generated content;
* intentional circumvention of Article 50 controls.

**Very low tolerance**

* repeated disclosure failures;
* systematic marking failures;
* unauthorized publication of deepfakes;
* recurring failure of transparency testing.

**Managed tolerance**

* isolated technical failures detected quickly and corrected;
* temporary non-material control degradation with documented compensating controls.

Risk appetite should be approved by appropriate management authority.

---

Transparency risks should be recorded in the enterprise AI risk register.

Examples include:

Risk owners should be assigned based on actual control responsibility.

### 65. Fundamental-Rights Governance

Transparency controls have a direct relationship with fundamental-rights protection.

The ability of a person to understand that AI is involved can affect:

* autonomy;
* dignity;
* informed participation;
* freedom of choice;
* ability to challenge an outcome;
* trust in information;
* protection against deception.

Transparency should therefore be considered within the organization's broader fundamental-rights risk framework.

However, transparency alone does not eliminate fundamental-rights risk.

An organization should separately evaluate whether the AI system:

* discriminates;
* manipulates;
* improperly profiles;
* interferes with protected rights;
* produces harmful decisions;
* creates disproportionate impacts.

---

### 66. Privacy Governance

AI transparency governance should be coordinated with privacy governance.

Relevant privacy questions include:

* Does the AI system process personal data?
* Does it process biometric data?
* Does it infer sensitive information?
* Is emotion recognition involved?
* What is the legal basis?
* What information must be provided under GDPR?
* How are data-subject rights supported?
* How long is data retained?
* Who receives the information?
* Is automated decision-making involved?

The organization should avoid creating two contradictory disclosure frameworks.

AI Act disclosures and GDPR information should be coordinated while preserving the distinct legal requirements of each framework.

---

### 67. Security Governance

Machine-readable marking and provenance mechanisms can become security-sensitive assets.

Threats may include:

* unauthorized alteration of provenance;
* metadata manipulation;
* removal of markings;
* spoofed AI-origin indicators;
* compromised content-management systems;
* unauthorized access to provenance repositories;
* manipulation of disclosure configurations;
* malicious model changes.

Security controls should therefore include:

* access control;
* integrity protection;
* authentication;
* cryptographic protection where appropriate;
* secure APIs;
* logging;
* vulnerability management;
* configuration management;
* monitoring.

A transparency mechanism that can be silently altered by an unauthorized user represents both a compliance and cybersecurity weakness.

---

### 68. Human Factors and Automation Bias

Transparency controls should account for human behavior.

Users may:

* ignore disclosures;
* misunderstand terminology;
* assume AI is more accurate than it is;
* confuse AI-generated content with verified information;
* treat a human-like AI assistant as a human;
* disregard labels after repeated exposure.

Therefore, transparency design should consider:

* wording;
* prominence;
* placement;
* timing;
* repetition where appropriate;
* user comprehension;
* context.

The purpose of transparency is not simply to technically display a label. It is to provide meaningful information to the affected person.

---

### 69. User Comprehension Testing

For material AI deployments, organizations should consider testing whether intended users understand the disclosure.

Testing can include:

* usability testing;
* comprehension surveys;
* controlled user studies;
* accessibility testing;
* multilingual testing;
* user interviews.

Questions may include:

> "Who or what were you interacting with?"

> "Did you understand that the content was AI-generated?"

> "Could you identify that the image had been manipulated?"

The results can identify situations where a technically compliant disclosure is ineffective in practice.

### 83. Transparency Incident Governance

A transparency incident should have a defined governance process:

**Detection**

Identify the failure.

**Triage**

Determine scope and severity.

**Containment**

Prevent continued exposure.

**Legal Assessment**

Determine regulatory implications.

**Correction**

Restore required transparency.

**Notification**

Determine whether any notification or reporting is required.

**Root Cause Analysis**

Identify why the control failed.

**Remediation**

Implement corrective action.

**Assurance**

Verify that the corrective action works.

The process should be integrated with enterprise incident management.

---

### 84. Corrective and Preventive Action

Corrective action should address both the immediate failure and the underlying cause.

For example:

**Problem:** AI-generated video published without disclosure.

**Immediate correction:** Add appropriate disclosure.

**Root cause:** Publishing system did not identify AI-generated media.

**Corrective action:** Add AI-content classification to publishing workflow.

**Preventive action:** Integrate AI inventory with content-management system.

**Assurance:** Conduct regression testing across all publishing channels.

This prevents recurring failures caused by superficial remediation.

### 93. Governance Principle

Transparency governance should establish a clear chain of accountability:

**Regulation → Interpretation → Applicability → Ownership → Control → Evidence → Testing → Monitoring → Escalation → Assurance**

The objective is to ensure that transparency obligations are not merely implemented technically but are governed as continuing regulatory responsibilities with accountable owners, independent challenge, documented decisions and demonstrable control effectiveness.
