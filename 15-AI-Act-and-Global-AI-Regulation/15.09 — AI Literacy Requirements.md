# 15.09 — AI Literacy Requirements

## Part 1 — Foundations

### 1. AI Literacy as an AI Governance Requirement

AI literacy is a foundational component of responsible AI governance because the effectiveness of AI controls depends not only on technology, policies, and technical safeguards, but also on whether people understand how AI systems operate, where their limitations lie, and how they are expected to use them.

Under Article 4 of the EU AI Act, providers and deployers of AI systems must take measures to support the development of AI literacy among their staff and other persons who operate or use AI systems on their behalf. The obligation requires consideration of the person's technical knowledge, experience, education and training, the context in which the AI system is used, and the persons or groups on whom the AI system is used.

AI literacy should therefore be treated as an organizational control rather than simply an employee-training initiative.

An effective AI governance program should establish a connection between:

* AI system risk;
* intended use;
* user responsibilities;
* required knowledge;
* training and awareness;
* human oversight;
* acceptable-use requirements;
* escalation procedures;
* security and privacy responsibilities;
* regulatory obligations; and
* evidence of competency development.

The objective is not to make every employee an AI engineer. The objective is to ensure that people who interact with AI systems possess the knowledge necessary to use, supervise, govern, or challenge those systems appropriately.

### 2. Current Legal Position of Article 4

Article 4 entered into application on **2 February 2025**. The obligation therefore already applies and should not be treated as a future compliance requirement.

The provision was subsequently amended by the **2026 Digital Omnibus on AI**. The amended Article 4 requires providers and deployers to take measures to support the development of AI literacy rather than requiring them to guarantee a specific level of AI literacy for every individual.

This distinction is important for GRC programs.

The legal obligation is therefore better understood as a **reasonable organizational capability-building obligation** rather than a universal examination requirement.

An organization should not interpret the amended wording as meaning that training is optional. The organization remains responsible for taking appropriate measures to develop AI literacy.

### 3. Applicability to Providers and Deployers

Article 4 applies to both:

* **providers** of AI systems; and
* **deployers** of AI systems.

This creates a broad organizational scope.

A provider developing an AI-powered product may need AI literacy among software engineers, product managers, security personnel, compliance personnel, support teams, and other individuals involved in operating or using the system.

A deployer using an AI system internally may need AI literacy among:

* employees;
* contractors;
* administrators;
* business users;
* supervisors;
* managers;
* human reviewers;
* compliance personnel;
* security personnel; and
* other persons operating or using the system on its behalf.

The relevant population is therefore determined by the organization's AI activities rather than by job title alone.

### 4. AI Literacy Is Not Limited to Technical Employees

A common governance failure is to treat AI literacy as an IT or data-science responsibility.

AI systems can influence decisions made by:

* HR;
* finance;
* legal;
* procurement;
* customer service;
* marketing;
* operations;
* healthcare;
* education;
* security;
* risk management;
* compliance;
* executive management; and
* ordinary business users.

Consequently, AI literacy should be distributed according to **role and exposure**, not organizational department.

For example, an employee using a generative AI assistant to draft correspondence may need to understand confidential-information restrictions and hallucination risks, while an AI engineer may additionally require knowledge of model security, evaluation, adversarial testing, data poisoning, and model supply-chain risks.

### 5. AI Literacy Is Context-Dependent

Article 4 explicitly requires consideration of the context in which the AI system is used and the persons or groups affected by that system.

This means an organization should not assume that one generic AI training course is sufficient for every employee.

AI literacy requirements should reflect:

* the type of AI system;
* the intended purpose;
* the user's role;
* the degree of human involvement;
* the consequences of incorrect output;
* the sensitivity of the data;
* the population affected;
* the level of autonomy;
* the applicable regulatory requirements;
* the security threat environment; and
* the organization's internal risk classification.

A user operating a low-impact productivity assistant and a human reviewer supervising an AI system involved in employment decisions should not necessarily receive identical training.

### 6. AI Literacy Versus AI Expertise

AI literacy and AI expertise are different capabilities.

**AI literacy** generally concerns the ability to understand and appropriately interact with AI systems.

**AI expertise** involves deeper technical or professional competence, such as:

* machine-learning engineering;
* model architecture;
* statistical evaluation;
* adversarial machine learning;
* AI security engineering;
* model governance;
* AI assurance;
* AI risk management;
* algorithmic auditing; or
* regulatory interpretation.

An enterprise AI governance program should therefore define different competency levels rather than attempting to make all personnel AI experts.

A practical competency architecture may include:

| Level                | Typical population                           | Primary capability                                       |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Level 1 — Awareness  | General workforce                            | Recognize AI, basic risks and acceptable use             |
| Level 2 — User       | Operational AI users                         | Safely use AI and recognize unreliable output            |
| Level 3 — Supervisor | Human reviewers/managers                     | Evaluate AI output and exercise meaningful oversight     |
| Level 4 — Specialist | Technical/risk professionals                 | Assess technical, security, privacy and governance risks |
| Level 5 — Governance | Executives, legal, compliance, AI governance | Make risk, regulatory and accountability decisions       |

This structure is an organizational governance model rather than a legally mandated certification framework.

### 7. AI Literacy Should Be Risk-Based

AI literacy should be proportional to the risk associated with the person's interaction with AI.

A low-risk employee using an AI writing assistant may require awareness of:

* confidential information;
* personal data;
* hallucinations;
* copyright;
* prohibited uses;
* verification requirements; and
* organizational acceptable-use rules.

A person supervising a high-impact AI system may additionally need knowledge of:

* system limitations;
* performance metrics;
* human oversight;
* bias and discrimination;
* escalation procedures;
* incident reporting;
* decision override;
* logging;
* documentation;
* regulatory obligations; and
* fundamental-rights risks.

A person developing an AI security control may require substantially deeper expertise.

The organization should therefore maintain a documented relationship between **AI risk classification and required competency**.

### 8. AI Literacy and Human Oversight

AI literacy is closely connected to human oversight.

A human cannot provide meaningful oversight of an AI system if the human does not understand:

* what the system is designed to do;
* what it is not designed to do;
* how reliable its output is;
* when the output requires verification;
* what warning signs indicate failure;
* when the system should not be used;
* when a decision must be escalated; and
* when human intervention must override the AI output.

For high-risk AI systems, this becomes particularly important because the AI Act establishes specific requirements concerning human oversight.

AI literacy should therefore be incorporated into the organization's human-oversight design rather than treated as an independent training activity.

### 9. AI Literacy and Human Agency

AI literacy supports human agency by reducing inappropriate reliance on automated recommendations.

Users should understand that AI output is generally an input to human decision-making rather than automatically authoritative evidence.

Training should address risks such as:

* automation bias;
* over-reliance;
* confirmation bias;
* authority bias;
* anchoring on AI recommendations;
* failure to challenge plausible-looking output;
* treating generated content as verified fact; and
* assuming confidence expressed by an AI system represents factual certainty.

The objective is not merely to teach users how to operate AI interfaces.

It is to teach users **when to trust, verify, challenge, escalate, or reject AI output**.

### 10. AI Literacy and Generative AI

Generative AI creates a particularly broad AI literacy requirement because ordinary employees can become AI users without formal technical onboarding.

Generative AI literacy should address, as appropriate:

* hallucination;
* fabricated citations;
* inaccurate reasoning;
* prompt manipulation;
* prompt injection;
* sensitive-data disclosure;
* confidential-information leakage;
* intellectual-property risks;
* personal-data processing;
* insecure generated code;
* malicious or misleading generated content;
* deepfakes;
* synthetic media;
* output verification;
* human review;
* acceptable-use restrictions; and
* incident escalation.

The organization should distinguish between **using an approved enterprise AI service** and **using an unapproved public AI service**.

Both may create different security, privacy, contractual, and regulatory risks.

### 11. AI Literacy and Cybersecurity

AI literacy is increasingly part of cybersecurity awareness.

Employees should understand that AI systems introduce security risks that differ from conventional software.

Depending on the user's role, awareness may include:

* prompt injection;
* indirect prompt injection;
* data exfiltration through AI workflows;
* malicious documents;
* model manipulation;
* poisoned training data;
* insecure plugins or integrations;
* excessive AI permissions;
* insecure generated code;
* model or API credential exposure;
* supply-chain risks;
* adversarial inputs;
* unauthorized model access;
* model extraction;
* sensitive-output leakage; and
* abuse of autonomous AI agents.

Security teams may require substantially deeper training covering threat modeling, adversarial testing, AI-specific attack surfaces, model security, and AI incident response.

### 12. AI Literacy and Privacy

AI literacy should also incorporate data-protection awareness.

Users should understand when AI processing may involve:

* personal data;
* special categories of personal data;
* confidential business information;
* employee information;
* customer information;
* authentication data;
* proprietary information;
* legally privileged information; or
* regulated information.

Employees should know the organization's approved mechanisms for handling such information.

AI literacy should therefore connect with:

* privacy policies;
* data-classification policies;
* retention requirements;
* access controls;
* data minimization;
* approved AI tools;
* vendor requirements; and
* incident reporting.

AI literacy does not replace GDPR or other privacy training. It supplements those controls by addressing AI-specific processing risks.

### 13. AI Literacy and Accuracy

AI literacy must include the ability to recognize that an AI-generated answer can be fluent, coherent, and wrong.

Users should understand the difference between:

* generated content;
* verified information;
* authoritative information;
* statistical prediction;
* recommendation;
* classification;
* inference; and
* factual evidence.

The appropriate verification method should depend on the use case.

For example:

* legal information may require verification against authoritative legal sources;
* financial information may require validation against approved financial systems;
* cybersecurity recommendations may require technical testing;
* medical information may require qualified professional review;
* employment decisions may require appropriate human assessment and applicable legal safeguards.

The organization should avoid training users to treat a generic “AI disclaimer” as sufficient risk management.

### 14. AI Literacy and Role-Based Competence

An effective AI literacy program should define competencies by role.

For example:

**General users** should understand acceptable use, confidentiality, privacy, verification, and reporting.

**Managers** should understand accountability, approval requirements, human oversight, and AI-related decision risks.

**Developers** should understand secure AI development, testing, data handling, model limitations, and AI security.

**Data scientists** should understand evaluation, bias, robustness, data quality, model limitations, and model governance.

**Security professionals** should understand AI-specific threats, attack surfaces, monitoring, incident response, and adversarial testing.

**Legal and compliance personnel** should understand AI regulatory classification, obligations, evidence, documentation, and regulatory change.

**Executives and boards** should understand strategic AI risk, regulatory exposure, accountability, risk appetite, and material AI incidents.

### 15. AI Literacy Is a Lifecycle Capability

AI literacy should not be limited to employee onboarding.

AI capabilities, technologies, threats, and regulatory obligations evolve continuously.

A sustainable AI literacy program should therefore operate across the AI lifecycle:

1. AI strategy;
2. procurement;
3. design;
4. development;
5. testing;
6. deployment;
7. operation;
8. monitoring;
9. incident management;
10. material change;
11. regulatory reassessment; and
12. retirement.

Training requirements should be reassessed when an AI system changes materially or when a user's responsibilities change.

### 16. AI Literacy and Organizational Accountability

Article 4 creates an organizational responsibility for providers and deployers to take measures supporting AI literacy.

Responsibility should therefore not be assigned exclusively to the Human Resources department.

A mature operating model typically distributes accountability across:

* executive management;
* AI Governance;
* Legal and Compliance;
* Human Resources/Learning and Development;
* Information Security;
* Privacy;
* Data Governance;
* Engineering;
* Risk Management;
* Internal Audit; and
* business-unit leadership.

HR or Learning and Development may operate the training platform, but AI Governance should generally define the competency requirements associated with AI risk.

### 17. AI Literacy as a Preventive Control

AI literacy is primarily a **preventive governance control**.

It reduces the likelihood that personnel will:

* deploy AI incorrectly;
* disclose sensitive information;
* rely on unreliable output;
* bypass required approval;
* misunderstand regulatory restrictions;
* ignore AI security warnings;
* fail to recognize AI-generated misinformation;
* misuse AI capabilities; or
* fail to escalate an AI incident.

The control should therefore be integrated with other preventive controls rather than treated as an isolated awareness campaign.

### 18. AI Literacy and the Three Lines Model

AI literacy responsibilities can be aligned with the Three Lines Model.

**First Line — Operations**

Business and technical teams need practical competence to operate AI systems safely.

**Second Line — Risk, Compliance, Privacy and Security**

These functions need deeper competency to establish standards, assess risk, monitor compliance, and advise the first line.

**Third Line — Internal Audit**

Internal Audit needs sufficient AI literacy to evaluate whether AI governance, risk management, and controls operate effectively.

Internal auditors do not necessarily need to become AI engineers. However, they need sufficient understanding to assess whether the organization's AI-related controls are appropriately designed and operating.

### 19. AI Literacy Evidence

An organization should be able to demonstrate that it has taken reasonable measures to develop AI literacy.

Potential evidence includes:

* AI literacy policy;
* competency framework;
* training requirements;
* role-to-training matrix;
* training curricula;
* learning records;
* attendance records;
* assessment results;
* completion dashboards;
* awareness communications;
* acceptable-use acknowledgements;
* role-specific training;
* technical training;
* refresher training;
* training exceptions;
* remediation records;
* competency assessments;
* management reports; and
* periodic program reviews.

Training completion alone should not automatically be treated as proof of effective AI literacy.

The organization should also evaluate whether the training is appropriate for the individual's actual role and AI exposure.

### 20. AI Literacy Effectiveness

A mature AI literacy program should measure effectiveness rather than only activity.

Weak measurements include:

* number of employees invited;
* number of courses published;
* number of emails sent;
* percentage of employees assigned training.

Stronger measurements may include:

* training completion by risk tier;
* assessment performance;
* recurring AI-related incidents;
* confidential-data leakage events;
* policy violations;
* inappropriate AI usage;
* successful identification of AI-generated misinformation;
* escalation quality;
* phishing or prompt-injection simulation results;
* user comprehension;
* role-specific competency;
* remediation rates; and
* changes in AI incident patterns.

The purpose is to determine whether the organization is actually reducing human-related AI risk.

### 21. AI Literacy and Regulatory Evidence

AI literacy should be integrated into the organization's broader AI compliance evidence architecture.

For each material AI use case, the organization should be able to determine:

* who operates the system;
* who supervises it;
* what competency is required;
* what training is assigned;
* whether required training was completed;
* whether additional specialist competency is required;
* what evidence demonstrates the training;
* when competency was last reviewed; and
* whether material system changes require additional training.

This creates an auditable relationship between:

**AI system → risk → role → competency → training → evidence → oversight.**

### 22. AI Literacy as a Governance Culture

AI literacy ultimately extends beyond formal courses.

An effective AI governance culture encourages personnel to:

* question AI output;
* report unexpected behavior;
* challenge inappropriate AI use;
* escalate uncertainty;
* verify important results;
* protect confidential information;
* respect human decision-making;
* recognize AI limitations;
* follow approved AI processes; and
* stop or escalate AI use when risks are unclear.

The organization should create an environment in which asking **“Should we use AI for this?”** is considered a normal governance question.

Similarly, employees should feel authorized to ask:

* “Is this AI system approved?”
* “Can I enter this data into the system?”
* “Does this use case require human review?”
* “Can I rely on this output?”
* “Who is accountable for this decision?”
* “What should I do if the system behaves unexpectedly?”
* “Does this change the system's regulatory classification?”
* “Should this incident be reported?”

This culture is an important practical expression of AI literacy and an essential foundation for an effective AI Security Governance program.

# Part 2 — AI Literacy Assessment, Controls, and Implementation

### 23. AI Literacy Governance Assessment

An organization should establish a formal process for determining the AI literacy requirements associated with each role, AI system, and use case.

The assessment should identify:

* the AI system involved;
* the system's regulatory classification;
* the person's relationship to the system;
* the person's authority over the system;
* the type of AI activity performed;
* the potential consequences of misuse;
* affected individuals or groups;
* required technical knowledge;
* required regulatory knowledge;
* required security and privacy knowledge;
* required human-oversight capability;
* required training;
* required competency evidence; and
* reassessment triggers.

The assessment should produce a documented **AI Literacy Requirement Record**.

### 24. AI Literacy Role Mapping

AI literacy requirements should be mapped to organizational roles rather than relying exclusively on job titles.

A role mapping process can classify personnel according to functions such as:

| Role category              | Typical AI exposure                      | Literacy requirement               |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| General employee           | Uses approved AI tools                   | Basic AI awareness                 |
| AI user                    | Regularly creates or evaluates AI output | User-level competency              |
| AI supervisor              | Reviews or overrides AI output           | Oversight competency               |
| AI administrator           | Configures or manages AI systems         | Technical and security competency  |
| AI developer               | Builds or modifies AI systems            | Advanced technical competency      |
| AI risk specialist         | Assesses AI risk                         | Risk and regulatory competency     |
| AI governance professional | Governs AI compliance                    | Advanced governance competency     |
| Executive decision-maker   | Approves material AI use                 | Strategic AI governance competency |
| Internal auditor           | Provides independent assurance           | AI assurance competency            |

The matrix should be maintained as a controlled governance artifact.

### 25. AI Literacy Risk Tiering

The organization should establish literacy tiers based on AI-related risk.

A practical model can use factors such as:

* regulatory classification;
* impact on individuals;
* degree of automation;
* sensitivity of information;
* autonomy;
* scale of deployment;
* decision significance;
* security exposure;
* privacy exposure;
* likelihood of misuse; and
* potential severity of harm.

Higher-risk roles should require stronger competency requirements.

This prevents the organization from applying the same training package to every employee regardless of their exposure.

### 26. AI Literacy Requirement Register

Each material AI system should have an associated literacy requirement record containing, at minimum:

* AI system identifier;
* business owner;
* technical owner;
* provider/deployer role;
* intended purpose;
* regulatory classification;
* affected population;
* user groups;
* required competency levels;
* mandatory training;
* optional specialist training;
* assessment requirements;
* refresher frequency;
* training exceptions;
* evidence location;
* last review date; and
* next review trigger.

The register should be connected to the organization's AI inventory.

### 27. AI Literacy Control Objective

The primary control objective should be:

> Personnel operating or using AI systems on behalf of the organization possess knowledge and competence appropriate to their role, the AI system, and the context in which it is used.

Supporting objectives should include:

* preventing inappropriate AI use;
* supporting meaningful human oversight;
* reducing security incidents;
* reducing privacy incidents;
* improving output verification;
* supporting regulatory compliance;
* improving escalation;
* maintaining competency after material changes; and
* producing auditable evidence.

### 28. AI-LIT-01 — AI Literacy Policy

The organization should establish an AI Literacy Policy defining:

* scope;
* applicability;
* roles and responsibilities;
* competency expectations;
* training requirements;
* risk-based training tiers;
* assessment requirements;
* refresher requirements;
* exceptions;
* evidence retention;
* monitoring;
* reporting;
* enforcement; and
* review requirements.

The policy should distinguish between mandatory regulatory requirements and internally established controls.

### 29. AI-LIT-02 — AI Literacy Competency Framework

The organization should maintain a competency framework describing what personnel are expected to understand at each AI literacy level.

Competencies may include:

* AI fundamentals;
* AI limitations;
* hallucination;
* data protection;
* cybersecurity;
* intellectual property;
* AI Act requirements;
* acceptable use;
* human oversight;
* bias;
* discrimination;
* transparency;
* incident reporting;
* prompt security;
* AI output verification;
* model limitations; and
* organizational escalation.

Technical roles may require additional competencies such as:

* model evaluation;
* robustness;
* adversarial testing;
* secure development;
* model supply-chain security;
* model monitoring; and
* AI-specific threat modeling.

### 30. AI-LIT-03 — Role-Based Training Matrix

The organization should create a matrix linking each role to required training.

For example:

| Role              | Awareness |   Secure Use |      Privacy |    AI Act |    Technical |    Oversight |
| ----------------- | --------: | -----------: | -----------: | --------: | -----------: | -----------: |
| General user      |  Required |     Required |     Required |  Required |        Basic |        Basic |
| AI supervisor     |  Required |     Required |     Required |  Required | Intermediate |     Advanced |
| Developer         |  Required |     Required |     Required |  Required |     Advanced | Intermediate |
| Security engineer |  Required |     Required |     Required |  Required |     Advanced |     Advanced |
| Legal/Compliance  |  Required | Intermediate |     Advanced |  Advanced | Intermediate |     Advanced |
| Executive         |  Required |        Basic |        Basic | Strategic |        Basic |    Strategic |
| Internal Audit    |  Required | Intermediate | Intermediate |  Advanced | Intermediate |     Advanced |

The exact matrix should be determined by the organization's AI environment.

### 31. AI-LIT-04 — AI Literacy Onboarding

AI literacy requirements should be incorporated into onboarding.

Personnel should not receive unrestricted access to material AI systems before completing required training where the organization has determined that training is a prerequisite for safe operation.

The onboarding workflow should connect:

**identity → role → AI access → required competency → training → authorization.**

This creates a preventive control rather than relying on voluntary employee education.

### 32. AI-LIT-05 — Access-Linked AI Training

For systems presenting material risk, AI access should be technically or procedurally linked to competency requirements.

Possible controls include:

* mandatory training before access;
* role-based access;
* privileged-access restrictions;
* manager approval;
* competency validation;
* periodic recertification;
* removal of access after prolonged non-compliance; and
* enhanced training for privileged users.

This is particularly relevant where AI systems can access:

* sensitive personal information;
* confidential business data;
* production environments;
* security infrastructure;
* regulated systems;
* customer information; or
* decision-making workflows.

### 33. AI-LIT-06 — Generative AI Acceptable-Use Training

Personnel using generative AI should receive training appropriate to the organization's approved use cases.

Training should address:

* approved AI platforms;
* prohibited inputs;
* confidential information;
* personal data;
* credentials and secrets;
* proprietary information;
* output verification;
* copyright;
* hallucinations;
* generated code;
* external sharing;
* prompt injection;
* malicious documents;
* third-party integrations; and
* reporting procedures.

The training should correspond to the actual capabilities of the tools available to employees.

### 34. AI-LIT-07 — AI Output Verification Competency

Where AI-generated output can affect business decisions, personnel should be trained to verify output appropriately.

Verification should consider:

* source reliability;
* factual accuracy;
* completeness;
* context;
* bias;
* mathematical correctness;
* logical consistency;
* legal implications;
* security implications; and
* consequences of error.

The required verification threshold should increase with the potential impact of the decision.

### 35. AI-LIT-08 — Human Oversight Competency

Personnel assigned human-oversight responsibilities should receive specialized training.

The training should address:

* system purpose;
* known limitations;
* expected operating range;
* performance indicators;
* error conditions;
* escalation procedures;
* override mechanisms;
* intervention authority;
* automation bias;
* human decision-making;
* monitoring information; and
* circumstances requiring suspension of AI use.

A person should not be designated as a human overseer merely because that person is physically present in the workflow.

### 36. AI-LIT-09 — High-Risk AI Competency

Personnel involved with high-risk AI systems should receive training proportionate to their responsibilities.

Competencies may include:

* intended purpose;
* relevant limitations;
* human oversight;
* performance characteristics;
* foreseeable misuse;
* fundamental-rights considerations;
* incident reporting;
* record keeping;
* privacy;
* cybersecurity;
* decision review;
* escalation; and
* applicable organizational procedures.

For high-risk systems, literacy should be incorporated into the human-oversight and operational-control design.

### 37. AI-LIT-10 — AI Security Competency

Personnel with technical or security responsibilities should receive AI-specific cybersecurity training.

Topics may include:

* prompt injection;
* indirect prompt injection;
* model manipulation;
* data poisoning;
* adversarial examples;
* model extraction;
* model inversion;
* sensitive-output leakage;
* insecure tool use;
* agent abuse;
* excessive permissions;
* model supply-chain compromise;
* malicious models;
* compromised dependencies;
* API security;
* model endpoint security; and
* AI incident response.

The depth of training should correspond to technical responsibility.

### 38. AI-LIT-11 — AI Privacy Competency

Personnel processing personal data through AI systems should understand:

* approved processing purposes;
* data minimization;
* lawful processing requirements;
* sensitive information;
* retention;
* access restrictions;
* data leakage;
* external AI services;
* vendor processing;
* data subject considerations;
* privacy incidents; and
* escalation to Privacy or Data Protection functions.

AI literacy should complement, not replace, applicable data-protection training.

### 39. AI-LIT-12 — AI Regulatory Literacy

Personnel whose responsibilities involve AI governance should receive regulatory training appropriate to their role.

Relevant topics may include:

* EU AI Act;
* risk classification;
* prohibited practices;
* high-risk systems;
* transparency obligations;
* GPAI requirements;
* AI literacy;
* provider and deployer responsibilities;
* technical documentation;
* conformity assessment;
* post-market monitoring;
* incident reporting;
* enforcement;
* regulatory transition dates; and
* applicable national or sectoral requirements.

The training should be updated when material regulatory changes occur.

### 40. AI-LIT-13 — Fundamental-Rights and Bias Awareness

Where AI systems can affect individuals, relevant personnel should understand:

* discrimination risks;
* unfair outcomes;
* protected characteristics;
* proxy variables;
* biased training data;
* unequal error rates;
* accessibility;
* human dignity;
* autonomy;
* privacy;
* due process;
* contestability; and
* inappropriate reliance on automated recommendations.

This is especially important for systems used in areas such as:

* employment;
* education;
* access to services;
* law enforcement;
* migration;
* credit;
* insurance; and
* other high-impact decisions.

### 41. AI-LIT-14 — AI Incident Awareness

Personnel should understand what constitutes an AI-related incident or near miss.

Examples include:

* harmful AI output;
* discriminatory output;
* unauthorized disclosure;
* security compromise;
* prompt injection;
* unexpected autonomous behavior;
* model malfunction;
* inaccurate high-impact recommendation;
* prohibited use;
* missing transparency disclosure;
* failure of human oversight;
* unauthorized AI deployment; and
* material deviation from intended purpose.

Employees should know how and where to report such events.

### 42. AI-LIT-15 — AI Acceptable-Use Attestation

Organizations may require personnel to acknowledge AI acceptable-use requirements.

An attestation may confirm that the individual understands:

* approved AI systems;
* prohibited uses;
* data restrictions;
* verification obligations;
* security requirements;
* privacy requirements;
* human-oversight requirements;
* reporting obligations; and
* consequences of non-compliance.

An attestation should supplement training rather than substitute for it.

### 43. AI-LIT-16 — AI Literacy Assessment

For higher-risk roles, the organization should assess competency rather than relying solely on course completion.

Assessment methods may include:

* quizzes;
* scenario-based examinations;
* practical exercises;
* simulations;
* tabletop exercises;
* supervised operation;
* technical assessments;
* case studies; and
* role-specific competency demonstrations.

The assessment should test whether the employee can apply the knowledge in realistic situations.

### 44. Scenario-Based AI Literacy Testing

Scenario-based testing is particularly useful because AI risks often arise from context rather than from simple factual knowledge.

A scenario might ask an employee:

> A generative AI service requests confidential customer information to improve the answer. What should you do?

Another might ask:

> An AI system produces a recommendation that appears plausible but conflicts with authoritative business data. What is the appropriate response?

The objective is to test judgment, not memorization.

### 45. AI Literacy Simulation

Organizations with material AI exposure should consider simulations.

Examples include:

* prompt-injection simulations;
* sensitive-data handling exercises;
* AI phishing scenarios;
* deepfake identification exercises;
* hallucination-detection exercises;
* human-oversight simulations;
* AI incident tabletop exercises; and
* AI acceptable-use scenarios.

Simulation results can identify weaknesses that conventional training completion statistics cannot reveal.

### 46. AI-LIT-17 — Refresher Training

AI literacy should be refreshed periodically and when material changes occur.

Refresher triggers may include:

* new AI systems;
* major model changes;
* new AI capabilities;
* material security incidents;
* regulatory changes;
* policy changes;
* changes in employee responsibilities;
* new prohibited uses;
* new privacy requirements;
* new threats; or
* recurring control failures.

The frequency should be risk-based.

### 47. AI-LIT-18 — Change-Triggered Training

Material changes to an AI system should trigger a review of competency requirements.

Examples include:

* introduction of autonomous agents;
* new external integrations;
* access to additional data;
* new decision-making capabilities;
* material model changes;
* new deployment environments;
* expanded user populations;
* changes to human oversight;
* changes to regulatory classification; or
* new security threats.

Training should be updated before or as part of the relevant change-management process where appropriate.

### 48. AI-LIT-19 — Training Exception Management

Training exceptions should be formally controlled.

An exception record should include:

* individual or role;
* affected AI system;
* requirement being waived;
* reason;
* business justification;
* risk assessment;
* compensating controls;
* approving authority;
* expiration date;
* remediation plan; and
* evidence.

Exceptions should not silently convert mandatory competency requirements into optional activities.

### 49. AI-LIT-20 — Contractor and Third-Party Literacy

AI literacy requirements should extend to relevant contractors and third parties where they operate or use AI systems on the organization's behalf.

The organization should determine:

* which external personnel require training;
* who provides the training;
* what evidence is required;
* whether equivalent third-party training is acceptable;
* how competency is verified; and
* how non-compliance is handled.

Contracts should establish these requirements where appropriate.

### 50. AI-LIT-21 — Vendor AI Literacy Requirements

Where an organization relies on a third party to operate an AI system, vendor due diligence should consider whether the provider maintains appropriate AI competency.

Relevant evidence may include:

* staff competency framework;
* AI security training;
* privacy training;
* incident response training;
* AI governance roles;
* specialist qualifications;
* training completion;
* assurance reports; and
* competency requirements for privileged personnel.

The organization should avoid assuming that a vendor's technical reputation automatically establishes AI governance competence.

### 51. AI-LIT-22 — Management Training

Managers should receive additional AI governance training because they often approve AI use and supervise employees using AI.

Management training should address:

* AI accountability;
* acceptable use;
* risk escalation;
* human oversight;
* employee monitoring considerations;
* AI-related incidents;
* regulatory obligations;
* decision accountability;
* procurement controls;
* vendor risk; and
* consequences of inappropriate AI deployment.

Managers should understand that delegation of AI operation does not necessarily eliminate management accountability.

### 52. AI-LIT-23 — Executive and Board AI Literacy

Executives and boards should receive AI governance education appropriate to their oversight responsibilities.

The focus should include:

* strategic AI risk;
* material regulatory exposure;
* risk appetite;
* significant AI deployments;
* high-risk use cases;
* systemic AI dependencies;
* major incidents;
* cybersecurity;
* privacy;
* fundamental rights;
* third-party concentration;
* AI investment decisions; and
* assurance results.

Board-level AI literacy should focus on oversight rather than technical implementation.

### 53. AI-LIT-24 — Internal Audit AI Literacy

Internal Audit should maintain sufficient AI competency to provide effective assurance.

Auditors should be able to evaluate:

* AI governance;
* AI inventories;
* risk classification;
* AI literacy controls;
* training evidence;
* access controls;
* human oversight;
* security controls;
* privacy controls;
* transparency controls;
* incident management;
* regulatory compliance; and
* control effectiveness.

Specialist expertise may be obtained when the audit requires technical capabilities beyond the internal audit team's competence.

### 54. AI-LIT-25 — AI Literacy Evidence Repository

Training and competency evidence should be stored in a controlled repository.

The evidence model should support:

* employee identification;
* role;
* AI system;
* competency tier;
* training assignment;
* completion date;
* assessment result;
* expiration or review date;
* exception status;
* remediation;
* evidence source; and
* audit history.

Access to the repository should be appropriately restricted because training records may contain personnel information.

### 55. AI Literacy Control Testing

AI literacy controls should be periodically tested.

Testing should determine whether:

* required roles have been identified;
* training requirements are risk-based;
* training is assigned correctly;
* required training is completed;
* assessments are effective;
* exceptions are controlled;
* access is appropriately linked to competency;
* refresher triggers operate;
* material changes trigger training review;
* contractor requirements are enforced; and
* evidence is complete and reliable.

Sampling should include both compliant and potentially non-compliant populations.

### 56. AI Literacy Control Effectiveness

Control effectiveness should distinguish between:

**Design effectiveness**

Whether the AI literacy framework is appropriately designed.

**Operating effectiveness**

Whether the controls operate consistently.

**Outcome effectiveness**

Whether the program actually reduces AI-related human risk.

For example, a 99% training completion rate does not necessarily demonstrate outcome effectiveness if employees continue to disclose confidential information into unauthorized AI systems.

### 57. AI Literacy Dashboard

Management reporting should provide meaningful visibility into AI literacy.

Potential metrics include:

* required training completion;
* overdue training;
* competency assessment results;
* high-risk role coverage;
* privileged-user competency;
* contractor compliance;
* training exceptions;
* repeat failures;
* AI-related incidents;
* near misses;
* simulation performance;
* remediation status; and
* change-triggered training completion.

Metrics should be segmented by risk level and business function where useful.

### 58. AI Literacy Risk Indicators

Potential KRIs include:

* percentage of AI users without required training;
* percentage of high-risk AI operators without validated competency;
* unauthorized AI usage;
* AI-related data leakage incidents;
* AI security incidents involving user error;
* failed human-oversight exercises;
* repeated AI policy violations;
* overdue mandatory training;
* expired competency certifications;
* unresolved training exceptions; and
* incidents attributable to misunderstanding of AI limitations.

These indicators can be incorporated into the organization's broader AI risk dashboard.

### 59. AI Literacy Audit Trail

The organization should maintain an auditable chain showing:

**requirement → role → assignment → training → assessment → competency → authorization → monitoring → reassessment.**

This allows an auditor, regulator, or management reviewer to reconstruct why an individual was considered appropriately trained for a particular AI responsibility.

### 60. AI Literacy and Access Recertification

Where AI systems are material or sensitive, access recertification should include competency status.

During periodic access reviews, managers should confirm:

* the user still requires access;
* the user's role remains appropriate;
* required training remains current;
* required competency remains valid;
* the user understands current system capabilities; and
* material system changes have been incorporated into training.

Access should not continue indefinitely merely because it was approved at initial deployment.

### 61. AI Literacy and Privileged Access

Privileged AI users may require enhanced competency controls.

Examples include personnel able to:

* modify system prompts;
* change safety configurations;
* access model weights;
* modify AI policies;
* change tool permissions;
* connect external data sources;
* change model versions;
* disable monitoring;
* modify human-oversight controls; or
* deploy AI systems to production.

For such users, training should be combined with privileged-access management and separation-of-duties controls.

### 62. AI Literacy and Secure Development

Developers should understand that AI systems require security considerations throughout the development lifecycle.

Developer literacy should cover:

* secure architecture;
* threat modeling;
* secure prompt handling;
* input validation;
* output validation;
* authentication;
* authorization;
* secrets management;
* logging;
* monitoring;
* dependency management;
* model provenance;
* evaluation;
* adversarial testing;
* secure deployment; and
* incident response.

AI security training should therefore be integrated with the organization's secure software development lifecycle.

### 63. AI Literacy and Procurement

Procurement personnel should understand how AI differs from conventional software procurement.

They should be able to identify questions concerning:

* AI functionality;
* data processing;
* model providers;
* training data;
* third-party dependencies;
* model hosting;
* security;
* privacy;
* regulatory classification;
* transparency;
* human oversight;
* subcontractors;
* model changes; and
* termination or data-return requirements.

Procurement literacy helps prevent AI systems from entering the organization without appropriate governance review.

### 64. AI Literacy and Shadow AI

AI literacy should specifically address **shadow AI**.

Employees may independently adopt AI services because they are convenient or more capable than approved enterprise tools.

Training should explain:

* what constitutes unauthorized AI use;
* why unapproved tools create risk;
* how to request approval;
* what information must not be submitted;
* how to report an existing unauthorized use; and
* how approved alternatives should be used.

The objective should be risk reduction rather than merely punitive enforcement.

### 65. AI Literacy and AI Tool Discovery

Organizations should combine literacy controls with technical discovery where possible.

Security and IT teams may identify AI usage through:

* application inventories;
* SaaS discovery;
* network telemetry;
* API monitoring;
* browser controls;
* endpoint controls;
* cloud logs;
* identity systems; and
* procurement records.

Discovery results can be compared against training and authorization records.

This creates a stronger control than relying exclusively on employee declarations.

### 66. AI Literacy and Human Factors

Training should account for how humans actually interact with AI.

Important factors include:

* cognitive overload;
* automation bias;
* time pressure;
* interface design;
* alert fatigue;
* confidence effects;
* authority effects;
* organizational incentives;
* workload;
* user experience; and
* cultural expectations concerning AI.

A technically accurate training course can still be ineffective if the operational environment encourages users to accept AI recommendations without adequate review.

### 67. AI Literacy and Accessibility

AI literacy programs should be accessible to the people who need them.

Considerations may include:

* disability access;
* language;
* literacy level;
* training format;
* remote access;
* assistive technologies;
* captions;
* transcripts;
* alternative assessment formats; and
* culturally appropriate examples.

Accessibility should not result in removal of essential competency requirements.

### 68. AI Literacy and Multilingual Workforces

Multinational organizations should provide AI literacy in languages appropriate to the workforce where necessary.

Translated material should preserve the meaning of:

* mandatory requirements;
* prohibited uses;
* security warnings;
* privacy instructions;
* escalation procedures;
* human-oversight requirements; and
* regulatory obligations.

Critical training should be validated to avoid translation errors that could alter the operational meaning of a control.

### 69. AI Literacy and Training Quality Assurance

Training content should itself be subject to quality assurance.

Reviews should assess:

* factual accuracy;
* regulatory accuracy;
* technical accuracy;
* applicability;
* clarity;
* outdated information;
* inappropriate examples;
* accessibility;
* assessment validity; and
* alignment with current organizational policies.

AI-generated training material should undergo appropriate human review before becoming authoritative training content.

### 70. AI Literacy and AI-Generated Training Content

Organizations may use AI to create or personalize training material.

Where this occurs, governance should address:

* factual verification;
* source validation;
* privacy;
* copyright;
* hallucination;
* bias;
* confidentiality;
* approval;
* version control; and
* human review.

AI should not automatically become the authoritative source for explaining AI governance requirements.

### 71. AI-LIT-26 — Training Content Change Control

Training material should be subject to formal change management.

Changes should identify:

* affected modules;
* regulatory changes;
* policy changes;
* technical changes;
* security changes;
* impacted roles;
* required retraining;
* effective date;
* content owner;
* approver; and
* evidence of publication.

Material regulatory changes should trigger a documented impact assessment.

### 72. AI-LIT-27 — Competency Expiration and Renewal

Where the organization uses formal competency validation, renewal requirements should be established.

Renewal may be triggered by:

* elapsed time;
* material system change;
* regulatory change;
* role change;
* significant incident;
* failed assessment;
* extended absence; or
* introduction of new AI capabilities.

The renewal period should reflect risk and the pace of change.

### 73. AI-LIT-28 — Failed Competency Remediation

When an employee fails an AI competency assessment, the organization should establish an appropriate remediation process.

Possible measures include:

* targeted retraining;
* supervised operation;
* temporary access restrictions;
* reassessment;
* mentoring;
* additional approval requirements; or
* temporary reassignment of AI responsibilities.

The response should be proportionate to the role and risk.

### 74. AI-LIT-29 — AI Literacy Escalation

Employees should have a defined escalation mechanism when they encounter uncertainty.

Escalation destinations may include:

* AI Governance;
* Information Security;
* Privacy;
* Legal;
* Compliance;
* Risk Management;
* business management; or
* designated AI system owners.

The escalation mechanism should be simple enough to use during normal operational activity.

### 75. AI-LIT-30 — AI Literacy Deployment Gate

For material AI deployments, AI literacy readiness should form part of the deployment gate.

The gate should verify:

* required user populations identified;
* competency requirements established;
* training completed where required;
* human oversight personnel trained;
* privileged users trained;
* support personnel trained;
* incident-response personnel trained;
* operating procedures available; and
* training evidence recorded.

Where competency is essential to safe operation, deployment should not proceed until the requirement is satisfied or an approved compensating control exists.

### 76. AI Literacy and Regulatory Classification

The organization's literacy assessment should reflect the legal classification of the AI system.

Different requirements may apply depending on whether the system is:

* prohibited;
* high-risk;
* subject to specific transparency obligations;
* a GPAI model or system;
* systemic-risk GPAI;
* minimal-risk; or
* subject to other sector-specific regulation.

Internal risk classifications may be more granular, but they should not replace the legal assessment.

### 77. AI Literacy and Prohibited AI Practices

Personnel involved in AI approval, procurement, development, or deployment should understand prohibited AI practices relevant to the organization's activities.

Training should help personnel recognize situations involving:

* prohibited manipulation;
* exploitation of vulnerabilities;
* prohibited social scoring;
* specified criminal-risk assessment;
* certain biometric categorization;
* certain emotion-recognition uses;
* prohibited biometric identification;
* and other prohibited practices applicable under the AI Act.

The purpose is preventive recognition and escalation, not legal self-certification by ordinary employees.

### 78. AI Literacy and Transparency Obligations

Personnel operating AI systems subject to transparency requirements should understand when disclosures are required.

Training may cover:

* AI interaction disclosure;
* synthetic-content marking;
* deepfake disclosure;
* emotion-recognition disclosure;
* biometric categorization disclosure;
* public-interest AI-generated text;
* machine-readable marking; and
* applicable exceptions.

This should be connected to technical controls so that compliance does not depend exclusively on user memory.

### 79. AI Literacy and GPAI

Personnel working with GPAI systems should understand the organization's governance boundaries concerning:

* approved models;
* model providers;
* downstream use;
* model versions;
* system integrations;
* sensitive data;
* model outputs;
* security controls;
* acceptable use;
* and escalation.

Personnel involved in provider-side GPAI governance may require deeper knowledge of technical documentation, downstream information, copyright policy, training-content summaries, systemic-risk requirements, and associated compliance evidence.

### 80. AI Literacy Governance Record

The organization should maintain a formal AI Literacy Governance Record for each material AI program.

The record should connect:

* applicable legal requirements;

* AI inventory;
* AI risk classification;
* affected roles;
* competency requirements;
* training;
* assessment;

This record provides the foundation for demonstrating that AI literacy has been implemented as an operational governance capability rather than treated merely as a general awareness exercise.

# Part 3 — Governance, Accountability, Risk Management, and Assurance

### 81. AI Literacy Governance Framework

AI literacy should be governed as an enterprise capability within the organization's broader AI Governance Framework.

The governance framework should establish:

* accountability;
* authority;
* competency requirements;
* risk-based training;
* policy ownership;
* oversight;
* assurance;
* reporting;
* escalation;
* exception management;
* evidence management; and
* continuous improvement.

The framework should define how AI literacy connects with AI risk management, cybersecurity, privacy, legal compliance, human resources, operational governance, and internal audit.

### 82. Board Oversight of AI Literacy

The board should understand whether the organization possesses sufficient AI literacy to manage its material AI exposure.

Board oversight should focus on questions such as:

* Does management understand the organization's AI risk profile?
* Are personnel using AI appropriately?
* Are high-risk AI responsibilities assigned to competent personnel?
* Are material AI incidents linked to competency weaknesses?
* Is management investing appropriately in AI capability?
* Are regulatory requirements being addressed?
* Are AI literacy risks included in enterprise risk reporting?

The board does not need to administer training programs, but it should receive sufficient information to challenge management where AI literacy weaknesses create material risk.

### 83. Executive Accountability

Executive management should be accountable for establishing an AI literacy capability appropriate to the organization's AI exposure.

Executive responsibilities may include:

* approving the AI literacy strategy;
* establishing sufficient resources;
* defining risk appetite;
* ensuring accountability;
* reviewing material competency gaps;
* addressing significant incidents;
* supporting cross-functional governance;
* ensuring regulatory obligations are considered; and
* requiring remediation where AI literacy controls fail.

AI literacy should not become an isolated Human Resources initiative without executive sponsorship.

### 84. AI Governance Committee

An AI Governance Committee should oversee enterprise AI competency where the organization's AI footprint justifies such a body.

Membership may include:

* AI Governance;
* Legal;
* Compliance;
* Information Security;
* Privacy;
* Data Governance;
* Technology;
* Engineering;
* Risk Management;
* Human Resources;
* business leadership; and
* Internal Audit as an independent observer where appropriate.

The committee should approve competency principles and monitor significant gaps rather than managing individual employee training records.

### 85. Three Lines of Accountability

AI literacy governance should align with the Three Lines Model.

**First Line**

Business and technical teams are responsible for maintaining the competence necessary to use and operate AI systems safely.

**Second Line**

Risk, Compliance, Privacy, Security, and AI Governance establish requirements, advise the first line, monitor compliance, and challenge deficiencies.

**Third Line**

Internal Audit independently evaluates whether the governance framework and associated controls are appropriately designed and operating effectively.

This separation prevents the function responsible for delivering training from becoming the sole judge of its effectiveness.

### 86. AI Literacy Ownership

The organization should establish clear ownership.

A typical model may assign:

* **Board:** oversight;
* **Executive Management:** accountability;
* **AI Governance:** framework owner;
* **HR/Learning & Development:** training administration;
* **Business Owners:** role-specific competency;
* **System Owners:** system-specific requirements;
* **Security:** AI security competency;
* **Privacy:** privacy competency;
* **Legal/Compliance:** regulatory competency;
* **Managers:** employee compliance;
* **Employees:** individual responsibility; and
* **Internal Audit:** independent assurance.

The exact allocation should reflect organizational structure.

### 87. Competency Accountability

Each material AI use case should have an accountable owner for determining the competency requirements of its users.

The system owner should identify:

* users;
* supervisors;
* administrators;
* privileged personnel;
* technical personnel;
* support personnel;
* affected business functions; and
* required competency levels.

This prevents generic enterprise training from being mistaken for system-specific readiness.

### 88. AI Literacy Risk Appetite

The organization should define its tolerance for AI literacy deficiencies.

For example, the organization may establish a low tolerance for:

* untrained personnel operating high-risk AI;
* untrained privileged administrators;
* unapproved AI usage;
* untrained human overseers;
* expired competency for critical AI functions;
* repeated AI-related user errors; and
* material training gaps associated with regulatory obligations.

Risk appetite should distinguish between minor awareness deficiencies and competency gaps that could cause material harm.

### 89. AI Literacy Risk Register

AI literacy risks should be recorded in an appropriate risk register.

Examples include:

| Risk                         | Potential impact             | Example treatment        |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Insufficient user training   | Incorrect AI use             | Role-based training      |
| Untrained high-risk operator | Regulatory/human-rights risk | Competency gate          |
| AI security knowledge gap    | Security incident            | Specialist training      |
| Poor output verification     | Incorrect decisions          | Verification training    |
| Shadow AI                    | Data/security exposure       | Awareness + discovery    |
| Outdated training            | Control failure              | Change-triggered refresh |
| Vendor competency gap        | Third-party risk             | Contractual requirements |

The risk register should identify owners, treatments, target dates, and residual risk.

### 90. AI Literacy and Enterprise Risk Management

AI literacy should be integrated into enterprise risk management rather than maintained as a separate training metric.

Material competency gaps may contribute to:

* operational risk;
* cybersecurity risk;
* privacy risk;
* regulatory risk;
* legal risk;
* model risk;
* third-party risk;
* reputational risk;
* financial risk; and
* human-rights risk.

Risk reporting should therefore consider whether personnel capability is an underlying risk factor.

### 91. AI Literacy and Fundamental Rights

Where AI systems can affect individuals, governance should ensure that relevant personnel understand fundamental-rights implications.

This includes risks concerning:

* equality;
* non-discrimination;
* privacy;
* dignity;
* autonomy;
* freedom;
* access to services;
* employment;
* education; and
* due process.

Training should be proportionate to the person's ability to influence outcomes.

### 92. AI Literacy and Human Oversight Governance

The organization should formally identify personnel responsible for human oversight.

Governance should define:

* who is authorized to intervene;
* what intervention means;
* when intervention is required;
* what information the human receives;
* what authority the human has;
* how overrides are recorded;
* when escalation is required; and
* what competency the overseer must possess.

A governance framework should not designate a nominal “human in the loop” without establishing meaningful authority and competence.

### 93. Automation Bias Governance

AI literacy governance should explicitly address automation bias.

Management should recognize that users may:

* over-trust AI recommendations;
* defer to AI because it appears objective;
* ignore contradictory evidence;
* accept highly confident outputs;
* reduce independent analysis; or
* perceive AI-generated recommendations as mandatory.

Training, interface design, procedures, and performance management should collectively reduce these risks.

### 94. AI Literacy and Decision Accountability

The organization should clearly establish who remains accountable for decisions involving AI.

AI literacy should reinforce that:

* AI does not automatically become the accountable decision-maker;
* human decision-makers must understand relevant limitations;
* AI recommendations should be challenged where appropriate;
* required human review cannot be replaced by training completion; and
* responsibility must be assigned to an identifiable role.

This principle is particularly important where AI influences high-impact decisions.

### 95. AI Literacy and Privacy Governance

Privacy governance should establish specific competency requirements for personnel using AI with personal data.

The privacy function should help define:

* relevant training topics;
* prohibited data handling;
* approved processing methods;
* privacy incident escalation;
* data minimization requirements;
* vendor considerations; and
* role-specific privacy competency.

Privacy training should be integrated with, but not replaced by, general AI literacy.

### 96. AI Literacy and Cybersecurity Governance

Security governance should define the AI security competencies required for relevant roles.

The security function should consider:

* AI threat landscape;
* AI-specific attack techniques;
* secure AI development;
* prompt security;
* agent security;
* model access;
* data protection;
* logging;
* monitoring;
* incident response; and
* third-party AI security.

Security competency should increase with technical privilege and potential impact.

### 97. AI Literacy and Data Governance

Data Governance should establish competency requirements for personnel who:

* prepare AI training data;
* select datasets;
* manage data pipelines;
* operate retrieval systems;
* configure data connectors;
* approve data sources; or
* evaluate data quality.

Personnel should understand:

* data quality;
* provenance;
* classification;
* access controls;
* retention;
* data minimization;
* inappropriate data use; and
* AI-specific data risks.

### 98. AI Literacy and Legal Governance

Legal should define where specialized regulatory knowledge is required.

Legal competency may be necessary for personnel responsible for:

* AI regulatory classification;
* prohibited-practice assessment;
* high-risk assessment;
* contractual requirements;
* intellectual property;
* privacy;
* transparency;
* regulatory reporting;
* regulatory investigations; and
* AI-related claims.

Ordinary users should not be expected to independently resolve complex legal questions.

### 99. AI Literacy and Compliance Governance

Compliance should establish mechanisms for determining whether AI literacy obligations are being met.

Compliance activities may include:

* policy monitoring;
* control testing;
* training compliance;
* exception monitoring;
* regulatory change analysis;
* issue tracking;
* management reporting; and
* evidence validation.

Compliance should distinguish between training completion and demonstrated competency.

### 100. AI Literacy and Human Resources

Human Resources or Learning & Development may operate the enterprise training infrastructure.

Responsibilities may include:

* course administration;
* assignment;
* enrollment;
* completion tracking;
* assessment administration;
* reminders;
* reporting;
* employee records; and
* training platform management.

However, HR should not independently determine technical or regulatory competency requirements without appropriate input from AI Governance and subject-matter functions.

### 101. AI Literacy and Performance Management

Organizations may incorporate AI literacy into performance expectations for relevant roles.

For example, personnel responsible for material AI systems may be expected to:

* maintain required competency;
* complete mandatory training;
* follow approved procedures;
* participate in simulations;
* report incidents;
* maintain certifications where applicable; and
* demonstrate appropriate AI judgment.

Performance management should not create incentives that encourage employees to use AI inappropriately merely to increase productivity.

### 102. AI Literacy and Organizational Culture

A mature AI governance culture encourages employees to question unsafe AI use.

Management should reinforce that:

* reporting an AI concern is acceptable;
* stopping unsafe AI activity is acceptable;
* escalating uncertainty is encouraged;
* employees will not be penalized for legitimate good-faith reporting;
* AI output should be challenged when appropriate; and
* productivity targets do not override AI governance requirements.

Culture is therefore an important control environment component.

### 103. Speak-Up and Escalation

Personnel should have accessible channels for reporting AI concerns.

Reports may involve:

* unauthorized AI use;
* unsafe AI output;
* discriminatory behavior;
* privacy issues;
* security vulnerabilities;
* suspicious AI behavior;
* prohibited practices;
* inadequate human oversight;
* misleading AI-generated content; or
* unclear accountability.

Escalation channels should be integrated into existing ethics, security, privacy, compliance, and incident-reporting mechanisms where practical.

### 104. AI Literacy and Whistleblowing Protection

Where personnel report potentially serious AI governance failures, existing whistleblowing and protected-reporting mechanisms should be considered.

Employees should not be discouraged from reporting concerns because:

* the AI system is commercially important;
* management approved it;
* the system was supplied by a major vendor; or
* the concern could delay a deployment.

An effective governance culture must permit challenge to AI decisions.

### 105. AI Literacy Exception Governance

Exceptions to competency requirements should be governed under the organization's broader exception-management framework.

Exception approval should consider:

* risk;
* duration;
* compensating controls;
* alternative personnel;
* supervision;
* business necessity;
* regulatory restrictions; and
* remediation.

Exceptions should never be used to circumvent legal requirements.

### 106. AI Literacy and Risk Acceptance

Where residual AI literacy risk remains, risk acceptance should be assigned to an appropriately authorized individual.

Risk acceptance should document:

* identified competency gap;
* affected AI system;
* potential consequences;
* existing controls;
* residual risk;
* duration;
* accountable owner;
* approval authority; and
* remediation plan.

The employee performing the task should not automatically have authority to accept the resulting organizational risk.

### 107. AI Literacy and Separation of Duties

Separation of duties should be considered for material AI systems.

Where appropriate, the same individual should not independently:

* define competency requirements;
* approve their own exception;
* authorize their own privileged access;
* operate the system;
* disable monitoring; and
* certify compliance.

Segregation should be proportionate to risk.

### 108. AI Literacy and Vendor Governance

Vendor governance should include AI competency where third parties perform material AI activities.

Due diligence should consider:

* provider competency;
* security expertise;
* privacy expertise;
* AI governance;
* regulatory knowledge;
* incident response;
* personnel qualifications;
* training programs; and
* subcontractor capabilities.

Contractual controls should establish appropriate notification and evidence rights.

### 109. Third-Party Training Assurance

Where vendors claim that their personnel are appropriately trained, the organization should determine what evidence is reasonably necessary.

Evidence may include:

* attestations;
* certifications;
* training summaries;
* independent assurance;
* contractual representations;
* audit reports; or
* targeted assessments.

The required level of evidence should correspond to the risk of the outsourced AI activity.

### 110. AI Literacy and Supply-Chain Risk

AI supply chains can involve:

* foundation-model providers;
* cloud providers;
* model hosting services;
* data providers;
* software vendors;
* AI agents;
* plugins;
* APIs;
* system integrators; and
* managed service providers.

The organization should determine where competency failures within the supply chain could affect its own AI risk.

### 111. AI Literacy and Regulatory Change

Regulatory change should trigger review of AI literacy requirements.

The regulatory-change process should identify:

* new legal requirements;
* amended obligations;
* new guidance;
* enforcement expectations;
* implementation deadlines;
* affected roles;
* affected training;
* policy changes; and
* required remediation.

The organization should maintain evidence that material regulatory changes were assessed.

### 112. AI Literacy and Policy Governance

Policies should establish the organization's expectations for AI literacy.

Relevant policies may include:

* AI Governance Policy;
* AI Acceptable-Use Policy;
* AI Security Policy;
* Data Protection Policy;
* Information Security Policy;

### 129. AI Literacy Program Maturity

A maturity model can help organizations assess program development.

**Level 1 — Ad Hoc**

Training is informal and AI use is poorly understood.

**Level 2 — Aware**

General AI awareness exists but role-specific requirements are limited.

**Level 3 — Defined**

Competency frameworks, policies, and role-based training exist.

**Level 4 — Managed**

Training is linked to AI risk, access, monitoring, and assurance.

**Level 5 — Optimized**

Competency is continuously measured, automated where appropriate, integrated with AI lifecycle governance, and adjusted dynamically as AI risk changes.

# Part 4 — Operationalization, Monitoring, Evidence, and Continuous Compliance

### 136. Operational AI Literacy Management

AI literacy should be operationalized through the organization's AI lifecycle rather than managed as an annual training exercise.

The operational model should connect:

**AI inventory → risk classification → role mapping → competency requirements → training → authorization → operation → monitoring → reassessment.**

Every material AI system should have an identifiable population of personnel whose competence is necessary for safe and compliant operation.

### 137. AI Literacy Lifecycle Gate

AI literacy should be incorporated into AI lifecycle gates.

A practical lifecycle may include:

1. AI use-case intake;
2. regulatory assessment;
3. risk assessment;
4. role identification;
5. competency assessment;
6. training assignment;
7. authorization;
8. deployment;
9. operational monitoring;
10. material-change assessment;
11. retraining; and
12. retirement.

Where competency is a prerequisite for safe operation, the relevant gate should prevent deployment or access until the requirement is satisfied.

Human oversight should be treated as an operational control, not simply an organizational designation.

Verification requirements should increase with decision impact.

* policy checks;
* security checks;

* legal review; or

### 159. AI-LIT-52 — AI Literacy Incident Trigger

An AI incident should automatically trigger a review of the relevant competency requirements.

The review should ask:

* Did the user understand the procedure?
* Was training current?
* Was the training sufficient?

### 186. AI-LIT-79 — AI Literacy Knowledge Management

### 194. AI-LIT-87 — AI Literacy Training Approval

### 195. AI-LIT-88 — AI Literacy Vendor Training Validation

* provides usable assessments; and
* produces sufficient evidence.

Third-party training should not automatically be accepted without review.
