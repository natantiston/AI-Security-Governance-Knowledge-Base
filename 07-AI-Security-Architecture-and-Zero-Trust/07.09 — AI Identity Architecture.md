# 07.09 — AI Identity Architecture

## Part 1 — Identity Architecture Foundations and AI Identity Model

### 1. AI Identity Architecture

AI identity architecture defines how identities are established, represented, authenticated, authorized, governed, and monitored across an enterprise AI environment.

AI systems introduce more than traditional human identities. A complete AI environment may contain:

* Human users.
* AI applications.
* AI agents.
* Service accounts.
* Workloads.
* APIs.
* Models.
* Data services.
* Tools.
* External services.

A secure architecture must establish **who or what is requesting access, on whose behalf, to which resource, and for what purpose**.

---

### 2. Why Identity Is Critical to AI Security

AI systems increasingly interact with sensitive information and perform actions on behalf of users.

A simplified architecture is:

```text id="ai-identity-core"
Human User
     ↓
AI Application
     ↓
AI Agent
     ↓
AI Model
     ↓
Enterprise Data
     ↓
Enterprise Tools
```

If identity controls are weak, a compromise at one point can potentially provide access to multiple downstream resources.

---

### 3. Traditional Identity vs. AI Identity

Traditional enterprise identity architecture primarily focuses on:

```text id="traditional-identity"
Human
 ↓
Application
 ↓
Resource
```

AI environments introduce additional relationships:

```text id="ai-identity"
Human
 ↓
AI Application
 ↓
AI Agent
 ↓
Tool
 ↓
Enterprise Resource
```

The identity architecture must therefore preserve authorization context across multiple AI components.

---

### 4. AI Identity Architecture Objectives

The primary objectives are:

1. Establish trustworthy identities.
2. Authenticate identities appropriately.
3. Authorize access according to least privilege.
4. Maintain identity context across AI workflows.
5. Distinguish human and non-human identities.
6. Prevent unauthorized delegation.
7. Maintain accountability.
8. Support continuous monitoring.
9. Control identity lifecycle.
10. Enable rapid revocation.

---

### 5. Identity Domains in an AI Environment

An enterprise AI architecture may contain several identity domains:

```text id="identity-domains"
                  AI Environment
                       │
       ┌───────────────┼────────────────┐
       ↓               ↓                ↓
 Human Identity   Workload Identity   Machine Identity
       │               │                │
       ↓               ↓                ↓
   Users          Applications       Services
```

These identities should be managed according to their different characteristics.

---

### 6. Human Identity

Human identities represent people interacting with AI systems.

Examples include:

* Employees.
* Contractors.
* Administrators.
* Developers.
* Security personnel.
* Auditors.
* Customers.

Human identity should normally be connected to the organization's enterprise identity provider.

---

### 7. Human Identity Flow

A simplified flow is:

```text id="human-flow"
User
 ↓
Identity Provider
 ↓
Authentication
 ↓
Access Policy
 ↓
AI Application
 ↓
Authorized AI Capability
```

The identity provider establishes the user's identity, while the application and authorization layers determine what the user can do.

---

### 8. Workforce Identity

Workforce identities are identities belonging to employees and other authorized personnel.

They should normally have:

* Unique accounts.
* Appropriate authentication.
* Role assignments.
* Lifecycle management.
* Access reviews.
* Deprovisioning.

Shared human accounts should be avoided.

---

### 9. Customer Identity

Customer-facing AI services may use customer identities managed through:

* Enterprise identity systems.
* Customer identity and access management platforms.
* Federated identity.
* OAuth/OIDC.

Customer identity requirements may differ from workforce identity requirements.

---

### 10. Privileged Identity

Privileged users may have access to:

* AI platform administration.
* Model configuration.
* Security policies.
* Identity configuration.
* Data stores.
* Production tools.

Privileged identities should receive stronger controls than ordinary users.

---

### 11. Privileged Access Flow

```text id="privileged-flow"
Administrator
     ↓
Strong Authentication
     ↓
Privileged Access Management
     ↓
Approved Administrative Session
     ↓
AI Management Plane
```

Administrative access should be tightly controlled and auditable.

---

### 12. Non-Human Identity

AI systems depend heavily on non-human identities.

These may include:

* Applications.
* Services.
* Agents.
* Containers.
* Virtual machines.
* Functions.
* APIs.
* Automated workflows.

Each significant workload should have an identifiable security principal.

---

### 13. Workload Identity

Workload identity represents an application or workload rather than a person.

Example:

```text id="workload-identity"
AI Application
      ↓
Workload Identity
      ↓
Authentication
      ↓
Vector Database
```

This avoids relying on embedded static credentials.

---

### 14. Application Identity

An AI application should have its own identity where appropriate.

For example:

```text id="application-identity"
HR AI Assistant
       ↓
Application Identity
       ↓
Approved APIs
       ↓
Approved Data
```

This allows security teams to distinguish one application from another.

---

### 15. Agent Identity

An AI agent should have a distinct identity or security context when it can perform actions.

Example:

```text id="agent-identity"
User
 ↓
AI Agent
 ↓
Agent Identity
 ↓
Tool
 ↓
Enterprise System
```

This improves accountability and supports granular authorization.

---

### 16. Why Agent Identity Matters

Without a distinct agent identity, activity may appear as if it were performed directly by the user or by a generic application.

This creates problems for:

* Auditing.
* Incident investigation.
* Authorization.
* Accountability.
* Risk analysis.

---

### 17. Machine Identity

Machine identities represent technical entities that communicate with other systems.

Examples:

* API clients.
* Service accounts.
* Containers.
* Cloud workloads.
* Kubernetes workloads.
* Serverless functions.

Machine identities should be uniquely identifiable and lifecycle-managed.

---

### 18. Service Accounts

Service accounts may be used by AI applications to access:

* Databases.
* APIs.
* Storage.
* Model endpoints.
* Messaging systems.

They should not have broad administrative permissions simply because they are used by an AI system.

---

### 19. Service Account Least Privilege

Example:

```text id="service-least"
AI Retrieval Service
       ↓
Service Account
       ├── Read Approved Knowledge Base ✓
       ├── Write Knowledge Base ✗
       ├── Manage IAM ✗
       └── Delete Data ✗
```

Permissions should match the application's actual requirements.

---

### 20. Identity Federation

Identity federation allows an AI service or workload to use trusted identity relationships across systems.

Example:

```text id="federation"
Enterprise Identity
       ↓
Federation
       ↓
Cloud AI Platform
       ↓
AI Workload
```

This can reduce the need for long-lived credentials.

---

### 21. Identity Provider

The identity provider is a foundational component of the identity architecture.

It may provide:

* Authentication.
* User lifecycle.
* Group membership.
* Federation.
* Token issuance.
* Conditional access.
* MFA integration.

The AI architecture should integrate with approved enterprise identity services.

---

### 22. Identity as a Security Boundary

Identity should be treated as a security boundary rather than merely a login mechanism.

```text id="identity-boundary"
Identity
   ↓
Authentication
   ↓
Context
   ↓
Authorization
   ↓
Resource Access
```

A valid identity does not automatically establish trust for every action.

---

### 23. Identity Context

AI authorization decisions may need to consider:

* User identity.
* Agent identity.
* Application identity.
* Tenant.
* Device.
* Location.
* Session.
* Resource.
* Requested action.

Example:

```text id="identity-context"
User
 +
Application
 +
Agent
 +
Device
 +
Resource
 +
Action
 =
Access Decision
```

---

### 24. Identity Propagation

Identity information should propagate through the AI workflow when appropriate.

```text id="identity-propagation"
User
 ↓
AI Application
 ↓
AI Agent
 ↓
Tool
 ↓
Enterprise API
```

The downstream system should be able to determine the relevant identity context rather than treating every action as an anonymous service request.

---

### 25. Identity Propagation Risk

If identity context is lost:

```text id="identity-loss"
User
 ↓
AI Application
 ↓
Generic Service Account
 ↓
Database
```

The database may see only the generic service account.

This can weaken:

* Authorization.
* Accountability.
* Auditing.
* Incident investigation.

---

### 26. Delegated Identity

AI agents frequently operate on behalf of humans.

The architecture should distinguish:

```text id="delegated-identity"
Human Identity
      ↓
Delegates Authority
      ↓
AI Agent
      ↓
Authorized Action
```

Delegation should be explicit and constrained.

---

### 27. Impersonation vs. Delegation

These concepts should be distinguished.

**Impersonation:**

An application acts as though it were the user.

**Delegation:**

An application acts on behalf of the user within defined permissions.

Delegation is generally preferable when the architecture needs clear separation between the human and the AI system.

---

### 28. Delegation Scope

Delegated authority should specify:

* Which resources.
* Which actions.
* Which duration.
* Which tools.
* Which environment.

Example:

```text id="delegation-scope"
User
 ↓
AI Agent
 ↓
Permission:
Read HR Policy Documents
 ↓
Allowed

Permission:
Modify Employee Records
 ↓
Denied
```

---

### 29. Delegation Duration

Delegated permissions should not necessarily remain active indefinitely.

Short-lived delegation reduces exposure.

```text id="delegation-duration"
Grant
 ↓
Active
 ↓
Expiration
 ↓
Revoked
```

---

### 30. Identity Lifecycle

AI identities require lifecycle management from creation to retirement.

```text id="identity-lifecycle"
Request
 ↓
Approve
 ↓
Provision
 ↓
Use
 ↓
Review
 ↓
Rotate / Update
 ↓
Disable
 ↓
Retire
```

This applies to both human and non-human identities.

---

### 31. Identity Provisioning

Provisioning should be based on an approved business or technical requirement.

The process should establish:

* Identity owner.
* Purpose.
* Permissions.
* Environment.
* Expiration.
* Authentication method.

---

### 32. Identity Ownership

Every important AI identity should have an accountable owner.

For example:

| Identity                 | Owner              |
| ------------------------ | ------------------ |
| AI Assistant             | AI Platform Team   |
| Retrieval Service        | Data Platform Team |
| AI Agent                 | Application Owner  |
| Model Management Account | AI Engineering     |
| Security Admin           | Security Team      |

Ownership enables effective reviews and incident response.

---

### 33. Identity Access Review

AI identities should be periodically reviewed.

Review questions include:

* Is the identity still required?
* Who owns it?
* What can it access?
* Are permissions excessive?
* Is the workload still active?
* Are credentials current?
* Has the business purpose changed?

---

### 34. Orphaned AI Identities

An orphaned identity is an identity that remains active without a valid owner or business purpose.

Example:

```text id="orphaned"
AI Project Retired
       ↓
Application Deleted
       ↓
Service Account Remains
       ↓
Unused Privileged Credential
```

Orphaned identities create unnecessary attack paths.

---

### 35. Dormant Identity Detection

Identity monitoring should identify accounts that have not been used for an expected period.

Potential responses include:

* Review.
* Disable.
* Revoke permissions.
* Remove.
* Escalate to owner.

---

### 36. Identity Deprovisioning

When an AI workload is retired:

```text id="deprovisioning"
Workload Retired
 ↓
Disable Identity
 ↓
Revoke Credentials
 ↓
Remove Permissions
 ↓
Remove API Access
 ↓
Archive Evidence
```

Simply deleting the application may not remove all associated identities.

---

### 37. Credential Lifecycle

Credentials associated with AI identities should be governed through:

* Creation.
* Storage.
* Rotation.
* Monitoring.
* Revocation.
* Destruction.

Credentials should never be considered permanent assets.

---

### 38. Secrets vs. Identity

Identity and secrets are related but different.

**Identity answers:**

> Who or what is requesting access?

**Secret or credential answers:**

> What proves that identity?

Examples of credentials include:

* Passwords.
* API keys.
* Certificates.
* Private keys.
* Tokens.

---

### 39. Strong Workload Authentication

Workloads should use strong authentication mechanisms appropriate to their environment.

Possible mechanisms include:

* Workload identity.
* Certificates.
* OAuth tokens.
* Federated credentials.
* Cloud-native identity.

Static credentials should be minimized.

---

### 40. Short-Lived Credentials

Short-lived credentials reduce the potential impact of compromise.

```text id="short-lived"
Credential Issued
      ↓
Limited Lifetime
      ↓
API Access
      ↓
Expiration
```

This is particularly useful for automated AI workloads.

---

### 41. Identity Token

A token can represent authenticated identity and associated claims.

Example conceptual structure:

```text id="token"
Identity
+
Audience
+
Scope
+
Expiration
+
Issuer
```

Applications should validate relevant token properties before granting access.

---

### 42. Token Audience

A token should be intended for the service receiving it.

For example:

```text id="audience"
Token Intended For:
AI API A

Request:
AI API B

Result:
Reject
```

Audience validation helps prevent token misuse across services.

---

### 43. Token Scope

Tokens should contain appropriately limited permissions.

Example:

```text id="scope"
Scope:
ai.inference.read

Allowed:
Invoke Approved AI Service

Not Allowed:
model.admin
iam.write
data.delete
```

---

### 44. Token Expiration

Tokens should have appropriate expiration periods.

Long-lived tokens increase the time available for abuse if compromised.

---

### 45. Token Validation

The receiving service should validate relevant claims, such as:

* Issuer.
* Audience.
* Expiration.
* Signature.
* Scope.
* Subject.

A token should not be trusted merely because it is present.

---

### 46. Identity and Zero Trust

AI identity architecture strongly supports Zero Trust principles.

The basic model is:

```text id="identity-zero-trust"
Never Assume Trust
       ↓
Verify Identity
       ↓
Evaluate Context
       ↓
Authorize
       ↓
Monitor
```

Being inside the enterprise network should not automatically grant access.

---

### 47. Explicit Verification

Each access request should be evaluated based on available security context.

```text id="explicit-verification"
Who?
 +
What?
 +
Which Resource?
 +
Which Action?
 +
What Context?
```

The result determines whether access is permitted.

---

### 48. Least Privilege

AI identities should receive only the permissions necessary for their defined function.

This applies to:

* Users.
* Agents.
* Applications.
* Service accounts.
* Workloads.

---

### 49. Assume Breach

AI identity architecture should assume that an identity could eventually be compromised.

Therefore, the architecture should limit:

* Permissions.
* Network reach.
* Data access.
* Tool capabilities.
* Credential lifetime.

---

### 50. Identity Segmentation

Different AI components should use separate identities when their functions and trust levels differ.

```text id="identity-segmentation"
AI Chatbot Identity
        ↓
Read-only Knowledge

AI Admin Identity
        ↓
Management APIs

AI Deployment Identity
        ↓
Deployment Resources
```

Using one identity for all functions increases blast radius.

---

### 51. Identity-Based Network Access

Network controls can incorporate workload identity.

For example:

```text id="identity-network"
AI Workload Identity
       ↓
Network Policy
       ↓
Approved Database
```

This allows security decisions to consider both identity and network context.

---

### 52. Identity-Aware API Gateway

An API gateway can use identity information to enforce policy.

```text id="identity-gateway"
Request
 ↓
API Gateway
 ↓
Identity Validation
 ↓
Policy Evaluation
 ↓
AI API
```

This centralizes important access decisions.

---

### 53. Identity-Aware RAG

RAG systems should preserve user authorization context.

```text id="identity-rag"
User Identity
     ↓
AI Application
     ↓
Authorization Context
     ↓
Vector Search
     ↓
Authorized Documents
```

The retrieval system should not expose documents solely because they are semantically relevant.

---

### 54. Identity-Aware Tool Access

Tools should evaluate identity and authorization before execution.

```text id="identity-tool"
AI Agent
 ↓
Tool Request
 ↓
Identity Context
 ↓
Authorization
 ↓
Tool
```

The model's request alone should not constitute authorization.

---

### 55. Identity and Data Access

AI identity architecture should connect identity to data access.

```text id="identity-data"
Identity
 ↓
Authorization
 ↓
Data Classification
 ↓
Approved Dataset
```

Sensitive data should require appropriate identity and authorization controls.

---

### 56. Identity and Model Access

Different users and workloads may have different permissions to invoke models.

Example:

```text id="identity-model"
Employee
 ↓
Approved General Model ✓

Developer
 ↓
Development Model ✓

Administrator
 ↓
Model Management ✓
```

Model access should be explicitly controlled.

---

### 57. Identity and Environment Separation

Development, testing, and production should use appropriately separated identities.

```text id="env-identity"
Development Identity
      ↓
Development Resources

Production Identity
      ↓
Production Resources
```

Development identities should not automatically have production privileges.

---

### 58. Identity Boundary Between Environments

Cross-environment access should be explicitly authorized.

For example:

```text id="cross-env"
Development
    ↓
Production
    ✗
```

unless there is a documented and approved requirement.

---

### 59. Identity for AI Infrastructure

AI infrastructure may contain:

* GPU clusters.
* Kubernetes clusters.
* Model servers.
* Vector databases.
* Storage.
* API gateways.
* CI/CD pipelines.

Each component may require its own identity and access model.

---

### 60. Identity Architecture Principle

A foundational AI identity architecture can be represented as:

```text id="identity-architecture"
                  HUMAN
                    │
                    ↓
             Identity Provider
                    │
                    ↓
              AI Application
                    │
              ┌─────┴─────┐
              ↓           ↓
          AI Agent      Workload
              │           │
              ↓           ↓
            Tools       APIs
              │           │
              └─────┬─────┘
                    ↓
             Enterprise Data
```

At every significant interaction, the architecture should preserve sufficient identity context to answer:

> **Who is acting, what is acting on their behalf, what is it allowed to do, and which resource is being accessed?**

# 07.09 — AI Identity Architecture

## Part 2 — Identity Federation, Workload Identity, Delegation, and Access Context

### 61. Identity Federation

Identity federation allows identities established by one trusted identity domain to be recognized by another system.

In an AI environment, federation can connect:

```text
Enterprise Identity Provider
          ↓
      Federation
          ↓
   AI Platform / Cloud
          ↓
      AI Workload
```

This reduces the need to create separate identities for every AI service.

---

### 62. Federation Trust

Federation creates a trust relationship between identity domains.

The architecture should define:

* Who is the identity provider.
* Who is the relying party.
* What claims are trusted.
* Which users or workloads can federate.
* What authentication assurance is required.
* How trust is revoked.

---

### 63. Federation Protocols

Common identity federation technologies include:

* SAML.
* OAuth 2.0.
* OpenID Connect.
* WS-Federation in legacy environments.

For modern application and API architectures, OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect are particularly important.

---

### 64. OpenID Connect

OpenID Connect provides an identity layer on top of OAuth 2.0.

Conceptually:

```text
User
 ↓
Identity Provider
 ↓
Authentication
 ↓
ID Token
 ↓
Application
```

The application can use the identity information to establish the authenticated user context.

---

### 65. OAuth 2.0

OAuth 2.0 primarily addresses authorization and delegated access.

A simplified model is:

```text
Resource Owner
      ↓
Authorization
      ↓
Access Token
      ↓
AI Application
      ↓
Protected API
```

OAuth should not be treated as a replacement for identity governance.

---

### 66. Access Token

An access token represents authorization to access a protected resource.

For an AI API, the token may communicate:

* Subject.
* Client/application.
* Scope.
* Audience.
* Expiration.
* Issuer.

The API should validate the token before accepting the request.

---

### 67. ID Token vs. Access Token

The distinction is important.

**ID token:**

Provides identity information to the client application.

**Access token:**

Provides authorization to access a protected resource.

A resource server should not automatically accept an ID token as an API access token.

---

### 68. OAuth Client Identity

An AI application acting as an OAuth client should have a distinct client identity.

Example:

```text
AI Assistant
     ↓
OAuth Client ID
     ↓
Authorization Server
     ↓
Access Token
     ↓
Enterprise API
```

This provides application-level accountability.

---

### 69. Client Credentials Flow

Machine-to-machine AI applications may use a client credentials flow.

Conceptually:

```text
AI Workload
     ↓
Client Authentication
     ↓
Authorization Server
     ↓
Access Token
     ↓
Enterprise API
```

Permissions should be limited to the workload's actual requirements.

---

### 70. Authorization Code Flow

For user-facing applications, an authorization-code-based flow can establish user authorization without requiring the AI application to handle the user's password.

```text
User
 ↓
AI Application
 ↓
Identity Provider
 ↓
User Authentication
 ↓
Authorization Code
 ↓
Application
 ↓
Access Token
```

---

### 71. PKCE

Proof Key for Code Exchange (PKCE) strengthens authorization-code flows by helping protect against authorization-code interception.

It is particularly relevant to public clients and modern application architectures.

---

### 72. Workload Identity

Workload identity provides a way for applications and services to authenticate without relying on manually managed long-lived secrets.

```text
AI Workload
    ↓
Workload Identity
    ↓
Identity Provider
    ↓
Short-Lived Credential
    ↓
Target Resource
```

---

### 73. Why Workload Identity Matters for AI

AI environments can contain many automated components.

For example:

```text
AI Gateway
AI Orchestrator
AI Agent
RAG Service
Vector Database Connector
Model Server
Tool Service
```

Giving each component a distinct workload identity improves isolation and accountability.

---

### 74. Static Credential Problem

A traditional architecture might look like:

```text
AI Application
      ↓
Hardcoded API Key
      ↓
Enterprise API
```

If the key is leaked, an attacker may be able to use the same credential outside the intended workload.

---

### 75. Federated Workload Authentication

A stronger architecture can use federation:

```text
AI Workload
      ↓
Workload Identity
      ↓
Federated Trust
      ↓
Short-Lived Token
      ↓
Enterprise Resource
```

This reduces dependence on static secrets.

---

### 76. Cloud Workload Identity

Cloud platforms commonly provide mechanisms for assigning identities to workloads.

The architectural objective is:

```text
Workload
 ↓
Native Identity
 ↓
Authorized Resource
```

rather than:

```text
Workload
 ↓
Stored Password/API Key
 ↓
Resource
```

---

### 77. Kubernetes Workload Identity

AI workloads running in Kubernetes may use workload-level identities.

Conceptually:

```text
Pod
 ↓
Service Account
 ↓
Workload Identity
 ↓
Cloud / Enterprise Resource
```

The service account should have only the permissions required by the workload.

---

### 78. Service Mesh Identity

A service mesh can provide identity and authentication between services.

```text
AI Service A
      ↓
Service Identity
      ↓
Service Mesh
      ↓
Service Identity
      ↓
AI Service B
```

This can support workload authentication and encrypted service-to-service communication.

---

### 79. Mutual TLS

Mutual TLS allows both sides of a connection to authenticate.

```text
AI Service A
   ⇄ mTLS ⇄
AI Service B
```

Unlike ordinary TLS, where the server authenticates to the client, mTLS provides mutual certificate-based authentication.

---

### 80. AI Service-to-Service Identity

An AI architecture may contain many internal service calls.

Example:

```text
API Gateway
    ↓
AI Orchestrator
    ↓
RAG Service
    ↓
Vector Database
    ↓
Model Service
```

Each service-to-service relationship should have an appropriate identity and authorization model.

---

### 81. Service Identity Isolation

Separate service identities reduce blast radius.

```text
RAG Identity
 ↓
Vector DB Read

Model Identity
 ↓
Model Endpoint

Notification Identity
 ↓
Messaging API
```

Compromising one service does not automatically provide all other service permissions.

---

### 82. AI Agent Identity

AI agents should have an identity that distinguishes them from the underlying application where appropriate.

Example:

```text
Human User
     ↓
AI Application
     ↓
Agent Identity
     ↓
Tool
```

This allows the organization to determine that an action was initiated through an AI agent.

---

### 83. Agent Identity vs. User Identity

These identities represent different security principals.

```text
User Identity:
Jonathan

Agent Identity:
HR-AI-Agent

Application Identity:
Enterprise-AI-Portal
```

The security system should be able to distinguish among them.

---

### 84. Composite Identity Context

A useful model is:

```text
User
 +
Application
 +
Agent
 +
Device
 +
Session
 +
Resource
 +
Action
```

The combined context can support more precise authorization.

---

### 85. On-Behalf-Of Flow

An AI application may need to call a downstream service on behalf of a user.

Conceptually:

```text
User
 ↓
AI Application
 ↓
Delegated Token
 ↓
Enterprise API
```

The downstream API can make an authorization decision based on the delegated user context.

---

### 86. Token Exchange

Token exchange can allow an application to obtain a token appropriate for a downstream resource.

```text
User Context
     ↓
AI Application
     ↓
Token Exchange
     ↓
Downstream Token
     ↓
Enterprise API
```

The resulting token should be limited to the required audience and scope.

---

### 87. Delegated Authority

Delegation should define the exact authority given to the AI system.

Example:

```text
User
 ↓
Delegates
 ↓
AI Agent

Allowed:
Read Calendar

Denied:
Delete Calendar
```

The agent should not inherit unrestricted user authority.

---

### 88. Delegation Boundaries

Delegation should consider:

* Resource.
* Action.
* Scope.
* Duration.
* Environment.
* Business purpose.

These boundaries prevent excessive authority.

---

### 89. Privilege Amplification

A critical AI identity risk occurs when an AI component has greater privileges than the user who initiated the request.

Example:

```text
User
 ↓
Limited Access
 ↓
AI Agent
 ↓
Administrator Credential
 ↓
Database
```

This creates a privilege-amplification path.

---

### 90. Preventing Privilege Amplification

The architecture should enforce:

```text
Effective Authority
=
User Authority
∩
Agent Authority
∩
Application Authority
```

The resulting permission should not exceed the intended authorization boundary.

---

### 91. Agent Capability Boundaries

An AI agent should have explicit capabilities.

Example:

```text
HR Agent

✓ Search HR Policies
✓ Read Approved Employee Information
✗ Modify Payroll
✗ Change IAM
✗ Delete Records
```

Capabilities should be technically enforced rather than merely described in prompts.

### 92. Capability-Based Security

For AI agents, capabilities can be represented as:

```text
 ↓

 ↓

 ↓
Specific Operation
 ↓

```

This can provide finer control than broad role assignments.

```text

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓
Authorization
 ↓
```

```text
 ↓
```

```text

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

```

without checking whether the user is actually authorized to access those documents.

```text
       ↓

       ↓

       ↓

       ↓
```

```text
      ↓

      ↓
```

```text
   ↓
```

### 121. AI Identity Governance

AI identity governance establishes how identities are created, approved, assigned, reviewed, monitored, modified, and retired.

Unlike traditional application environments, AI ecosystems may contain large numbers of non-human identities.

A governance model should therefore cover:

* Human identities.
* Application identities.
* Workload identities.
* Agent identities.
* Service accounts.
* API identities.
* Administrative identities.
* External identities.

### 122. AI Identity Inventory

An enterprise should maintain an inventory of significant AI-related identities.

Example:

| Identity     | Type        | Owner          | Purpose            | Risk     |
| ------------ | ----------- | -------------- | ------------------ | -------- |
| AI-HR-Agent  | Agent       | HR IT          | HR assistance      | High     |
| RAG-Service  | Workload    | AI Platform    | Document retrieval | High     |
| AI-Gateway   | Application | Security       | API gateway        | High     |
| Model-Deploy | Service     | AI Engineering | Model deployment   | Critical |
| AI-Admin     | Privileged  | Security       | Administration     | Critical |

### 123. Identity Ownership

Every important AI identity should have a clearly assigned owner.

The owner should be accountable for:

* Business purpose.
* Access requirements.
* Periodic review.
* Risk acceptance.
* Decommissioning.
* Incident coordination.

An identity without an owner should be treated as a governance risk.

---

### 124. Identity Classification

Identities can be classified according to their function and privilege.

For example:

```text id="identity-classification"
Human
 ├── Standard
 ├── Privileged
 └── External

Non-Human
 ├── Application
 ├── Workload
 ├── Agent
 └── Service Account
```

Different identity classes can have different control requirements.

### 125. Identity Risk Classification

```text id="identity-risk"
Privilege
+
Data Access
+
Tool Access
+
Network Reach
+
Autonomy
+
Business Criticality
=
Identity Risk
```

An autonomous AI agent with production administrative capabilities should receive significantly stronger controls than a read-only chatbot.

---

### 126. Joiner-Mover-Leaver Model

Identity governance should support the traditional:

**Joiner → Mover → Leaver**

model.

For AI environments:

```text id="jml"
Create Identity
     ↓
Assign Permissions
     ↓
Change Role / Function
     ↓
Adjust Permissions
     ↓
Retire Identity
     ↓
Revoke Access
```

---

### 127. AI Identity Joiner Process

When a new AI workload or agent is introduced:

1. Identify business purpose.
2. Assign owner.
3. Register identity.
4. Define required permissions.
5. Approve access.
6. Provision identity.
7. Configure monitoring.
8. Validate controls.

---

### 128. AI Identity Mover Process

When an AI system changes purpose or capability:

```text id="identity-mover"
Existing Identity
 ↓
New Business Function
 ↓
Reassess Permissions
 ↓
Reassess Risk
 ↓
Modify Access
 ↓
Validate
```

Permissions should not simply accumulate over time.

When an AI system is retired:

```text id="identity-leaver"

 ↓

 ↓
Revoke Tokens
 ↓
Remove Permissions
 ↓
Disable Integrations
 ↓
Rotate Shared Secrets if Required
 ↓
Archive Evidence
```

### 130. Identity Lifecycle Automation

Automation can reduce identity governance gaps.

For example:

```text id="identity-automation"

 ↓
Automatic Provisioning
 ↓
Policy Assignment
 ↓
Monitoring
 ↓
Periodic Review
 ↓
Automatic Expiration
```

Automation is particularly valuable for large AI environments containing many workloads.

```text id="identity-expiration"
 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

```

```text id="jit"
 ↓
```

### 184. Central Identity Provider

It can provide:

* Group membership.
* Conditional access.
* Identity lifecycle.

AI applications should integrate with approved identity infrastructure rather than creating isolated identity stores unnecessarily.

* Access reviews.

### 186. Policy Enforcement Point

```text id="pep"

 ↓
Policy Enforcement Point
 ↓

 ↓
Policy Decision
 ↓
Allow / Deny
```

The AI application should not be the only enforcement point for sensitive access.

The policy decision point evaluates the request using relevant attributes.

```text id="pdp"

+

+
Resource
+
Context
 ↓
Policy Decision
 ↓
Permit / Deny
```

This allows identity decisions to be separated from application logic.

### 188. Policy Information Point

A policy engine may retrieve additional information needed for authorization.

Examples:

* User role.

* Device status.

```text id="pip"
 ↓
Policy Engine
 ↑
Identity / Device / Data / Risk Context
```

### 189. Identity and Policy Enforcement

A strong architecture separates:

**Authentication**

> Establish who or what is acting.

**Authorization**

> Determine what that identity is allowed to do.

**Enforcement**

> Technically prevent unauthorized activity.

This distinction is essential in AI environments.

---

### 190. Authentication Architecture

Authentication should be appropriate to the identity type.

| Identity      | Typical Authentication                 |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Employee      | MFA / federated authentication         |
| Administrator | Strong MFA / privileged access         |
| Application   | OAuth / workload identity              |
| Service       | Certificate / workload identity        |
| Agent         | Agent/workload identity                |
| API client    | OAuth / certificate / managed identity |

The implementation should be based on risk and architecture.

---

### 191. Authentication Assurance

Not every authentication event provides the same assurance.

Higher-risk operations may require stronger assurance.

```text id="assurance"
Low Risk
 ↓
Standard Authentication

High Risk
 ↓
Strong Authentication
+
Additional Verification
```

---

### 192. Multi-Factor Authentication

Human administrative access to AI infrastructure should generally use strong authentication controls, including MFA where appropriate.

MFA reduces the risk associated with compromised passwords.

---

### 193. Phishing-Resistant Authentication

For high-value administrative identities, phishing-resistant authentication provides stronger protection than password-only authentication.

Examples can include hardware-backed or cryptographic authentication mechanisms.

---

### 194. Machine Authentication

Machine identities should use authentication mechanisms appropriate to the workload.

Preferred approaches may include:

* Managed identities.
* Workload identity.
* Federated authentication.
* Certificates.
* Short-lived tokens.

Long-lived shared secrets should be minimized.

### 195. Authorization Architecture

AI authorization should evaluate more than identity alone.

A useful conceptual model is:

```text id="authorization-model"

+

+
Resource
+
Action
+
Context
+
Policy
=
Authorization Decision
```

### 196. Resource-Level Authorization

Permissions should be applied as close as practical to the protected resource.

Example:

```text id="resource-auth"
AI Agent
 ↓
API Gateway
 ↓
Authorization
 ↓
Customer Database
 ↓
Row / Record-Level Authorization
```

This creates multiple defensive layers.

---

### 197. Fine-Grained Authorization

AI systems often require granular permissions.

Instead of:

> Agent can access HR database.

Use:

> Agent can read approved HR policy records but cannot modify employee records.

The more consequential the resource, the more important fine-grained authorization becomes.

---

### 198. Attribute-Based AI Authorization

AI access decisions can consider attributes such as:

* User role.
* Agent identity.
* Data classification.
* Resource owner.
* Environment.
* Device trust.
* Session risk.
* Requested operation.

Example:

```text id="abac-ai"
User = HR Manager
+
Agent = HR-Agent
+
Resource = Confidential HR Data
+
Action = Read
+
Managed Device = Yes
```

The policy engine can determine whether the combination is permitted.

---

### 199. Relationship-Based Authorization

For example:

```text id="relationship"
 ↓
```

### 200. Dynamic Authorization

### 201. AI Identity Policy

An enterprise policy should define minimum identity requirements for AI systems.

Potential requirements include:

* Every production AI workload must have a unique identity.
* Shared privileged accounts are prohibited.
* Human administrative access requires strong authentication.
* AI agents must have controlled capabilities.
* Service identities require owners.
* Privileges must follow least privilege.
* High-risk actions require additional authorization.
* Identity activity must be auditable.

### 213. AI Memory and Identity

AI memory systems can introduce identity risks.

If an AI assistant stores user-specific information, the architecture should associate memory with the appropriate identity or tenant.

```text id="memory"

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓
Future Retrieval
```

Unauthorized users should not inherit another user's memory context.

### 214. Identity-Aware Memory

Memory access should follow authorization rules.

```text id="memory-auth"
User
 ↓
Identity
 ↓
Authorization
 ↓
Memory Store
 ↓
Authorized Memory
```

Memory should not be treated as universally available application context.

### 215. Identity and Vector Stores

Vector databases should support authorization boundaries.

A vector similarity match does not itself establish permission.

The architecture should apply access control before returning sensitive content.

---

### 216. Identity and API Tokens

AI APIs should use controlled tokens with:

* Defined audience.
* Limited scope.
* Appropriate expiration.
* Trusted issuer.
* Valid signature.
* Revocation or rotation mechanisms where applicable.

---

### 217. API Key Governance

Where API keys are unavoidable:

* Assign ownership.
* Store securely.
* Restrict permissions.
* Monitor usage.
* Rotate regularly.
* Revoke when no longer required.

API keys should not be embedded in source code.

### 230. Identity Failure Modes

Architects should consider what happens when:

* Identity provider is unavailable.
* Token validation fails.
* Authorization service is unavailable.
* Credential expires.
* Agent identity is revoked.

Failing open can create significant security exposure.

### 231. Fail-Closed Identity Design

For high-risk operations:


### 246. AI Identity Architecture Review Questions

An architect should ask:

1. Does every production AI workload have a unique identity?
2. Are human and machine identities separated?
3. Does every agent have an accountable owner?
4. Are agent permissions explicitly defined?
5. Is delegation controlled?
6. Are privileges minimized?
7. Are production and development identities separated?
8. Are privileged identities protected?
9. Are credentials short-lived where possible?
10. Can compromised identities be rapidly revoked?

---

### 247. Advanced Review Questions

Additional questions include:

11. Can identity context be propagated across the AI workflow?
12. Can downstream systems identify the originating user?
13. Can the organization distinguish user, application, and agent actions?
14. Are tenant boundaries enforced through trusted identity context?
15. Can RAG retrieval enforce user authorization?
16. Are tool permissions independently enforced?
17. Can autonomous actions require human approval?
18. Are toxic combinations of permissions identified?
19. Are identity anomalies monitored?
