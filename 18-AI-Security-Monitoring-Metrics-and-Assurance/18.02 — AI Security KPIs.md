# 18.02 — AI Security KPIs

## Part 1 — AI Security KPI Foundations, Measurement Principles, Governance, Design and Interpretation

### 1. AI Security KPI

An AI security Key Performance Indicator (KPI) is a defined measurement used to evaluate performance against an established AI security objective.

A KPI should provide information that supports governance, operational management, control improvement, or strategic decision-making.

### 2. KPI Objective

The objective of an AI security KPI is to translate an AI security objective into a measurable indicator that can be consistently evaluated over time.

A KPI should answer a defined management question rather than simply report an available data point.

### 3. KPI vs Metric

A metric is a quantitative or qualitative measurement.

A KPI is a metric selected because it has specific significance for evaluating performance against an objective.

Therefore:

**Every KPI is a metric, but not every metric is a KPI.**

### 4. KPI vs KRI

A KPI primarily measures performance against an objective.

A Key Risk Indicator (KRI) primarily provides an indicator of changing or elevated risk.

The two may use related data but serve different governance purposes.

### 5. KPI vs Control

A KPI measures performance.

A control is a mechanism designed to manage risk or achieve an objective.

A KPI can measure whether a control appears to be operating effectively, but the KPI itself is not necessarily the control.

### 6. KPI vs Control Objective

A control objective describes the desired condition a control should achieve.

A KPI provides evidence about performance against that objective.

### 7. KPI vs Target

A target defines the desired value or performance level for a KPI.

For example:

```text id="f5m8rx"
OBJECTIVE
   |
   v
KPI
   |
   v
TARGET
   |
   v
ACTUAL RESULT
   |
   v
VARIANCE
```

### 8. KPI vs Threshold

A target represents a desired performance level.

A threshold represents a boundary at which attention, escalation, or another action may be required.

A target and threshold should not automatically be treated as equivalent.

### 9. KPI Scope

AI security KPIs may measure performance across:

- AI systems;
- models;
- datasets;
- applications;
- agents;
- infrastructure;
- security controls;
- governance processes;
- suppliers;
- incident management;
- resilience.

### 10. KPI Measurement Domain

KPI domains may include:

- security;
- governance;
- risk;
- compliance;
- resilience;
- monitoring;
- incident management;
- model assurance;
- supplier security.

### 11. KPI Governance

AI security KPIs should operate within defined governance covering:

- ownership;
- definition;
- data source;
- calculation;
- frequency;
- target;
- interpretation;
- reporting;
- review.

### 12. KPI Owner

Each material KPI should have an identified owner responsible for:

- definition;
- data quality;
- calculation;
- reporting;
- interpretation;
- remediation where appropriate.

### 13. KPI Data Owner

The KPI owner and data owner may be different.

The data owner is responsible for the relevant source data, while the KPI owner is responsible for the measurement and its governance.

### 14. KPI Definition

A KPI definition should clearly establish:

- what is measured;
- why it is measured;
- how it is calculated;
- which population is included;
- which population is excluded;
- measurement frequency;
- data source;
- interpretation.

### 15. KPI Formula

Where a KPI is quantitative, its formula should be explicitly defined.

For example:

```text id="k3r7mx"
KPI = Numerator / Denominator × 100
```

The numerator and denominator must be defined consistently.

### 16. KPI Numerator

The numerator represents the quantity being counted or measured within the defined KPI calculation.

### 17. KPI Denominator

The denominator defines the reference population or total against which the numerator is evaluated.

An incorrectly defined denominator can materially distort the KPI.

### 18. KPI Population

The KPI population defines the systems, events, controls, users, models, or processes included in the measurement.

### 19. KPI Inclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria define what is counted within the KPI population.

Criteria should be explicit and consistently applied.

### 20. KPI Exclusion Criteria

Exclusion criteria define what is intentionally excluded from measurement.

Exclusions should have documented rationale where they materially affect interpretation.

### 21. KPI Measurement Period

The measurement period defines the time interval represented by the KPI.

Examples include:

- hourly;
- daily;
- weekly;
- monthly;
- quarterly;
- annually.

### 22. KPI Measurement Frequency

Measurement frequency defines how often KPI values are calculated or refreshed.

Frequency should be appropriate to:

- risk;
- operational volatility;
- decision requirements;
- data availability.

### 23. KPI Reporting Frequency

Reporting frequency defines how often KPI results are communicated to stakeholders.

Measurement frequency and reporting frequency do not have to be identical.

### 24. KPI Baseline

A KPI baseline is a reference measurement against which future performance may be compared.

A baseline may represent:

- historical performance;
- approved target state;
- pre-change condition;
- industry reference where appropriately comparable.

### 25. KPI Target

A KPI target defines the desired performance level.

Targets should have a documented rationale.

### 26. KPI Threshold

A KPI threshold defines a boundary at which additional management attention or action may be appropriate.

A threshold should not automatically be represented as a legal or regulatory requirement.

### 27. KPI Tolerance

Tolerance defines the acceptable range around a target or expected KPI value.

### 28. KPI Variance

Variance represents the difference between actual performance and the relevant target, baseline, or reference value.

### 29. KPI Trend

A KPI trend represents the direction of movement in the measurement over time.

A single KPI value may be less informative than a sustained trend.

### 30. KPI Stability

KPI stability describes the degree to which KPI values remain relatively consistent over time.

Stable performance does not necessarily mean effective security.

### 31. KPI Volatility

KPI volatility represents the degree of variation in KPI results over time.

High volatility may require contextual analysis before conclusions are drawn.

### 32. KPI Directionality

Directionality defines whether higher or lower KPI values generally represent better performance.

For example:

- higher control coverage may indicate stronger performance;
- lower unresolved critical findings may indicate stronger performance.

Directionality should be explicitly documented.

### 33. KPI Interpretation

KPI interpretation converts the measured value into meaningful management information.

Interpretation should consider:

- context;
- population;
- timeframe;
- target;
- baseline;
- material changes.

### 34. KPI Context

A KPI should be interpreted in the context of:

- AI system criticality;
- business function;
- threat environment;
- regulatory environment;
- architecture;
- operational changes.

### 35. KPI Comparability

KPI values should only be compared when their:

- definitions;
- populations;
- formulas;
- measurement periods;
- data sources

are sufficiently comparable.

### 36. KPI Consistency

KPI calculations should be applied consistently across measurement periods unless a documented methodology change occurs.

### 37. KPI Methodology

KPI methodology defines how data is collected, transformed, calculated, validated, and interpreted.

### 38. KPI Methodology Change

Changes to KPI methodology should be documented because they may affect comparability with historical results.

### 39. KPI Restatement

Where a material calculation error or methodology issue is identified, historical KPI values may require restatement.

Restatement should be governed and documented.

### 40. KPI Data Quality

KPI data quality should consider:

- accuracy;
- completeness;
- consistency;
- timeliness;
- validity;
- uniqueness where relevant.

### 41. KPI Data Lineage

Data lineage should identify the path from source data to reported KPI.

```text id="r8m4qx"
SOURCE DATA
    |
    v
COLLECTION
    |
    v
TRANSFORMATION
    |
    v
CALCULATION
    |
    v
VALIDATION
    |
    v
KPI
    |
    v
REPORTING
```

### 42. KPI Data Provenance

Data provenance identifies the origin and relevant processing history of information used to calculate a KPI.

### 43. KPI Evidence

KPI evidence is the underlying information supporting the reported KPI value.

Evidence should be sufficiently reliable to support the intended management conclusion.

### 44. KPI Evidence Sufficiency

The existence of a KPI value does not automatically demonstrate that sufficient evidence exists to support the value.

### 45. KPI Evidence Integrity

KPI evidence should be protected against inappropriate:

- alteration;
- deletion;
- manipulation;
- substitution.

### 46. KPI Calculation Integrity

KPI calculations should produce results consistent with the approved methodology.

Calculation logic should be appropriately controlled.

### 47. KPI Validation

KPI validation should confirm, as appropriate, that:

- source data is relevant;
- formulas are correct;
- calculations operate as intended;
- reported values are accurate.

### 48. KPI Assurance

KPI assurance provides confidence that a KPI is appropriately defined, calculated, reported, and supported by evidence.

KPI assurance is distinct from simply producing the KPI.

### 49. KPI Decision Usefulness

A KPI should provide information that can support a meaningful decision.

A KPI that is technically accurate but does not influence any management decision may have limited governance value.

### 50. Core Principle

**An AI security KPI should be an intentionally designed measurement that connects an AI security objective to reliable evidence and meaningful management action. KPI definitions, formulas, populations, targets, thresholds, ownership, and interpretation should be governed so that reported performance remains consistent, comparable, and decision-useful.**
