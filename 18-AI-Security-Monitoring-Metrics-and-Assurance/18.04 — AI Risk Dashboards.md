# 18.04 — AI Risk Dashboards

## Part 1 — AI Risk Dashboard Foundations, Risk Visualization, Governance, Design and Interpretation

### 1. AI Risk Dashboard

An **AI Risk Dashboard** is a structured management information interface that presents selected AI risk information in a form intended to support risk oversight, analysis, escalation, and decision-making.

An AI risk dashboard may combine:

- risk indicators;
- security indicators;
- control information;
- incidents;
- trends;
- risk appetite;
- thresholds;
- remediation;
- dependencies;
- assurance information.

A dashboard is a **presentation and decision-support mechanism**. It is not itself a security control or an assurance conclusion.

### 2. AI Risk Dashboard Objective

The objective of an AI risk dashboard is to provide decision-makers with sufficiently relevant, timely, accurate, and contextual information about AI risk.

The dashboard should help management understand:

- current risk conditions;
- changes in risk;
- material exposures;
- concentrations;
- emerging issues;
- exceptions;
- required decisions.

### 3. Dashboard vs Report

A report generally presents structured information for a defined audience and reporting purpose.

A dashboard emphasizes:

- current status;
- trends;
- exceptions;
- visualization;
- interactive analysis;
- decision support.

The distinction is architectural and functional rather than absolute.

### 4. Dashboard vs KPI

A KPI is a measurement selected to evaluate performance against an objective.

A dashboard may display multiple KPIs but is not itself a KPI.

### 5. Dashboard vs KRI

A KRI provides information about changing risk conditions.

A dashboard may display KRIs alongside other information.

The dashboard should preserve the distinction between risk indicators and performance indicators.

### 6. Dashboard vs Risk Register

A risk register records structured information about identified risks.

A dashboard presents selected risk information for monitoring and decision-making.

A dashboard should not replace the authoritative risk register unless the governance design explicitly provides equivalent functionality.

### 7. Dashboard vs Control

A dashboard is not a control merely because it displays control information.

The underlying controls remain responsible for managing the relevant risk.

### 8. Dashboard vs Assurance

A dashboard may provide evidence used by assurance activities.

It does not automatically constitute assurance.

Dashboard presentation should therefore be distinguished from independent validation or assurance.

### 9. Dashboard Audience

Dashboard design should identify the intended audience.

Possible audiences include:

- security operations;
- AI governance teams;
- risk management;
- compliance;
- senior management;
- executive committees;
- boards;
- internal audit.

Different audiences may require different levels of aggregation and detail.

### 10. Dashboard Decision Purpose

Each material dashboard element should support a defined decision, oversight responsibility, or governance objective.

A dashboard should avoid displaying information solely because the information is available.

### 11. Dashboard Scope

Dashboard scope defines the AI systems, risks, business units, services, suppliers, and environments represented.

Scope should be explicit.

### 12. Dashboard Boundary

The dashboard boundary defines what is included and excluded.

Possible boundaries include:

- enterprise AI;
- production AI;
- high-risk AI;
- AI security;
- AI third-party risk;
- AI resilience;
- specific business services.

### 13. Dashboard Coverage

Coverage represents the extent to which material AI risk domains are represented in the dashboard.

Coverage should be assessed against the organization's defined risk universe.

### 14. Dashboard Coverage Gap

A coverage gap exists when a material risk or risk population is not adequately represented.

A visually comprehensive dashboard can still have material coverage gaps.

### 15. Dashboard Data Source

Each material dashboard element should have an identifiable data source.

Sources may include:

- GRC platforms;
- SIEM systems;
- security monitoring;
- asset inventories;
- model registries;
- incident-management platforms;
- supplier-risk systems;
- audit systems.

### 16. Dashboard Data Lineage

Data lineage should establish the path from source information to the displayed dashboard value.

```text id="dashline01"
MANAGEMENT DECISION
```

### 17. Dashboard Data Provenance

Data provenance identifies where dashboard information originated and provides context about how it was produced.

Provenance supports interpretation and assurance.

### 18. Dashboard Data Quality

Dashboard data should be evaluated for:

- accuracy;
- completeness;
- consistency;
- timeliness;
- integrity;
- relevance.

Poor data quality can produce misleading dashboard conclusions.

### 19. Dashboard Data Freshness

Data freshness indicates how current the underlying information is.

Freshness requirements should reflect the decision being supported.

### 20. Dashboard Data Timeliness

Timeliness concerns whether information becomes available sufficiently early to support the intended decision.

Fresh data that arrives too late may have limited decision value.

### 21. Dashboard Data Completeness

Completeness should consider whether material AI systems, risks, events, and dependencies are represented.

### 22. Dashboard Data Accuracy

Accuracy concerns whether the displayed information correctly represents the underlying source data.

### 23. Dashboard Data Integrity

Dashboard information should be protected against unauthorized or unexplained alteration.

### 24. Dashboard Data Consistency

Definitions, classifications, and calculation methods should remain consistent across dashboard views unless an approved methodology change exists.

### 25. Dashboard Data Validation

Material dashboard values should be validated according to their significance and intended use.

Validation may include:

- automated checks;
- reconciliation;
- exception testing;
- sampling;
- independent review.

### 26. Dashboard Calculation Logic

Calculated dashboard values should use documented and controlled logic.

Material calculation changes should be subject to appropriate change management.

### 27. Dashboard Calculation Integrity

Management should be able to determine that displayed values have not been improperly altered during calculation or presentation.

### 28. Dashboard Refresh Frequency

Refresh frequency should be appropriate to the volatility and decision importance of the underlying information.

Possible frequencies include:

- real time;
- near real time;
- hourly;
- daily;
- weekly;
- monthly;
- quarterly.

### 29. Dashboard Real-Time Data

Real-time information may be appropriate for rapidly changing operational risks.

Real-time presentation does not automatically establish accuracy or assurance.

### 30. Dashboard Near-Real-Time Data

Near-real-time data may provide sufficient timeliness while reducing unnecessary infrastructure and processing requirements.

### 31. Dashboard Historical Data

Historical information should be retained where trend analysis or governance decisions require comparison over time.

### 32. Dashboard Trend

A trend represents the directional movement of a measurement over a defined period.

Trend interpretation should consider:

- duration;
- magnitude;
- volatility;
- baseline;
- context.

### 33. Dashboard Baseline

A baseline provides a reference point against which current dashboard values can be interpreted.

The baseline should have a documented definition and effective period.

### 34. Dashboard Target

A target represents a desired or intended condition.

Targets should not be confused with risk thresholds.

### 35. Dashboard Threshold

A threshold defines a boundary at which management attention, escalation, investigation, or action may be required.

An internally defined threshold is not automatically a statutory or regulatory threshold.

### 36. Dashboard Tolerance

Tolerance defines an acceptable range around a target, risk condition, or governance expectation.

### 37. Dashboard Variance

Variance represents the difference between the current measurement and a relevant reference such as:

- target;
- baseline;
- tolerance;
- prior period.

### 38. Dashboard Status

Dashboard status summarizes the current condition according to defined classification rules.

Status classifications should have documented meanings.

### 39. Dashboard Status Semantics

Terms such as:

- normal;
- attention;
- elevated;
- critical

should have defined internal criteria.

They should not be treated as universal regulatory classifications unless an applicable external framework defines them.

### 40. Dashboard Risk Appetite

Where applicable, dashboard information may display the relationship between current risk and approved risk appetite.

The dashboard should distinguish:

- risk appetite;
- risk tolerance;
- internal threshold;
- regulatory requirement.

### 41. Dashboard Risk Exposure

Risk exposure represents the organization's current or estimated exposure to defined risks.

Exposure should be distinguished from:

- risk event;
- incident;
- control failure;
- loss.

### 42. Dashboard Residual Risk

Residual risk represents the risk remaining after relevant controls and treatments have been considered.

Dashboard reporting should identify whether risk values represent inherent or residual risk where the distinction is material.

### 43. Dashboard Inherent Risk

Inherent risk represents risk before consideration of relevant controls or treatments.

The dashboard should clearly identify the basis of any displayed inherent-risk measurement.

### 44. Dashboard Risk Concentration

Dashboards should identify material concentrations where risk is clustered within:

- models;
- suppliers;
- cloud providers;
- business units;
- regions;
- datasets;
- applications.

### 45. Dashboard Risk Aggregation

Aggregation allows management to view risk across multiple assets or organizational units.

Aggregation should not conceal material local risk.

### 46. Dashboard Risk Disaggregation

Disaggregation allows management to examine risk by relevant dimensions.

Possible dimensions include:

- AI system;
- model;
- business service;
- supplier;
- geography;
- environment;
- risk category.

### 47. Dashboard Risk Context

Risk values should be accompanied by sufficient context to prevent misleading interpretation.

Context may include:

- population;
- criticality;
- trend;
- threshold;
- uncertainty;
- recent incidents;
- material dependencies.

### 48. Dashboard Materiality

Dashboard design should prioritize information according to materiality.

Materiality may consider:

- business impact;
- security impact;
- customer impact;
- regulatory significance;
- financial exposure;
- operational criticality.

### 49. Dashboard Decision Usefulness

A dashboard should be evaluated according to whether it helps authorized decision-makers:

- identify material conditions;
- understand trends;
- prioritize action;
- challenge assumptions;
- allocate resources;
- oversee risk.

### 50. Core Principle

**An AI risk dashboard should transform controlled risk information into decision-useful management information without obscuring uncertainty, context, concentration, or measurement limitations. A dashboard is a governance interface, not a substitute for the underlying risk register, controls, evidence, or independent assurance. Dashboard status, thresholds, colors, scores, and classifications should have explicitly defined meanings and should not be presented as legal or regulatory classifications unless an applicable external requirement establishes them.**

Internal `AI-SM-DASH-*` identifiers remain knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations.

## Part 2 — AI Risk Dashboard Controls, Data Governance, Visualization, Risk Thresholds and Reporting

### 51. AI Risk Dashboard Policy

The organization should establish a dashboard policy defining:

- dashboard governance;
- ownership;
- data requirements;
- design standards;
- refresh requirements;
- access controls;
- reporting;
- validation;
- change management;
- retention.

### 52. AI Risk Dashboard Inventory

A dashboard inventory should identify material dashboards and document:

- dashboard name;
- purpose;
- audience;
- owner;
- data sources;
- refresh frequency;
- risk domains;
- criticality;
- access classification.

### 53. AI Risk Dashboard Register

The dashboard register should provide a controlled record of approved dashboards and their associated governance requirements.

### 54. AI Risk Dashboard Ownership

Each material dashboard should have an accountable owner responsible for:

- purpose;
- scope;
- data requirements;
- interpretation;
- maintenance;
- governance;
- change management.

### 55. AI Risk Dashboard Data Ownership

Data ownership should define responsibility for the quality and availability of source information.

Dashboard ownership and source-data ownership may belong to different functions.

### 56. AI Risk Dashboard Audience Control

Dashboard access should be aligned with the intended audience.

A board dashboard, executive dashboard, security-operations dashboard, and technical dashboard may require materially different levels of detail.

### 57. AI Risk Dashboard Access Control

Access should be governed according to:

- role;
- business need;
- information sensitivity;
- organizational responsibility.

### 58. AI Risk Dashboard Classification

Dashboards should be classified according to the sensitivity of their contents.

Security dashboards may contain information about:

- vulnerabilities;
- incidents;
- security architecture;
- suppliers;
- critical systems;
- control weaknesses.

### 59. AI Risk Dashboard Segregation

Where appropriate, sensitive operational information should be separated from broader executive information to reduce unnecessary exposure.

### 60. AI Risk Dashboard Purpose Definition

Each dashboard should have a documented purpose stating:

- what decisions it supports;
- what risks it represents;
- who uses it;
- what information it requires.

### 61. AI Risk Dashboard Scope Control

Dashboard scope should be controlled so that material AI systems and risk populations are neither unintentionally omitted nor inconsistently included.

### 62. AI Risk Dashboard Risk-Domain Coverage

The dashboard should identify the AI risk domains it covers, such as:

- cybersecurity;
- model security;
- data security;
- privacy;
- resilience;
- third-party risk;
- AI safety.

### 63. AI Risk Dashboard Asset Coverage

Material AI assets should be appropriately represented according to dashboard purpose and risk significance.

### 64. AI Risk Dashboard Model Coverage

Where model-level risk is material, dashboards should distinguish relevant model populations and versions.

### 65. AI Risk Dashboard Business-Service Coverage

Dashboards should map material AI risks to critical business services where this improves decision-making.

### 66. AI Risk Dashboard Supplier Coverage

Material AI suppliers and dependencies should be represented where supplier exposure affects enterprise risk.

### 67. AI Risk Dashboard Fourth-Party Coverage

Where material, dashboards should provide appropriate visibility into fourth-party dependencies.

### 68. AI Risk Dashboard Risk-Register Integration

Where appropriate, dashboard risk information should be linked to authoritative risk-register records.

The dashboard should not create conflicting versions of the same risk information.

### 69. AI Risk Dashboard Asset-Inventory Integration

Dashboard populations should be reconciled with relevant AI asset inventories where practical.

### 70. AI Risk Dashboard Incident Integration

Material incident information may be incorporated into dashboards to provide context for current risk conditions.

### 71. AI Risk Dashboard Control Integration

Dashboard information may include control-performance information where it helps explain risk exposure.

Control performance should remain distinguishable from risk exposure.

### 72. AI Risk Dashboard KPI Integration

Relevant AI security KPIs may be displayed alongside risk indicators.

KPI and KRI values should remain clearly differentiated.

### 73. AI Risk Dashboard KRI Integration

Material KRIs should be incorporated where they provide meaningful visibility into changing risk conditions.

### 74. AI Risk Dashboard Assurance Integration

Dashboard views may include assurance status such as:

- assessment completion;
- audit findings;
- control-testing results;
- validation status.

Assurance status should not be interpreted as equivalent to complete assurance.

### 75. AI Risk Dashboard Remediation Integration

Material remediation information should be connected to relevant risks where appropriate.

### 76. AI Risk Dashboard Exception Integration

Material exceptions should be visible where they could materially affect risk interpretation.

### 77. AI Risk Dashboard Data-Lineage Control

Material dashboard values should have traceable lineage from source data through transformation and calculation to presentation.

### 78. AI Risk Dashboard Source Validation

Critical data sources should be validated for:

- accuracy;
- completeness;
- timeliness;
- integrity;
- relevance.

### 79. AI Risk Dashboard Source Reconciliation

Where appropriate, dashboard data should be reconciled with authoritative source systems.

### 80. AI Risk Dashboard Data Transformation Control

Material data transformations should be documented and controlled.

### 81. AI Risk Dashboard Calculation Control

Calculated values should use approved and version-controlled calculation logic.

### 82. AI Risk Dashboard Calculation Testing

Calculation logic should be tested to verify that displayed values correspond to approved methodology.

### 83. AI Risk Dashboard Refresh Control

Refresh processes should be monitored to identify:

- failed refreshes;
- delayed refreshes;
- incomplete updates;
- stale information.

### 84. AI Risk Dashboard Staleness Detection

Dashboards should identify when displayed information is materially older than the defined freshness requirement.

### 85. AI Risk Dashboard Timestamping

Material dashboard information should include appropriate timestamps indicating:

- measurement period;
- calculation time;
- refresh time;
- reporting period.

### 86. AI Risk Dashboard Historical Comparison

Where trend analysis is required, dashboards should preserve sufficient historical information for meaningful comparison.

### 87. AI Risk Dashboard Baseline Control

Baselines used in dashboard comparisons should be documented and governed.

### 88. AI Risk Dashboard Target Control

Targets should have:

- defined ownership;

- approval;
- review criteria.

### 89. AI Risk Dashboard Threshold Control

Thresholds should have documented:

- definition;
- rationale;
- owner;

### 90. AI Risk Dashboard Threshold Calibration

- historical conditions;
- risk appetite;

### 91. AI Risk Dashboard Threshold Testing

Threshold logic should be tested to determine whether it produces appropriate:

- alerts;

### 92. AI Risk Dashboard Risk-Appetite Mapping

### 93. AI Risk Dashboard Risk-Tolerance Mapping

Dashboard views should identify relevant tolerance boundaries where formally defined.

### 94. AI Risk Dashboard Status Logic

### 95. AI Risk Dashboard Status Consistency

Equivalent conditions should produce consistent status classifications unless an approved methodology explicitly provides otherwise.

- authorized user;

- date;
- supporting evidence.

Dashboard design should avoid relying solely on color to communicate material information.

Alternative indicators such as:

- text;
- symbols;
- values;
- patterns

may improve accessibility and interpretation.

Scoring should document:

- input variables;
- weighting;

- classification;
- limitations.

### 103. AI Risk Dashboard Composite Indicator

### 104. AI Risk Dashboard Score Decomposition

A heat map may visualize risk according to dimensions such as:

- likelihood;
- impact;
- criticality;
- exposure.

Heat-map methodology should be documented.

Heat maps simplify complex risk information.

They should not replace detailed risk analysis where material decisions depend on underlying evidence.

### 107. AI Risk Dashboard Risk Trend Visualization

Trend visualizations should clearly distinguish:

- observed values;
- targets;
- thresholds;
- forecasts;
- baselines.

### 108. AI Risk Dashboard Forecast Visualization

Forecasts should be visually distinguishable from observed measurements.

Forecast uncertainty should be communicated where material.

Where measurement uncertainty is material, dashboards may display:

- confidence ranges;
- uncertainty bands;

- evidence-confidence levels.

### 110. AI Risk Dashboard Data-Quality Indicator

Dashboards should identify material data-quality limitations where they could affect decision-making.

### 111. AI Risk Dashboard Data-Completeness Indicator

### 112. AI Risk Dashboard Data-Freshness Indicator

- current;
- delayed;
- stale;
- unavailable.

### 113. AI Risk Dashboard Evidence-Confidence Indicator

Where appropriate, dashboard elements may include an indication of evidence confidence.

Confidence should reflect the quality of the underlying evidence rather than the visual prominence of the dashboard element.

### 114. AI Risk Dashboard Uncertainty Disclosure

Material uncertainty should be disclosed rather than hidden through apparently precise numerical values.

### 115. AI Risk Dashboard Population Indicator

Material dashboard metrics should identify the relevant population where omission could cause misinterpretation.

### 116. AI Risk Dashboard Denominator Visibility

Where percentages are displayed, the underlying denominator should be available or clearly defined where it materially affects interpretation.

### 117. AI Risk Dashboard Aggregation Control

Aggregate dashboard values should preserve sufficient information to identify material concentrations.

### 119. AI Risk Dashboard Filtering

Filtering should use controlled dimensions such as:

- risk category;
- criticality;
- business unit;
- model;
- supplier;
- geography;
- environment.

### 120. AI Risk Dashboard Filter Governance

Filters should not silently exclude material populations.

Users should be able to determine the active filter conditions where appropriate.

### 121. AI Risk Dashboard Aggregation Integrity

Aggregation logic should be tested to ensure that lower-level data is represented correctly at higher levels.

### 122. AI Risk Dashboard Exception Drill-Down

Material exceptions should be traceable to the underlying:

- risk;
- system;
- control;
- finding;
- evidence.

### 123. AI Risk Dashboard Management Commentary

Material dashboard deviations should support management commentary explaining:

- what changed;
- why it changed;
- whether it is material;
- what action is being taken.

### 124. AI Risk Dashboard Escalation

Dashboard conditions meeting defined escalation criteria should trigger the appropriate governance process.

A dashboard escalation condition is an internal governance mechanism unless a specific legal, regulatory, or contractual requirement establishes an external obligation.

### 125. Core Control Principle

**AI risk dashboard controls should ensure that dashboard information is accurate, traceable, appropriately current, consistently defined, access-controlled, and presented with sufficient context for its intended audience. Visualization, aggregation, scoring, thresholds, and status classifications should be governed so that they do not conceal material risk or create false precision. A dashboard should support risk governance without being treated as a substitute for underlying evidence, risk assessment, controls, or independent assurance.**

## Part 3 — Continuous AI Risk Dashboard Monitoring, Advanced Analytics, Assurance and Resilience

### 126. AI Risk Dashboard Continuous Monitoring

Material AI risk dashboards should be monitored to ensure that displayed information remains:

- current;
- accurate;
- complete;
- available;
- appropriately contextualized.

Continuous dashboard monitoring should be proportionate to the importance and volatility of the information presented.

### 127. AI Risk Dashboard Monitoring Frequency

Dashboard monitoring frequency should reflect:

- risk criticality;
- data volatility;
- refresh requirements;
- decision urgency;
- operational dependency.

### 128. AI Risk Dashboard Refresh Monitoring

Refresh processes should be monitored for:

- failed refreshes;
- delayed refreshes;
- partial updates;
- stale data;
- unexpected changes.

### 129. AI Risk Dashboard Staleness Detection

Material dashboard elements should be evaluated for data staleness.

A stale dashboard should not present outdated information as current.

### 130. AI Risk Dashboard Availability Monitoring

Critical dashboards should be monitored for availability where their loss could impair material risk decisions.

### 131. AI Risk Dashboard Integrity Monitoring

Dashboard integrity monitoring should identify unauthorized or unexplained changes to:

- calculations;
- thresholds;
- filters;
- classifications;
- displayed values.

### 132. AI Risk Dashboard Calculation Monitoring

Automated dashboard calculations should be monitored for:

- execution failure;
- unexpected results;
- missing inputs;
- calculation errors;
- unauthorized logic changes.

### 133. AI Risk Dashboard Data-Pipeline Monitoring

Critical data pipelines supporting dashboards should be monitored for:

- interruption;
- latency;
- incomplete processing;
- transformation errors;
- source-system failures.

### 134. AI Risk Dashboard Pipeline Failure

Pipeline failure should be distinguished from genuine changes in risk.

A missing or corrupted dataset should not automatically produce a normal-risk dashboard state.

### 135. AI Risk Dashboard Measurement Failure

Measurement failure should be explicitly represented where dashboard information cannot be reliably calculated.

### 136. AI Risk Dashboard Data-Quality Monitoring

Dashboard data quality should be continuously assessed where deterioration could materially affect risk interpretation.

### 137. AI Risk Dashboard Completeness Monitoring

Completeness monitoring should identify:

- missing AI systems;
- missing risks;
- missing suppliers;
- missing incidents;
- missing control information.

### 138. AI Risk Dashboard Data-Lineage Monitoring

Material changes in dashboard data lineage should be identified and reviewed.

### 139. AI Risk Dashboard Source-System Change

Changes to source systems should be assessed for their effect on:

- data definitions;
- calculations;
- population;
- historical comparability;
- dashboard interpretation.

### 140. AI Risk Dashboard Methodology Change

Changes to dashboard methodology should be governed and documented.

Material methodology changes may affect historical comparisons.

### 141. AI Risk Dashboard Historical Comparability

Where necessary, historical values may be reconstructed using the revised methodology, with the reconstruction clearly identified.

### 142. AI Risk Dashboard Trend Monitoring

Dashboard trends should be monitored for:

- sustained deterioration;
- improvement;
- volatility;
- trend breaks;
- recurring exceptions.

- genuine risk change;
- control change;
- model change;
- supplier change;
- population change;
- measurement change.

### 144. AI Risk Dashboard Risk Acceleration

Dashboards may identify increasing rates of risk deterioration where acceleration provides useful early-warning information.

### 145. AI Risk Dashboard Risk Persistence

Persistent risk conditions should be distinguishable from isolated threshold excursions.

### 146. AI Risk Dashboard Risk Recurrence

Recurring risk conditions should be identified where previously addressed conditions return.

### 147. AI Risk Dashboard Outlier Analysis

Material outliers should be investigated rather than automatically removed.

The dashboard should distinguish genuine outliers from known data-quality errors where appropriate.

### 148. AI Risk Dashboard Volatility Analysis

High volatility should be evaluated to determine whether it represents:

- genuine risk variation;

- operational variability;
- measurement instability.

### 149. AI Risk Dashboard Statistical Analysis

- anomaly detection;
- trend analysis;

Dashboard analysis may compare related KPIs to determine whether performance changes correspond with changes in risk.

Performance improvement does not automatically establish risk reduction.

## Part 4 — Enterprise AI Risk Dashboard Governance, Strategic Assurance, Advanced Analytics, Automation and Maturity

### 211. Enterprise Dashboard Governance
Enterprise dashboard governance establishes the organizational framework for defining, approving, operating, maintaining, and assuring AI risk dashboards. Governance should establish ownership, decision rights, data responsibilities, access rules, reporting expectations, change controls, evidence requirements, and escalation mechanisms. A dashboard should be governed as an enterprise decision-support capability rather than merely as a visualization product.

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-211`

### 212. Dashboard Governance Committee
Organizations may establish a governance forum responsible for significant dashboard decisions, including dashboard scope, materiality, risk representation, metric definitions, threshold changes, access, methodology changes, and assurance requirements. The existence and authority of such a committee are governance design choices unless required by a specific organizational or regulatory framework.

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-212`

### 213. Dashboard Accountability
Dashboard accountability should identify the individuals or functions responsible for dashboard accuracy, data quality, calculation methodology, risk interpretation, access administration, technical operation, and management use. Accountability should not be transferred merely because dashboard production is automated or outsourced.

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-213`

### 214. Dashboard Three-Lines Alignment
Dashboard responsibilities should align with the organization's governance and assurance model. Management functions may own dashboard production and risk management, independent risk or compliance functions may provide oversight, and internal audit or another independent assurance function may evaluate the effectiveness of relevant governance and controls.

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-214`

### 215. Dashboard Taxonomy
The organization should establish a consistent taxonomy for dashboard domains, risk categories, assets, models, business services, suppliers, controls, KPIs, KRIs, incidents, exceptions, and assurance activities. A controlled taxonomy improves aggregation and comparability while reducing inconsistent interpretation across dashboards.

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-215`

### 216. Dashboard Portfolio Management
Large organizations may operate multiple dashboards across business units, security functions, AI platforms, geographic regions, and management levels. Portfolio management should identify overlapping dashboards, duplicated metrics, inconsistent definitions, conflicting thresholds, obsolete dashboards, and unmanaged reporting dependencies.

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-216`

### 217. Dashboard Rationalization
Dashboard rationalization evaluates whether each dashboard has a defined audience, decision purpose, owner, data source, reporting requirement, and continued business value. Dashboards that duplicate other reporting capabilities or no longer support decisions should be modified, consolidated, or retired.

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-217`

### 218. Dashboard Standardization
Standardization should establish common conventions for metric definitions, risk terminology, timestamps, aggregation levels, status semantics, threshold representation, metadata, ownership, and evidence references. Standardization should not eliminate legitimate differences between business contexts.

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-218`

### 219. Dashboard Reference Definitions
Organizations should maintain authoritative definitions for important dashboard measures. A reference definition should identify the metric name, meaning, formula, population, data source, calculation method, owner, frequency, and relevant interpretation rules.

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-219`

### 220. Dashboard Measurement Hierarchy
Dashboard information should be structured across appropriate levels, such as enterprise, business unit, portfolio, system, model, service, control, and operational event. **Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-220`

### 221. Strategic Dashboard Alignment
Strategic dashboards should connect AI security information to organizational objectives, business services, critical processes, material risks, and strategic dependencies. A technically sophisticated dashboard may still have limited governance value if it does not support material organizational decisions.

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-221`

### 222. Business-Service Alignment
AI risk dashboards should identify where AI capabilities support important business services. Risk visibility should therefore extend beyond individual models to the business processes and services that depend on those models.

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-222`

### 223. Risk-Appetite Alignment

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-223`

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-224`

### 225. Dashboard Materiality
Dashboard design should reflect materiality. Highly detailed information may be appropriate for operational users, while executive dashboards should emphasize material exposures, significant changes, critical dependencies, unresolved issues, and decisions requiring management attention.

### 400. Core Enterprise Dashboard Assurance Principle
AI risk dashboards are enterprise governance mechanisms for converting complex AI security, risk, control, incident, resilience, and assurance information into decision-useful visibility.

A mature dashboard capability should provide:

- defined governance and accountability;
- reliable and traceable data;
- controlled calculations and methodologies;
- appropriate risk aggregation and disaggregation;
- materiality and criticality context;
- visibility into concentration and systemic dependencies;
- appropriate uncertainty and evidence representation;
- controlled thresholds, targets, and status semantics;
- resilience and reporting continuity;
- appropriate human oversight;
- controlled automation and escalation;
- supplier and fourth-party visibility;
- historical integrity and reproducibility;
- independent assurance where appropriate;
- continuous improvement and maturity management.

The following distinctions remain fundamental:

```text
DASHBOARD       ≠ RISK REGISTER
DASHBOARD       ≠ CONTROL
DASHBOARD       ≠ ASSURANCE
DASHBOARD       ≠ COMPLIANCE
DASHBOARD       ≠ SECURITY
DASHBOARD       ≠ RISK ELIMINATION
STATUS          ≠ TRUTH
AUTOMATION      ≠ ACCOUNTABILITY
VISIBILITY      ≠ ASSURANCE
AVAILABILITY    ≠ INTEGRITY
CORRELATION     ≠ CAUSATION
THRESHOLD       ≠ LEGAL REQUIREMENT
INTERNAL SCORE  ≠ REGULATORY CLASSIFICATION
METRIC          ≠ RISK
DASHBOARD       ≠ COMPLETE AI RISK VISIBILITY
```

The objective of enterprise AI risk dashboard governance is therefore not to create a visually sophisticated reporting environment. It is to establish a controlled, traceable, resilient, contextual, and appropriately assured mechanism through which decision-makers can understand AI risk exposure and take accountable action.

**Control ID:** `AI-SM-DASH-400`

**Core principle:** A dashboard is trustworthy only to the extent that its underlying data, methodology, calculations, context, dependencies, governance, and assurance are trustworthy. Dashboard sophistication, automation, availability, or visual clarity alone does not establish AI security effectiveness, regulatory compliance, or elimination of risk.
