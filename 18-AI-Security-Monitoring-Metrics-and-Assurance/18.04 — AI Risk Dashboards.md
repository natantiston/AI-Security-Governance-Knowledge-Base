# 18.04 — AI Risk Dashboards

## Part 1 — AI Risk Dashboard Foundations, Risk Visualization, Governance, Design and Interpretation

### 1. AI Risk Dashboard

An **AI Risk Dashboard** is a structured management information interface that presents selected AI risk information in a form intended to support risk oversight, analysis, escalation, and decision-making.

An AI risk dashboard may combine:

- risk indicators;
- security indicators;
- control information;
- incidents;
- trends;
- risk appetite;
- thresholds;
- remediation;
- dependencies;
- assurance information.

A dashboard is a **presentation and decision-support mechanism**. It is not itself a security control or an assurance conclusion.

### 2. AI Risk Dashboard Objective

The objective of an AI risk dashboard is to provide decision-makers with sufficiently relevant, timely, accurate, and contextual information about AI risk.

The dashboard should help management understand:

- current risk conditions;
- changes in risk;
- material exposures;
- concentrations;
- emerging issues;
- exceptions;
- required decisions.

### 3. Dashboard vs Report

A report generally presents structured information for a defined audience and reporting purpose.

A dashboard emphasizes:

- current status;
- trends;
- exceptions;
- visualization;
- interactive analysis;
- decision support.

The distinction is architectural and functional rather than absolute.

### 4. Dashboard vs KPI

A KPI is a measurement selected to evaluate performance against an objective.

A dashboard may display multiple KPIs but is not itself a KPI.

### 5. Dashboard vs KRI

A KRI provides information about changing risk conditions.

A dashboard may display KRIs alongside other information.

The dashboard should preserve the distinction between risk indicators and performance indicators.

### 6. Dashboard vs Risk Register

A risk register records structured information about identified risks.

A dashboard presents selected risk information for monitoring and decision-making.

A dashboard should not replace the authoritative risk register unless the governance design explicitly provides equivalent functionality.

### 7. Dashboard vs Control

A dashboard is not a control merely because it displays control information.

The underlying controls remain responsible for managing the relevant risk.

### 8. Dashboard vs Assurance

A dashboard may provide evidence used by assurance activities.

It does not automatically constitute assurance.

Dashboard presentation should therefore be distinguished from independent validation or assurance.

### 9. Dashboard Audience

Dashboard design should identify the intended audience.

Possible audiences include:

- security operations;
- AI governance teams;
- risk management;
- compliance;
- senior management;
- executive committees;
- boards;
- internal audit.

Different audiences may require different levels of aggregation and detail.

### 10. Dashboard Decision Purpose

Each material dashboard element should support a defined decision, oversight responsibility, or governance objective.

A dashboard should avoid displaying information solely because the information is available.

### 11. Dashboard Scope

Dashboard scope defines the AI systems, risks, business units, services, suppliers, and environments represented.

Scope should be explicit.

### 12. Dashboard Boundary

The dashboard boundary defines what is included and excluded.

Possible boundaries include:

- enterprise AI;
- production AI;
- high-risk AI;
- AI security;
- AI third-party risk;
- AI resilience;
- specific business services.

### 13. Dashboard Coverage

Coverage represents the extent to which material AI risk domains are represented in the dashboard.

Coverage should be assessed against the organization's defined risk universe.

### 14. Dashboard Coverage Gap

A coverage gap exists when a material risk or risk population is not adequately represented.

A visually comprehensive dashboard can still have material coverage gaps.

### 15. Dashboard Data Source

Each material dashboard element should have an identifiable data source.

Sources may include:

- GRC platforms;
- SIEM systems;
- security monitoring;
- asset inventories;
- model registries;
- incident-management platforms;
- supplier-risk systems;
- audit systems.

### 16. Dashboard Data Lineage

Data lineage should establish the path from source information to the displayed dashboard value.

### 17. Dashboard Data Provenance

Data provenance identifies where dashboard information originated and provides context about how it was produced.

Provenance supports interpretation and assurance.

### 18. Dashboard Data Quality

Dashboard data should be evaluated for:

- accuracy;
- completeness;
- consistency;
- timeliness;
- integrity;
- relevance.

Poor data quality can produce misleading dashboard conclusions.

### 19. Dashboard Data Freshness

Data freshness indicates how current the underlying information is.

Freshness requirements should reflect the decision being supported.

### 20. Dashboard Data Timeliness

Timeliness concerns whether information becomes available sufficiently early to support the intended decision.

Fresh data that arrives too late may have limited decision value.

### 21. Dashboard Data Completeness

Completeness should consider whether material AI systems, risks, events, and dependencies are represented.

### 22. Dashboard Data Accuracy

Accuracy concerns whether the displayed information correctly represents the underlying source data.

### 23. Dashboard Data Integrity

Dashboard information should be protected against unauthorized or unexplained alteration.

### 24. Dashboard Data Consistency

Definitions, classifications, and calculation methods should remain consistent across dashboard views unless an approved methodology change exists.

### 25. Dashboard Data Validation

Material dashboard values should be validated according to their significance and intended use.

Validation may include:

- automated checks;
- reconciliation;
- exception testing;
- sampling;
- independent review.

### 26. Dashboard Calculation Logic

Calculated dashboard values should use documented and controlled logic.

Material calculation changes should be subject to appropriate change management.

### 27. Dashboard Calculation Integrity

Management should be able to determine that displayed values have not been improperly altered during calculation or presentation.

### 28. Dashboard Refresh Frequency

Refresh frequency should be appropriate to the volatility and decision importance of the underlying information.

Possible frequencies include:

- real time;
- near real time;
- hourly;
- daily;
- weekly;
- monthly;
- quarterly.

### 29. Dashboard Real-Time Data

Real-time information may be appropriate for rapidly changing operational risks.

Real-time presentation does not automatically establish accuracy or assurance.

### 30. Dashboard Near-Real-Time Data

Near-real-time data may provide sufficient timeliness while reducing unnecessary infrastructure and processing requirements.

### 31. Dashboard Historical Data

Historical information should be retained where trend analysis or governance decisions require comparison over time.

### 32. Dashboard Trend

A trend represents the directional movement of a measurement over a defined period.

Trend interpretation should consider:

- duration;
- magnitude;
- volatility;
- baseline;
- context.

### 33. Dashboard Baseline

A baseline provides a reference point against which current dashboard values can be interpreted.

The baseline should have a documented definition and effective period.

### 34. Dashboard Target

A target represents a desired or intended condition.

Targets should not be confused with risk thresholds.

### 35. Dashboard Threshold

A threshold defines a boundary at which management attention, escalation, investigation, or action may be required.

An internally defined threshold is not automatically a statutory or regulatory threshold.

### 36. Dashboard Tolerance

Tolerance defines an acceptable range around a target, risk condition, or governance expectation.

### 37. Dashboard Variance

Variance represents the difference between the current measurement and a relevant reference such as:

- target;
- baseline;
- tolerance;
- prior period.

### 38. Dashboard Status

Dashboard status summarizes the current condition according to defined classification rules.

Status classifications should have documented meanings.

### 39. Dashboard Status Semantics

Terms such as:

- normal;
- attention;
- elevated;
- critical

should have defined internal criteria.

They should not be treated as universal regulatory classifications unless an applicable external framework defines them.

### 40. Dashboard Risk Appetite

Where applicable, dashboard information may display the relationship between current risk and approved risk appetite.

The dashboard should distinguish:

- risk appetite;
- risk tolerance;
- internal threshold;
- regulatory requirement.

### 41. Dashboard Risk Exposure

Risk exposure represents the organization's current or estimated exposure to defined risks.

Exposure should be distinguished from:

- risk event;
- incident;
- control failure;
- loss.

### 42. Dashboard Residual Risk

Residual risk represents the risk remaining after relevant controls and treatments have been considered.

Dashboard reporting should identify whether risk values represent inherent or residual risk where the distinction is material.

### 43. Dashboard Inherent Risk

Inherent risk represents risk before consideration of relevant controls or treatments.

The dashboard should clearly identify the basis of any displayed inherent-risk measurement.

### 44. Dashboard Risk Concentration

Dashboards should identify material concentrations where risk is clustered within:

- models;
- suppliers;
- cloud providers;
- business units;
- regions;
- datasets;
- applications.

### 45. Dashboard Risk Aggregation

Aggregation allows management to view risk across multiple assets or organizational units.

Aggregation should not conceal material local risk.

### 46. Dashboard Risk Disaggregation

Disaggregation allows management to examine risk by relevant dimensions.

Possible dimensions include:

- AI system;
- model;
- business service;
- supplier;
- geography;
- environment;
- risk category.

### 47. Dashboard Risk Context

Risk values should be accompanied by sufficient context to prevent misleading interpretation.

Context may include:

- population;
- criticality;
- trend;
- threshold;
- uncertainty;
- recent incidents;
- material dependencies.

### 48. Dashboard Materiality

Dashboard design should prioritize information according to materiality.

Materiality may consider:

- business impact;
- security impact;
- customer impact;
- regulatory significance;
- financial exposure;
- operational criticality.

### 49. Dashboard Decision Usefulness

A dashboard should be evaluated according to whether it helps authorized decision-makers:

- identify material conditions;
- understand trends;
- prioritize action;
- challenge assumptions;
- allocate resources;
- oversee risk.

### 50. Core Principle

**An AI risk dashboard should transform controlled risk information into decision-useful management information without obscuring uncertainty, context, concentration, or measurement limitations. A dashboard is a governance interface, not a substitute for the underlying risk register, controls, evidence, or independent assurance. Dashboard status, thresholds, colors, scores, and classifications should have explicitly defined meanings and should not be presented as legal or regulatory classifications unless an applicable external requirement establishes them.**

Internal `AI-SM-DASH-*` identifiers remain knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations.
