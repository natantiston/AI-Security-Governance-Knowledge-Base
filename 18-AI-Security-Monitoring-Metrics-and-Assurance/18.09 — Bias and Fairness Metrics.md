# 18.09 — Bias and Fairness Metrics

## Part 1 — Bias and Fairness Metric Foundations, Measurement Principles, Governance and Interpretation

### 1. Definition of Bias and Fairness Metrics

Bias and fairness metrics are quantitative or structured measurements used to identify, evaluate, compare, and monitor patterns that may indicate unequal treatment, unequal outcomes, disparate impact, discriminatory behavior, or other fairness-related conditions associated with an AI system.

These metrics may examine:

* Input data
* Representation
* Model predictions
* Decision outcomes
* Error rates
* Access
* Treatment
* Performance across groups
* Human review
* Post-deployment outcomes

Bias and fairness metrics should be selected according to the AI system's purpose, population, decision context, and applicable fairness objectives.

A fairness metric does not by itself determine whether an AI system is fair.

**Fairness metric ≠ Fairness.**

---

### 2. Purpose of Bias and Fairness Metrics

The purpose of bias and fairness measurement is to provide evidence that can support the identification and assessment of potentially problematic differences across relevant populations or groups.

Fairness measurement may support:

* AI risk assessment
* Model validation
* Bias testing
* Governance decisions
* Control monitoring
* Remediation
* Impact assessment
* Regulatory analysis
* Management reporting
* Continuous monitoring

The appropriate metric depends on what fairness question the organization is trying to answer.

```text id="bf18d9-01"
AI System
    |
    v
Population / Groups
    |
    v
Fairness Question
    |
    v
Measurement Method
    |
    v
Fairness Metric
    |
    v
Contextual Interpretation
    |
    v
Governance Decision
```

A metric should support a defined governance question rather than exist solely because it is technically available.

---

### 3. Bias and Fairness Metric vs KPI

A bias or fairness metric measures a fairness-related characteristic or condition.

A KPI is a metric selected because it is particularly important for evaluating progress toward a defined organizational objective.

A fairness metric may become a KPI when the organization formally designates it as a key performance indicator.

For example:

* Fairness metric — difference in approval rates between defined groups
* KPI — percentage of material fairness findings remediated within the approved target

Therefore:

**Fairness metric ≠ KPI automatically.**

The distinction depends on governance designation and decision purpose.

---

### 4. Bias and Fairness Metric vs KRI

A fairness metric measures a fairness-related condition.

A KRI is specifically designed to indicate changes in risk exposure.

A fairness metric may support a KRI but is not automatically a KRI.

For example:

```text id="bf18d9-02"
Fairness Metric
      |
      v
Observed Group Difference
      |
      v
Risk Interpretation
      |
      v
KRI
      |
      v
Risk Escalation
```

The measured difference and the risk interpretation should remain separate.

**Fairness metric ≠ KRI automatically.**

---

### 5. Bias and Fairness Metric vs Control

A metric measures a condition.

A control is a mechanism designed to prevent, detect, correct, or manage a condition.

For example:

* Fairness metric — selection-rate difference
* Control — required pre-deployment fairness assessment
* Control — independent model validation
* Control — documented approval before deployment
* Control — post-deployment fairness monitoring

The metric can provide evidence about the operation or effectiveness of a control.

**Measurement ≠ Control.**

---

### 6. Bias and Fairness Metric vs Control Objective

A control objective defines what a control is intended to achieve.

A fairness metric measures a specific condition relevant to that objective.

For example:

**Control objective:** Material differences in AI decision outcomes are identified and assessed.

**Metric:** Difference in positive decision rates between defined groups.

The control objective expresses the desired governance condition; the metric provides measurement evidence.

**Control objective ≠ Metric.**

---

### 7. Bias and Fairness Metric vs Target

A metric represents an observed condition.

A target establishes a desired value or outcome.

For example:

> Observed fairness gap: 8 percentage points.

versus:

> Internal monitoring target: investigate fairness gaps exceeding the approved threshold.

The target is a governance decision layered onto the measurement.

**Metric value ≠ Target.**

---

### 8. Bias and Fairness Metric vs Threshold

A threshold defines a boundary used for interpretation or action.

A metric provides the measured result.

For example:

\[
Fairness\ Gap = |Rate_A - Rate_B|
\]

A governance framework may establish an internal threshold requiring investigation when the gap exceeds a specified value.

The threshold should be justified by the applicable fairness objective, methodology, context, and risk.

**Metric ≠ Threshold.**

An internally selected threshold should not automatically be described as a legal limit.

---

### 9. Bias and Fairness Measurement Scope

The scope of fairness measurement defines which AI systems, decisions, populations, time periods, and outcomes are included.

Scope may include:

* Model
* Model version
* AI application
* Decision type
* Business process
* Geographic region
* User population
* Affected population
* Decision period
* Deployment environment
* Supplier component

Scope should be explicitly documented.

A fairness conclusion is only meaningful within the population and decision context that was actually measured.

---

### 10. Bias and Fairness Measurement Domain

Fairness measurement can operate across several domains.

Possible domains include:

* Representation
* Access
* Treatment
* Selection
* Approval
* Rejection
* Ranking
* Classification
* Recommendation
* Error rates
* False positives
* False negatives
* Outcomes
* Resource allocation

Different domains can produce different fairness conclusions.

An AI system can perform similarly across one fairness dimension while showing differences across another.

---

### 11. Bias and Fairness Governance

Bias and fairness metrics should be governed through defined policies, methodologies, ownership, evidence requirements, and review processes.

Governance should address:

* Metric selection
* Population definition
* Group definition
* Methodology
* Data requirements
* Statistical assumptions
* Thresholds
* Reporting
* Escalation
* Remediation
* Assurance

Governance should also define how conflicting fairness measurements are interpreted.

**Metric availability ≠ Fairness governance.**

---

### 12. Bias and Fairness Metric Owner

Each material fairness metric should have an accountable owner.

The owner may be responsible for:

* Metric definition
* Methodology
* Data availability
* Calculation
* Reporting
* Interpretation
* Escalation
* Review

Ownership should be distinct from accountability for the underlying model where appropriate.

---

### 13. Bias and Fairness Data Owner

The data owner is responsible for the relevant source data used to calculate the fairness metric.

Responsibilities may include:

* Data quality
* Data access
* Data lineage
* Data classification
* Population completeness
* Data retention
* Data protection

The data owner does not automatically determine the fairness conclusion.

---

### 14. Bias and Fairness Metric Definition

Every material fairness metric should have an approved definition.

The definition should specify:

* Metric name
* Fairness concept
* Population
* Comparison groups
* Outcome
* Formula
* Data source
* Measurement period
* Inclusion criteria
* Exclusion criteria
* Interpretation
* Limitations

Definitions should be sufficiently precise to permit reproducible measurement.

---

### 15. Bias and Fairness Metric Formula

The formula used to calculate a fairness metric should be documented and controlled.

Examples include:

**Selection-rate difference**

\[
SRD = P(\hat{Y}=1|A) - P(\hat{Y}=1|B)
\]

**Selection-rate ratio**

\[
SRR =
\frac{P(\hat{Y}=1|A)}
{P(\hat{Y}=1|B)}
\]

**False-positive-rate difference**

\[
FPRD = FPR_A - FPR_B
\]

**False-negative-rate difference**

\[
FNDR = FNR_A - FNR_B
\]

The formula should always be interpreted within the decision context and population used.

---

### 16. Bias and Fairness Metric Numerator

Where a fairness metric uses a numerator, the numerator should be explicitly defined.

For example, for a selection rate:

\[
Selection\ Rate =
\frac{Number\ of\ Positive\ Decisions}
{Eligible\ Population}
\]

The numerator may represent:

* Approved applications
* Positive classifications
* Recommended candidates
* Accepted transactions
* Successful interventions

The numerator should correspond to the actual outcome being evaluated.

---

### 17. Bias and Fairness Metric Denominator

The denominator establishes the population against which the numerator is measured.

Examples include:

* Eligible applicants
* Evaluated candidates
* Customers considered
* Transactions assessed
* Cases reviewed

Incorrect denominators can create misleading fairness conclusions.

**Denominator integrity is therefore a fundamental fairness-measurement requirement.**

---

### 18. Bias and Fairness Measurement Population

The measurement population defines the observations included in the fairness analysis.

Population characteristics may include:

* Time period
* Geography
* Product
* Customer segment
* Decision type
* Model version
* Eligibility criteria

The population should reflect the decision context being evaluated.

---

### 19. Bias and Fairness Inclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria define which observations enter the measurement.

Criteria may include:

* Eligible decisions
* Completed predictions
* Valid outcomes
* Defined demographic populations
* Specific model versions

Inclusion criteria should be consistently applied across comparison groups.

---

### 20. Bias and Fairness Exclusion Criteria

Exclusion criteria define observations intentionally omitted from measurement.

Potential exclusions include:

* Test records
* Duplicates
* Incomplete records
* Invalid decisions
* Synthetic data
* Out-of-scope populations

Exclusions should be documented and justified.

**Exclusion ≠ Absence of bias.**

---

### 21. Bias and Fairness Measurement Period

The measurement period identifies the time during which observations are evaluated.

It may be:

* Pre-deployment
* Validation period
* Initial production period
* Monthly
* Quarterly
* Annual
* Event-driven

The period should be long enough to support meaningful analysis while remaining relevant to the current model and population.

---

### 22. Bias and Fairness Measurement Frequency

Measurement frequency should reflect:

* Model criticality
* Decision volume
* Population volatility
* Risk
* Potential impact
* Availability of outcome data

High-impact models may require more frequent monitoring.

Frequency should be documented and periodically reassessed.

---

### 23. Bias and Fairness Reporting Frequency

Reporting frequency may differ from calculation frequency.

For example:

```text id="bf18d9-03"
Continuous Calculation
        |
        v
Daily Monitoring
        |
        v
Weekly Management Review
        |
        v
Monthly Governance Reporting
        |
        v
Quarterly Executive Review
```

The appropriate cadence should depend on materiality and decision requirements.

---

### 24. Bias and Fairness Baseline

A baseline establishes a reference point for interpreting fairness measurements.

Possible baselines include:

* Pre-deployment validation
* Historical production period
* Approved model version
* Reference population
* Benchmark dataset

Baseline selection should be documented.

**Baseline ≠ Ground Truth automatically.**

---

### 25. Bias and Fairness Target

A target establishes an intended fairness condition within the organization's governance framework.

Targets should be based on:

* Applicable requirements
* Risk
* Decision context
* Model purpose
* Population characteristics
* Statistical considerations

Targets should not be selected solely because they are convenient to achieve.

---

### 26. Bias and Fairness Threshold

A threshold defines a boundary requiring investigation, escalation, or other governance action.

Thresholds may be based on:

* Statistical evidence
* Practical significance
* Model criticality
* Historical variation
* Risk
* Applicable requirements

An internal threshold is a governance mechanism unless an applicable external requirement establishes a specific criterion.

---

### 27. Bias and Fairness Tolerance

Tolerance represents the degree of variation that can occur before additional action is required.

Tolerance should consider:

* Measurement uncertainty
* Sample size
* Population variability
* Statistical methodology
* Decision impact

Tolerance should not be used to normalize material discriminatory outcomes.

---

### 28. Bias and Fairness Variance

Variance represents a difference between observed fairness measurement and an established reference, target, or expected condition.

Variance may be evaluated across:

* Groups
* Time
* Model versions
* Business units
* Geographic populations

Variance should be investigated in context.

---

### 29. Bias and Fairness Trend

Trend analysis examines fairness measurements over time.

For example:

```text id="bf18d9-04"
Fairness Measurement
      |
      v
Historical Values
      |
      v
Trend
      |
      +--> Improving
      +--> Stable
      +--> Deteriorating
      +--> Volatile
```

A trend can reveal persistent conditions that may not be visible in a single measurement.

---

### 30. Bias and Fairness Stability

Stability measures whether fairness results remain reasonably consistent over time or across comparable populations.

Instability may result from:

* Small populations
* Population changes
* Model changes
* Data changes
* Environmental changes
* Genuine behavioral differences

**Fairness stability ≠ Fairness.**

---

### 31. Bias and Fairness Metric Directionality

Directionality defines how changes in a metric should be interpreted.

For example:

* Higher disparity may represent deterioration.
* Lower disparity may represent improvement.

However, directionality depends on the metric.

For some fairness measurements, movement toward zero may be desirable; for others, interpretation depends on the underlying objective.

Directionality should therefore be documented rather than assumed.

---

### 32. Bias and Fairness Metric Interpretation

Interpretation should consider:

* Metric type
* Comparison groups
* Population
* Sample size
* Statistical uncertainty
* Decision context
* Model purpose
* Outcome significance
* Other fairness dimensions

A numerical result should not be interpreted independently of these factors.

---

### 33. Bias and Fairness Metric Context

Context provides the information necessary to understand a fairness measurement.

Context may include:

* Model purpose
* Decision process
* Population
* Eligibility rules
* Historical patterns
* Business process
* Data limitations
* Model version
* Policy changes
* External environment

The same numerical disparity may have different governance implications in different contexts.

---

### 34. Bias and Fairness Metric Comparability

Fairness metrics should only be compared where the underlying methodology and populations are sufficiently comparable.

Comparability may be affected by:

* Different populations
* Different model versions
* Different definitions
* Different outcomes
* Different measurement periods
* Different statistical methods

A lower fairness gap in one population does not automatically mean better fairness overall.

---

### 35. Bias and Fairness Metric Consistency

Fairness measurements should use consistent definitions and methodologies unless a controlled change has been approved.

Consistency should cover:

* Group definitions
* Outcome definitions
* Population rules
* Statistical methods
* Data preparation
* Reporting

Methodology changes should be documented.

---

### 36. Bias and Fairness Metric Methodology

Fairness methodology defines how fairness is evaluated.

Possible approaches include:

* Group fairness
* Outcome disparity analysis
* Error-rate comparison
* Selection-rate comparison
* Statistical testing
* Subgroup analysis
* Intersectional analysis
* Individual-level analysis where appropriate

No single fairness methodology is universally applicable to every AI system.

---

### 37. Bias and Fairness Methodology Change

Changes to fairness methodology may materially affect historical results.

Changes may include:

* New fairness metric
* New comparison groups
* New population
* New outcome definition
* New statistical test
* New sampling method

Methodology changes should be version-controlled and assessed for historical comparability.

---

### 38. Bias and Fairness Metric Restatement

Restatement may be required when a material measurement error is discovered.

Restatement should preserve:

* Original result
* Corrected result
* Reason
* Affected periods
* Methodology
* Approval
* Impact on previous decisions

Historical changes should remain traceable.

---

### 39. Bias and Fairness Data Quality

Fairness conclusions depend heavily on data quality.

Important dimensions include:

* Accuracy
* Completeness
* Timeliness
* Consistency
* Validity
* Representativeness
* Label quality

Poor data can create:

* Apparent bias
* Hidden bias
* False disparity
* Missed disparity

**Data quality ≠ Fairness.**

---

### 41. Bias and Fairness Data Provenance

Data provenance identifies the origin and processing history of fairness-measurement data.

Provenance may include:

* Source
* Dataset version
* Collection period
* Processing history
* Model version
* Transformation history
* Responsible owner

**Provenance ≠ Data Quality.**

---

### 42. Bias and Fairness Evidence

Evidence should support the fairness measurement and its interpretation.

Evidence may include:

* Source datasets
* Population definitions
* Group definitions
* Calculation records
* Model version
* Statistical analysis
* Validation
* Governance decisions
* Remediation records

Evidence should remain traceable to the relevant measurement.

---

### 43. Bias and Fairness Evidence Sufficiency

Evidence sufficiency depends on the conclusion being made.

Evidence that:

> Group A had a lower positive-decision rate

does not automatically establish:

> The model discriminates against Group A.

Additional analysis may be required to consider:

* Eligibility
* Relevant characteristics
* Data quality
* Historical conditions
* Model logic
* Error rates
* Decision process
* Alternative explanations

---

### 44. Bias and Fairness Evidence Integrity

Evidence should be protected against unauthorized alteration.

Controls may include:

* Access control
* Versioning
* Audit trails
* Integrity checks
* Immutable storage where appropriate

Evidence integrity supports trust in the measurement record.

**Evidence integrity ≠ Correctness of the underlying conclusion.**

---

### 45. Bias and Fairness Calculation Integrity

Calculation integrity means that the approved methodology has been applied correctly to the intended data.

Controls should protect:

* Formula
* Population
* Group definitions
* Denominators
* Statistical methods
* Processing logic
* Outputs

A technically correct calculation can still produce a misleading conclusion if the wrong population or fairness definition was selected.

---

### 46. Bias and Fairness Validation

Fairness measurements should be validated before being relied upon for material governance decisions.

Validation may examine:

* Formula
* Data
* Group definitions
* Population
* Sampling
* Statistical assumptions
* Calculation
* Interpretation

Higher-impact AI systems may require independent validation.

---

### 47. Bias and Fairness Assurance

Assurance evaluates whether fairness measurement and governance provide sufficient evidence for their intended purpose.

Assurance may include:

* Independent recalculation
* Model validation
* Data testing
* Statistical review
* Internal audit
* External assessment where appropriate

**Fairness metric assurance ≠ Complete AI fairness assurance.**

---

### 48. Bias and Fairness Decision Usefulness

A fairness metric should provide information useful for an actual governance decision.

Decision usefulness depends on:

* Relevance
* Timeliness
* Accuracy
* Context
* Interpretability
* Actionability
* Reliability

A highly sophisticated fairness metric may have limited governance value if decision-makers cannot determine what action it should support.

---

### 50. Core Principle

**Bias and fairness metrics provide structured evidence about differences in AI-system behavior, outcomes, treatment, representation, or performance across defined populations; they do not independently determine whether an AI system is fair, unbiased, discriminatory, compliant, or acceptable.**

The fundamental distinctions are:

**Fairness metric ≠ Fairness.**

**Bias metric ≠ Proof of bias.**

**Fairness metric ≠ KPI automatically.**

**Fairness metric ≠ KRI automatically.**

**Fairness metric ≠ Control.**

**Fairness metric ≠ Control objective.**

**Metric ≠ Target.**

**Metric ≠ Threshold.**

**Statistical difference ≠ Discrimination automatically.**

**Group disparity ≠ Causation automatically.**

**Correlation ≠ Causation.**

**Statistical significance ≠ Practical significance.**

**Data quality ≠ Fairness.**

**Data provenance ≠ Data quality.**

**Evidence ≠ Proof of the underlying conclusion.**

**Fairness measurement ≠ Fairness assurance.**

**Fairness assurance ≠ Compliance.**

**Target achievement ≠ Absence of bias.**

**Threshold compliance ≠ Fairness automatically.**

**A single fairness metric ≠ Complete fairness assessment.**

**Internal fairness threshold ≠ Legal threshold automatically.**

**Fairness measurement should therefore be governed as a contextual, evidence-based measurement capability that considers population definition, group definition, statistical methodology, data quality, model purpose, decision context, uncertainty, multiple fairness dimensions, and the limitations of the selected measurement approach.**
