# 16.20 — Enterprise AI Third-Party Risk Management Framework

## Part 1 — Foundations, Scope, Governance, Risk Architecture, Third-Party Classification and Enterprise Dependency Management

### 1. Enterprise AI Third-Party Risk Management

Enterprise AI Third-Party Risk Management (AI-TPRM) is the organizational framework used to identify, assess, govern, monitor, and manage risks arising from external parties that provide, operate, develop, host, maintain, support, or otherwise materially enable AI capabilities.

The framework should address not only traditional vendors but also:

* AI model providers;
* foundation-model providers;
* AI SaaS providers;
* cloud AI providers;
* API providers;
* model marketplaces;
* open-source maintainers;
* software suppliers;
* dataset providers;
* managed service providers;
* AI implementation partners;
* technology subcontractors;
* fourth parties.

### 2. AI Third-Party Risk Objective

The objective of enterprise AI-TPRM is to ensure that third-party dependencies do not introduce unmanaged levels of:

* cybersecurity risk;
* privacy risk;
* data risk;
* model risk;
* operational risk;
* regulatory risk;
* contractual risk;
* resilience risk;
* concentration risk;
* supply-chain risk.

### 3. Third-Party AI Dependency

A third-party AI dependency exists when an external organization provides a capability, component, service, technology, data source, infrastructure layer, or operational function that materially supports an AI system.

### 4. Direct AI Provider

A direct AI provider has an explicit contractual or operational relationship with the organization.

Examples include:

* an AI SaaS provider;
* an external model API provider;
* a cloud AI service;
* an AI managed service provider.

### 5. Indirect AI Dependency

An indirect dependency exists when an AI capability depends on an external party through another supplier.

```text
Organization
     |
     v
Primary AI Vendor
     |
     v
Subprocessor / Fourth Party
     |
     v
Model / Cloud / Dataset / Infrastructure Provider
```

Indirect dependencies should not automatically be considered outside the organization's risk-management scope.

### 6. Fourth-Party Risk

Fourth-party risk arises from dependencies within a third party's own supply chain that can materially affect the organization's AI service.

Potential fourth parties include:

* cloud providers;
* model providers;
* subprocessors;
* software suppliers;
* dataset providers;
* infrastructure operators.

### 7. AI Supply-Chain Relationship

AI-TPRM should consider relationships among:

* vendors;
* models;
* datasets;
* software;
* infrastructure;
* APIs;
* identities;
* security services.

A vendor may appear independent at the contractual level while remaining highly dependent at the technical level.

### 8. Third-Party Risk Boundary

The organization should define the boundary of its AI-TPRM program.

The boundary should consider:

* business use;
* technical dependency;
* data processing;
* service criticality;
* regulatory exposure;
* security exposure.

### 9. Enterprise AI Vendor Inventory

The organization should maintain an inventory of material AI-related third parties.

The inventory may include:

* vendor;
* service;
* business owner;
* technology owner;
* AI capability;
* data processed;
* model dependency;
* hosting environment;
* criticality;
* contract status.

### 10. AI Third-Party Identification

Third parties should be identified through multiple sources, including:

* procurement;
* contracts;
* architecture;
* application inventories;
* cloud inventories;
* API inventories;
* security tooling;
* business assessments.

### 11. Shadow AI Vendor Dependency

AI-TPRM should address unauthorized or unmanaged third-party AI services used by employees, developers, or business functions.

Examples include:

* unapproved AI SaaS;
* personal AI accounts;
* unapproved model APIs;
* external transcription services;
* browser-based AI tools.

### 12. Shadow AI Discovery

Organizations should use appropriate mechanisms to identify material unauthorized AI dependencies.

Potential sources include:

* network telemetry;
* DNS;
* proxy logs;
* CASB;
* SaaS discovery;
* expense records;
* procurement records;
* application inventories.

### 13. Vendor Classification

Third parties should be classified according to their role and dependency characteristics.

Possible classifications include:

* AI model provider;
* AI SaaS provider;
* cloud provider;
* API provider;
* dataset provider;
* software supplier;
* marketplace;
* managed service provider.

### 14. Risk Classification

AI third parties should be assigned an internal risk classification based on relevant risk factors.

Classification should consider:

* criticality;
* data sensitivity;
* AI capability;
* autonomy;
* security exposure;
* regulatory relevance;
* concentration;
* substitutability.

### 15. Risk Tiering

Organizations may establish internal risk tiers such as:

* standard;
* elevated;
* high;
* critical.

These are internal governance classifications and should not be confused with regulatory classifications unless an applicable law or regulation explicitly uses the same terminology.

### 16. Risk Tier Criteria

Risk-tier criteria should be documented and consistently applied.

### 17. Critical AI Third Party

A third party may be internally classified as critical when failure, compromise, loss of service, or material change could significantly affect an important business service or risk exposure.

### 18. Criticality Assessment

Criticality should consider:

* business dependency;
* recovery requirements;
* data sensitivity;
* operational dependency;
* security impact;
* substitutability.

### 19. Business Service Dependency

Third-party AI services should be mapped to the business services they support.

### 20. Business Impact

Assessment should consider the consequences of:

* service outage;
* data compromise;
* model failure;
* security incident;
* provider withdrawal;
* regulatory restriction.

### 21. AI Use-Case Context

Third-party risk should be assessed in the context of the actual AI use case.

The same provider may represent different levels of risk for different applications.

### 22. Use-Case Criticality

Criticality should consider the consequences of the AI system's failure or misuse.

### 23. AI Autonomy

Third-party risk should consider whether the AI capability:

* provides information;
* recommends decisions;
* generates content;
* initiates workflows;
* executes actions;
* controls external systems.

### 24. Human Oversight

The organization should understand how much human oversight exists over third-party AI capabilities.

### 25. Autonomous Third-Party Capability

Greater autonomy may increase the importance of:

* authorization;
* monitoring;
* containment;
* auditability;
* incident response.

### 26. Data Dependency

Third-party assessment should identify what data is processed by the service.

### 27. Data Categories

Relevant data categories may include:

* public information;
* internal information;
* confidential information;
* personal data;
* sensitive personal data;
* regulated information;
* credentials;
* proprietary information.

### 28. Data Classification

The organization should apply its established data-classification requirements to third-party AI processing.

### 29. Data Flow Mapping

Material data flows to third parties should be documented.

```text
Business Application
        |
        v
AI Application
        |
```

### 30. Data Minimization

Third-party AI processing should use only the data necessary for the approved purpose, subject to applicable requirements.

### 31. Data Authorization

The organization should determine whether the data sent to the third party is authorized for that processing activity.

### 32. Data Ownership

Relevant ownership and custodianship responsibilities should be identified.

### 33. Data Retention

The organization should understand how long third parties retain material data.

### 34. Data Deletion

Deletion capabilities should be evaluated where relevant to the use case, contractual requirements, or applicable law.

### 35. Data Return

Where appropriate, contracts and operating processes should address return or export of organizational data.

### 36. Secondary Data Use

Third-party use of organizational data for purposes beyond the approved service should be identified and governed.

### 37. AI Training Use

The organization should determine whether submitted data may be used for:

* model training;
* fine-tuning;
* evaluation;
* service improvement;
* other secondary purposes.

### 38. Training-Use Governance

Where training or secondary use is not authorized, appropriate contractual and technical controls should be established.

### 39. Data Residency

The organization should understand where relevant AI processing occurs.

### 40. Cross-Border Processing

Cross-border processing should be assessed according to applicable legal, contractual, privacy, security, and organizational requirements.

### 41. Subprocessor Geography

Material subprocessor locations should be identified where relevant.

### 42. Data Transfer Controls

Where applicable, appropriate mechanisms should govern international data transfers.

### 43. Encryption

Third-party AI services should be assessed for appropriate protection of data:

* in transit;
* at rest;
* during relevant processing stages where applicable.

### 44. Key Management

Where organizational requirements apply, the organization should understand who controls relevant encryption keys.

### 45. Provider Personnel Access

Third-party personnel access to organizational data or systems should be assessed.

### 46. Privileged Provider Access

Privileged provider access should be:

* authorized;
* limited;
* monitored;
* reviewed.

### 47. Access Governance

Third-party access should follow appropriate:

* identity;
* authentication;
* authorization;
* least-privilege;
* access-review controls.

### 48. Service Accounts

Service accounts used for third-party AI integrations should be controlled and monitored.

### 49. Credential Management

Third-party credentials should be managed through approved security processes.

### 50. Secret Management

API keys, tokens, certificates, and other secrets should not be embedded unnecessarily in source code, prompts, notebooks, or configuration.

### 51. Authentication

Third-party AI integrations should use appropriate authentication mechanisms.

### 52. Multi-Factor Authentication

Administrative access should use stronger authentication mechanisms where appropriate.

### 53. Single Sign-On

Where supported and appropriate, enterprise identity controls may be integrated with third-party AI services.

### 54. Role-Based Access

Access should be based on defined roles and business requirements.

### 55. Privileged Access Management

Material privileged third-party access should be governed through appropriate privileged-access controls.

### 56. Segregation of Duties

Conflicting third-party administrative or operational responsibilities should be appropriately separated where required.

### 57. Access Review

Third-party access should be reviewed periodically according to risk.

### 58. Access Revocation

Access should be revoked when:

* no longer required;
* employment or role changes;
* contracts terminate;
* authorization expires.

### 59. API Dependency

AI-TPRM should identify material external APIs used by AI systems.

### 60. API Authentication

API integrations should use appropriate authentication and credential-management controls.

### 61. API Authorization

API permissions should be limited to required functions.

### 62. API Rate Limiting

Rate limits and quotas should be considered where availability, cost, or abuse risk is material.

### 63. API Availability

Critical API dependencies should have appropriate availability and fallback considerations.

### 64. API Change Risk

Material API changes should be governed because they can affect:

* security;
* functionality;
* compatibility;
* availability.

### 65. API Versioning

Critical API dependencies should identify relevant versions and migration requirements.

### 66. API Logging

Material API activity should be logged according to applicable monitoring requirements.

### 67. Cloud Dependency

Third-party AI services may depend on cloud infrastructure that introduces additional concentration or resilience risk.

### 68. Cloud Shared Responsibility

The organization should understand which security responsibilities remain with the provider and which remain with the organization.

### 69. Cloud Dependency Mapping

Material cloud dependencies should be mapped through the AI service architecture.

* performance;

### 209. AI-TPRM Governance Principle

Enterprise AI-TPRM should provide a risk-proportionate framework that connects third-party identification, classification, due diligence, contractual governance, technical assurance, continuous monitoring, concentration management, resilience, and exit capability.

Third-party risk should be assessed in the context of the actual AI use case and dependency architecture rather than solely on the vendor's corporate profile or generic security documentation.

### 210. Enterprise AI Third-Party Risk Management Decision Principle

An enterprise AI third-party risk decision should be based on a sufficiently complete understanding of:

* **who** provides the capability;
* **what** AI capability is being provided;
* **which data** is processed;
* **which models and technologies** are involved;
* **which fourth parties** support the service;
* **which business services** depend on it;
* **which security and privacy controls** apply;
* **which contractual protections** exist;
* **which concentration risks** are created;
* **how resilient** the dependency is;
* **how practical exit or substitution would be**;
* **what evidence** supports the assessment;
* **who owns the residual risk**.

Internal `AI-ETPRM-*` identifiers used in this knowledge base are organizational taxonomy identifiers. They are not statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, or mandatory identifiers established by external law or standards.
