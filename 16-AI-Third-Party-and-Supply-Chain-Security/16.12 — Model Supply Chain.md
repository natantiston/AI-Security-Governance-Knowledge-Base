# 16.12 — Model Supply Chain

## Part 1 — Foundations, Model Supply-Chain Dependency, Provenance, Integrity, Components, Threats and Governance

### 1. AI Model Supply-Chain Risk

AI model supply-chain risk is the risk that the models, model artifacts, supporting components, data, tooling, and external dependencies used to develop or operate an AI system may be compromised, manipulated, misrepresented, unavailable, vulnerable, or otherwise unsuitable.

Model supply-chain risk can arise before a model enters the organization and continue throughout:

* acquisition;
* development;
* fine-tuning;
* evaluation;
* deployment;
* operation;
* modification;
* retirement.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-001**

---

### 2. Objective of AI Model Supply-Chain Governance

The objective of model supply-chain governance is to establish reasonable confidence that models used by the organization are:

* identifiable;
* attributable;
* traceable;
* appropriately verified;
* protected against unauthorized modification;
* evaluated for relevant risks;
* monitored throughout their lifecycle.

Model supply-chain governance should connect technical controls with AI governance, cybersecurity, risk management, privacy, legal, procurement, and operational resilience.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-002**

---

### 3. AI Model Supply Chain

An AI model supply chain is the collection of parties, processes, artifacts, infrastructure, data, and technologies through which an AI model is:

```text id="m1sc01"
Created
   |
Trained
   |
Fine-Tuned
   |
Evaluated
   |
Packaged
   |
Distributed
   |
Acquired
   |
Deployed
   |
Updated
   |
Operated
   |
Retired
```

The chain may involve multiple organizations and technical environments.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-003**

---

### 4. Model Supply-Chain Dependency

A model supply-chain dependency exists when an AI system relies on an externally or internally produced model or model-related component.

Dependencies may include:

* foundation models;
* pretrained models;
* fine-tuned models;
* embedding models;
* classifiers;
* adapters;
* tokenizers;
* model runtimes;
* model-serving platforms.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-004**

---

### 5. Direct Model Dependency

A direct model dependency is a model explicitly incorporated into an AI system.

Examples include:

* a pretrained language model;
* an image-classification model;
* an embedding model;
* a speech-recognition model.

Direct dependencies should be identified in the AI system inventory.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-005**

---

### 6. Transitive Model Dependency

A transitive model dependency exists when an AI system indirectly relies on another model through an intermediate component or service.

For example:

```text id="m1sc02"
AI Application
      |
AI Framework
      |
Model Router
      |
Embedding Service
      |
Third-Party Model
```

The organization may not directly select every underlying model, but the dependency can still create risk.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-006**

---

### 7. Model Development Dependency

Model development may depend on:

* training frameworks;
* pretrained models;
* datasets;
* tokenizers;
* optimization libraries;
* evaluation tools;
* external APIs.

These dependencies should be considered when assessing model supply-chain risk.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-007**

---

### 8. Model Training Dependency

Training may depend on externally obtained:

* datasets;
* pretrained weights;
* checkpoints;
* code;
* configurations;
* hyperparameter definitions.

Compromise of any material training dependency can affect the resulting model.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-008**

---

### 9. Model Fine-Tuning Dependency

Fine-tuning can create dependencies on:

* base-model weights;
* fine-tuning datasets;
* training code;
* adapters;
* configuration;
* optimization libraries.

The organization should maintain sufficient lineage to understand how the final model was derived.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-009**

---

### 10. Model Deployment Dependency

Model deployment may depend on:

* model-serving infrastructure;
* inference engines;
* containers;
* runtime libraries;
* accelerators;
* model registries;
* external services.

Supply-chain governance should cover dependencies that can materially affect model operation.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-010**

---

### 11. Model Artifact

A model artifact is a representation of a model that can be stored, transferred, evaluated, or deployed.

Examples include:

* model weights;
* checkpoint files;
* serialized models;
* model packages;
* containerized models.

The artifact should be distinguished from the abstract model and from the system that executes it.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-011**

---

### 12. Model Weights

Model weights represent learned parameters used by a model during inference or further training.

Weights should be treated as security-sensitive artifacts when their compromise could alter:

* model behavior;
* outputs;
* security properties;
* intellectual property;
* system performance.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-012**

---

### 13. Model Checkpoint

A model checkpoint is a stored model state, potentially representing an intermediate or completed training stage.

Checkpoints should be governed according to their sensitivity and potential deployment impact.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-013**

---

### 14. Model Serialization

Model serialization converts model state or structure into a stored representation.

Serialization formats can introduce security considerations because some formats may support executable or otherwise unsafe behavior when processed improperly.

Model loading mechanisms should therefore be treated as part of the supply-chain attack surface.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-014**

---

### 15. Model Package

A model package may contain more than model weights.

It can include:

* configuration;
* tokenizer;
* metadata;
* custom code;
* preprocessing logic;
* postprocessing logic;
* dependencies.

The presence of additional executable or configurable components increases the need for package inspection.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-015**

---

### 16. Model Registry

A model registry is a controlled repository for storing and managing model artifacts and associated metadata.

A registry may maintain:

* model identity;
* versions;
* ownership;
* status;
* provenance;
* approvals;
* evaluation results.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-016**

---

### 17. Trusted Model Registry

A trusted model registry should provide appropriate controls for:

* authentication;
* authorization;
* version management;
* artifact integrity;
* audit logging;
* controlled promotion.

Trust in the registry does not automatically establish trust in every model stored within it.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-017**

---

### 18. Model Repository

A model repository is a location from which model artifacts can be obtained.

Repositories may be:

* internal;
* vendor-operated;
* public;
* community-operated;
* private.

Repository availability should not automatically be interpreted as model assurance.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-018**

---

### 19. Model Publisher

A model publisher is the organization or individual responsible for publishing a model artifact or making it available through a distribution channel.

Publisher identity should be established where practical.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-019**

---

### 20. Model Maintainer

A model maintainer is responsible for maintaining a model project or associated repository.

Maintainer assessment may consider:

* identity;
* activity;
* security responsiveness;
* release practices;
* ownership;
* continuity.

Maintainer activity does not itself prove model security.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-020**

---

### 21. Model Provenance

Model provenance is information describing where a model came from and how it was created, modified, packaged, and distributed.

Provenance may include:

* original publisher;
* base model;
* training source;
* fine-tuning;
* transformations;
* release;
* distribution channel.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-021**

---

### 22. Model Provenance Chain

A provenance chain records the sequence of transformations through which a model artifact passed.

```text id="m1sc03"
Base Model
    |
Fine-Tuning
    |
Adapter
    |
Quantization
    |
Conversion
    |
Packaging
    |
Distribution
    |
Deployment
```

The chain should be maintained where necessary to support assurance and incident investigation.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-022**

---

### 23. Model Lineage

Model lineage describes the relationship between a model and its predecessor artifacts, training processes, datasets, transformations, and deployment versions.

Lineage supports:

* reproducibility;
* impact analysis;
* rollback;
* incident response;
* assurance.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-023**

---

### 24. Model Parentage

Model parentage identifies the model or models from which another model was derived.

For example:

```text id="m1sc04"
Foundation Model
       |
       +---- Fine-Tuned Model A
       |
       +---- Fine-Tuned Model B
                  |
                  +---- Domain Model C
```

Parentage is important when a vulnerability or integrity issue affects a shared base model.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-024**

---

### 25. Model Transformation

Model transformations can include:

* fine-tuning;
* pruning;
* quantization;
* distillation;
* conversion;
* merging;
* adapter application.

Each transformation can alter security, performance, or behavior.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-025**

---

### 26. Model Integrity

Model integrity is the assurance that a model artifact has not been altered without authorization.

Integrity mechanisms may include:

* hashes;
* digital signatures;
* immutable storage;
* controlled registries;
* provenance records.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-026**

---

### 27. Model Hash

A model hash is a cryptographic digest used to identify a particular artifact state.

Hash verification can establish whether the inspected artifact matches the expected artifact.

A matching hash does not establish that the model itself is safe, unbiased, vulnerability-free, or appropriate for the intended use.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-027**

---

### 28. Model Signature

A digital signature can provide evidence regarding:

* artifact integrity;
* signing identity;
* authorization of publication.

Signature verification does not by itself establish model safety or absence of malicious behavior.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-028**

---

### 29. Model Authenticity

Model authenticity concerns whether the artifact originates from the expected publisher or source.

Authenticity should be distinguished from:

* integrity;
* security;
* quality;
* suitability.

An authentic model can still contain vulnerabilities or undesirable behavior.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-029**

---

### 30. Model Tampering

Model tampering is unauthorized modification of a model artifact.

Potential objectives include:

* changing model behavior;
* introducing a backdoor;
* degrading performance;
* altering safety behavior;
* compromising intellectual property.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-030**

---

### 31. Model Supply-Chain Compromise

A model supply-chain compromise occurs when an attacker compromises a point in the model lifecycle or distribution chain.

Potential targets include:

* publisher accounts;
* repositories;
* model registries;
* build systems;
* model-conversion pipelines;
* distribution channels.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-031**

---

### 32. Model Backdoor Risk

A model backdoor is behavior intentionally embedded so that particular inputs, conditions, or triggers produce attacker-controlled or otherwise unintended behavior.

Backdoors may survive conventional functional testing.

Testing should therefore consider targeted security evaluation where justified.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-032**

---

### 33. Model Poisoning Risk

Model poisoning involves manipulating training or adaptation inputs so that the resulting model exhibits unintended behavior.

Potential sources include:

* training datasets;
* fine-tuning datasets;
* externally supplied model updates;
* compromised training pipelines.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-033**

---

### 34. Model Weight Poisoning

Model weights themselves may be manipulated before distribution or deployment.

Weight-level compromise may be difficult to identify through ordinary application testing.

Integrity and provenance controls should therefore complement behavioral evaluation.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-034**

---

### 35. Model Training Pipeline Compromise

The training pipeline can become a supply-chain attack surface.

Potential targets include:

* source code;
* datasets;
* training infrastructure;
* credentials;
* dependencies;
* checkpoints;
* model export processes.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-035**

---

### 36. Model Evaluation Pipeline Compromise

Evaluation infrastructure may also be targeted.

An attacker could attempt to manipulate:

* evaluation datasets;
* evaluation code;
* scoring;
* thresholds;
* reports.

This could cause an unsafe model to appear acceptable.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-036**

---

### 37. Model Distribution Risk

Model distribution creates risks involving:

* unauthorized modification;
* malicious substitution;
* counterfeit artifacts;
* incomplete provenance;
* compromised repositories.

Distribution channels should therefore be included in supply-chain risk assessments.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-037**

---

### 38. Model Mirror Risk

A mirror is an alternate location distributing an existing model artifact.

Mirrors can improve availability but may introduce uncertainty concerning:

* authenticity;
* synchronization;
* integrity;
* provenance.

Artifacts obtained from mirrors should be validated against trusted references where practical.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-038**

---

### 39. Model Substitution Risk

Model substitution occurs when an expected model is replaced with another model.

Substitution may be:

* malicious;
* accidental;
* caused by automatic updates;
* caused by repository changes;
* caused by configuration drift.

Model identity should therefore be verified before deployment.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-039**

---

### 40. Model Version

A model version identifies a particular release or state of a model.

Version governance should allow the organization to determine:

* which version is approved;
* which version is deployed;
* which version was evaluated;
* which version was previously deployed.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-040**

---

### 41. Model Version Pinning

Critical AI systems should pin model versions where appropriate.

Version pinning reduces unexpected behavioral changes caused by:

* automatic upgrades;
* provider changes;
* repository updates;
* configuration changes.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-041**

---

### 42. Model Release Governance

Model releases should pass through controlled processes appropriate to risk.

Release governance may include:

* provenance verification;
* integrity verification;
* security testing;
* behavioral evaluation;
* approval;
* documentation.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-042**

---

### 43. Model Change Management

Material model changes should undergo impact assessment.

Changes may include:

* new weights;
* fine-tuning;
* quantization;
* conversion;
* architecture changes;
* tokenizer changes;
* configuration changes.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-043**

---

### 44. Model Behavioral Baseline

A behavioral baseline establishes expected model behavior under defined evaluation conditions.

It may include:

* accuracy;
* robustness;
* refusal behavior;
* latency;
* resource usage;
* safety performance;
* application-specific metrics.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-044**

---

### 45. Model Regression

Model regression occurs when a change causes deterioration in previously acceptable behavior.

Regression may affect:

* accuracy;
* robustness;
* safety;
* security;
* performance;
* compatibility.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-045**

---

### 46. Model Evaluation Evidence

Model evaluation evidence should identify:

* model version;
* evaluation methodology;
* datasets;
* metrics;
* results;
* limitations;
* evaluation date.

Evaluation evidence should correspond to the model actually deployed.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-046**

---

### 47. Model Benchmark Limitations

Benchmark performance does not necessarily establish suitability for a specific enterprise use case.

Benchmark limitations may arise from:

* dataset differences;
* distribution shifts;
* evaluation methodology;
* benchmark contamination;
* differences between benchmark tasks and production tasks.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-047**

---

### 48. Model Security Evaluation

Security evaluation should assess threats relevant to the model and its deployment context.

Depending on risk, evaluation may consider:

* adversarial inputs;
* prompt injection;
* model extraction;
* data leakage;
* backdoors;
* malicious behavior;
* unsafe tool interaction.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-048**

---

### 49. AI Model Supply-Chain Governance Principle

Model supply-chain governance should establish traceability from model origin through transformation, distribution, deployment, change, and retirement.

The organization should be able to determine:

```text id="m1sc05"
Where did the model come from?
          |
How was it transformed?
          |
Who approved it?
          |
Which version was evaluated?
          |
Which version was deployed?
          |
What dependencies support it?
          |
What changed?
          |
Can it be recovered or replaced?
```

**Control ID: AI-MSC-049**

---

### 50. AI Model Supply-Chain Trust Principle

Model supply-chain trust should be based on multiple independent forms of evidence rather than a single trust signal.

The organization should distinguish:

* model provenance from model integrity;
* integrity from authenticity;
* authenticity from security;
* publisher reputation from verified evidence;
* model popularity from assurance;
* benchmark performance from production suitability;
* version identity from behavioral stability;
* model artifact security from application security;
* documented lineage from complete lineage;
* model availability from operational resilience.

Internal `AI-MSC-*` identifiers are organizational governance taxonomy identifiers. They are not statutory or regulatory provisions unless a separate legal, regulatory, contractual, or authoritative mapping establishes that relationship.

**Core Principles**

1. A model should be identifiable before it is trusted.
2. Model provenance should be distinguished from model integrity.
3. Model integrity should be distinguished from model authenticity.
4. Authenticity does not establish security.
5. A valid hash does not establish that a model is safe.
6. A valid signature does not establish absence of vulnerabilities.
7. Publisher reputation should not replace technical verification.
8. Model popularity should not be treated as assurance.
9. Model lineage should extend through material transformations.
10. Base-model dependencies should be visible.
11. Fine-tuning can create new supply-chain dependencies.
12. Quantization and conversion can change model behavior.
13. Model versions should be traceable to evaluation evidence.
14. Model changes should be governed according to risk.
15. Behavioral evaluation should complement integrity controls.
16. Evaluation evidence should correspond to the deployed version.
17. Model supply-chain security extends beyond model weights.
18. Distribution channels form part of the trust boundary.
19. Model supply-chain governance should support recovery and replacement.
20. Internal control identifiers should not be represented as legal requirements without an explicit authoritative mapping.

## Part 2 — Model Supply-Chain Due Diligence, Security Controls, Model Assurance, Data Governance and Supply-Chain Security

### 51. Model Supply-Chain Due Diligence

Material AI models should undergo due diligence before being approved for organizational use.

Due diligence should consider:

* model identity;
* publisher;
* provenance;
* model lineage;
* version;
* licensing;
* evaluation evidence;
* security history;
* dependencies;
* maintenance;
* distribution channel;
* intended use.

The depth of due diligence should be proportionate to model criticality and risk.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-051**

---

### 52. Model Publisher Assessment

The organization should assess the publisher of a material model.

Assessment may consider:

* publisher identity;
* ownership;
* development practices;
* security practices;
* release process;
* vulnerability response;
* documentation;
* maintenance capability.

Publisher reputation should not substitute for technical evidence.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-052**

---

### 53. Model Maintainer Assessment

Material model projects should be assessed for maintenance continuity.

Relevant indicators may include:

* release activity;
* security response;
* issue management;
* documentation;
* contributor activity;
* ownership continuity.

Active maintenance does not by itself establish model security.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-053**

---

### 54. Model Repository Assessment

Repositories used to obtain material models should be assessed for:

* ownership;
* authenticity;
* access controls;
* release integrity;
* version management;
* security practices;
* auditability.

A trusted repository does not automatically make every model stored there trustworthy.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-054**

---

### 55. Model Source Verification

The organization should verify that a model originates from an approved or appropriately trusted source.

Verification may include:

* repository identity;
* publisher identity;
* model identifier;
* release information;
* hashes;
* signatures;
* provenance metadata.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-055**

---

### 56. Model Acquisition Governance

Model acquisition should occur through controlled channels.

Approved channels may include:

* enterprise model registries;
* approved repositories;
* authorized suppliers;
* verified distribution platforms.

Uncontrolled downloads should receive appropriate additional scrutiny.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-056**

---

### 57. Model Artifact Quarantine

Untrusted or newly acquired model artifacts should be quarantined before production use where appropriate.

Quarantine activities may include:

* malware analysis;
* serialization inspection;
* hash verification;
* signature verification;
* provenance review;
* vulnerability scanning;
* behavioral testing.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-057**

---

### 58. Model Artifact Promotion

Model artifacts should move through controlled promotion stages.

```text id="msc2a1"

    |

    |

    |

    |

Promotion controls should be proportionate to model criticality.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-058**

---

### 59. Model Hash Verification

Model artifacts should be checked against trusted reference hashes where available.

Hash verification establishes artifact consistency with the expected reference.

It does not establish:

* absence of malicious behavior;
* absence of vulnerabilities;
* suitability;
* safety;
* legal authorization.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-059**

---

### 60. Model Signature Verification

Where model artifacts are digitally signed, signatures should be verified according to the organization's trust model.

Verification should consider:

* signing identity;
* certificate or key status;
* signature validity;
* artifact association;
* trust-anchor configuration.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-060**

---

### 61. Trusted Model Registry

Approved model artifacts should be stored in a controlled registry where appropriate.

Registry controls may include:

* authentication;
* authorization;
* versioning;
* immutable storage;
* audit logging;
* provenance metadata;
* promotion controls.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-061**

---

### 62. Model Artifact Immutability

Production-approved model artifacts should be protected against unauthorized modification.

Controls may include:

* immutable storage;
* write restrictions;
* access separation;
* signed artifacts;
* controlled promotion.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-062**

Critical AI systems should pin approved model versions where appropriate.

Version pinning reduces unexpected changes caused by:

* automatic updates;

**Control ID: AI-MSC-063**

Material models should undergo security evaluation appropriate to their intended use.

* adversarial inputs;

**Control ID: AI-MSC-069**

High-risk models may require adversarial or red-team testing.

Testing may examine:

* jailbreak behavior;
* adversarial inputs;
* backdoors;
* data leakage;
* unsafe behavior;
* security-control bypass.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-070**

---

### 71. Model Safety Evaluation

Where a model is used in safety-relevant or high-impact contexts, safety evaluation should be appropriate to the intended application.

The evaluation should consider whether model changes can create unacceptable downstream effects.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-071**

---

### 72. Model Evaluation Environment

Model testing should occur in controlled environments appropriate to model sensitivity.

Testing environments should be separated from production where practical.

Sensitive production data should not be introduced into testing unless appropriately authorized and protected.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-072**

---

### 73. Model Evaluation Dataset

Evaluation datasets should be governed according to their sensitivity and purpose.

Governance may address:

* provenance;
* quality;
* representativeness;
* privacy;
* access;
* versioning;
* contamination.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-073**

---

### 74. Model Evaluation Dataset Integrity

Evaluation datasets should be protected from unauthorized modification.

A compromised evaluation dataset can produce misleading model assurance results.

Controls may include:

* access control;
* hashes;
* immutable storage;
* versioning;
* change monitoring.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-074**

---

### 75. Model Benchmark Evidence

Benchmark results may provide useful evidence regarding model capability.

However, benchmarks should be interpreted according to:

* methodology;
* dataset;
* model version;
* evaluation conditions;
* limitations.

Benchmark performance should not automatically be treated as production assurance.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-075**

---

### 76. Model Benchmark Contamination

Organizations should consider whether evaluation results may be affected by benchmark contamination.

Contamination can make benchmark performance appear stronger than the model's generalization capability.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-076**

---

### 77. Model Safety Configuration

Model safety configuration should be documented where it materially affects model behavior.

Relevant settings may include:

* safety filters;
* system instructions;
* output controls;
* policy configurations;
* moderation mechanisms.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-077**

---

### 78. Model Content Filtering

Where content filtering is part of the AI architecture, filtering mechanisms should be assessed separately from the underlying model.

A model may be safe under one configuration and behave differently under another.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-078**

---

### 79. Model Prompt Security

Where the model is exposed through prompts, prompt-security controls should protect against:

* prompt injection;
* instruction manipulation;
* sensitive-data disclosure;
* unauthorized behavior.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-079**

### 80. Model Prompt-Injection Protection

AI systems should assess prompt-injection risks where models process untrusted content.

Controls may include:

* input isolation;
* instruction hierarchy;
* content classification;
* tool restrictions;
* output validation;
* privilege separation.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-080**

---

### 81. Model Tool Authorization

Where a model can invoke external tools, tool permissions should be explicitly governed.

Authorization should consider:

* tool identity;
* permitted operations;
* data access;
* user context;
* model context;
* transaction sensitivity.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-081**

---

### 82. Model Agent Security

Models used within autonomous or agentic systems should be assessed for risks arising from:

* tool access;
* memory;
* retrieval;
* external communication;
* code execution;
* persistent actions.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-082**

### 83. Model High-Impact Action Approval

Model-initiated high-impact actions should require appropriate authorization.

* financial transactions;
* privileged configuration changes;
* account actions;

Model-driven processes should operate with the minimum privileges necessary.

Model access should be restricted according to:

* function;

* application;
* environment;
* data sensitivity.

* authentication;

```

The cycle should operate throughout the model lifecycle.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-393**

### 394. Model Strategic Resilience

Strategic model resilience requires more than maintaining a list of alternative models.

The organization should have reasonable confidence that an alternative can actually be:

* acquired;
* verified;
* integrated;
* evaluated;
* deployed;
* operated.

### 395. Model Enterprise Dependency Principle

Enterprise model dependency should be evaluated as a connected system rather than as isolated models.

A compromise or outage affecting a shared model, publisher, registry, or infrastructure component may create correlated exposure across multiple AI applications.

### 396. Model Assurance Principle

Model supply-chain assurance should combine:

* provenance;
* integrity;
* security evaluation;
* behavioral evaluation;
* dependency governance;
* monitoring;
* resilience;
* incident response.

No single mechanism provides complete assurance.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-396**

### 397. Model Resilience Principle

Model resilience should distinguish:

* documented recovery from tested recovery;
* alternative models from tested substitutes;
* model availability from business resilience;
* redundancy from independence;
* portability from behavioral equivalence.

**Control ID: AI-MSC-397**

### 398. Model Governance Distinction Principle

The organization should distinguish:

* framework mapping from legal applicability;
* internal control from statutory requirement;
* contractual obligation from legislation;
* supplier representation from independent assurance;
* technical capability from demonstrated operational capability.

### 399. AI Model Supply-Chain Enterprise Assurance Principle

Enterprise assurance should provide reasonable confidence that critical AI models are:

* identified;
* attributable;
* traceable;
* appropriately verified;
* evaluated;
* protected;
* monitored;
* recoverable;
* replaceable.

Assurance should be supported by evidence rather than assumptions alone.

### 400. AI Model Supply-Chain Governance Principle

Effective AI model supply-chain governance integrates model governance, cybersecurity, software security, data governance, procurement, privacy, legal, resilience, enterprise risk management, and assurance.

The central governance principle is:

```text id="msc4a13"
Know the Model
      |
Know Its Origin
      |
Know Its Lineage
      |
Verify Its Integrity
      |
Evaluate Its Behavior
      |
Understand Its Dependencies
      |
Control Its Use
      |
Monitor Its Change
      |
Test Its Recovery
      |
Maintain Its Exit
      |
Assure the Whole Chain
```

The organization should not assume that a model is trustworthy merely because it is:

* publicly available;
* widely used;
* highly downloaded;
* digitally signed;
* hosted by a reputable repository;
* accompanied by benchmark results;
* provided by a well-known publisher.

Likewise, the existence of provenance records, a model card, security questionnaire, evaluation report, certification, contractual commitment, or automated scanner does not by itself establish complete model supply-chain assurance.

Internal `AI-MSC-*` identifiers are organizational governance taxonomy identifiers. They are not provisions of legislation or regulation. Where a control is represented as mandatory, its actual legal, regulatory, contractual, or authoritative source should be separately identified and mapped.
