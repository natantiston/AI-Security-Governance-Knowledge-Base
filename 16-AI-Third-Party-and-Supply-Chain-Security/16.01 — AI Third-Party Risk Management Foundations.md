# 16.01 — AI Third-Party Risk Management Foundations

## Part 1 — Foundations, Scope, Risk Architecture, and Governance

### 1. Purpose of AI Third-Party Risk Management

AI Third-Party Risk Management (AI-TPRM) establishes the governance framework used to identify, assess, control, monitor, and terminate risks arising from external parties that provide, develop, host, operate, integrate, train, maintain, or support artificial intelligence capabilities.

AI third-party relationships can include:

* Large language model (LLM) providers
* Foundation-model providers
* AI SaaS providers
* Cloud AI platforms
* Managed AI services
* AI API providers
* Model marketplaces
* Open-source model repositories and maintainers
* Dataset providers
* Data-labeling providers
* AI software vendors
* AI security vendors
* Model-evaluation providers
* AI consulting and managed-service providers
* System integrators
* AI-enabled business-process outsourcing providers
* Third-party developers and contractors
* Model fine-tuning providers
* AI infrastructure providers
* AI agents and agentic-service providers

The objective is not merely to perform a conventional vendor risk assessment. AI-TPRM must determine how an external dependency can affect **AI safety, security, privacy, reliability, regulatory compliance, intellectual property, confidentiality, integrity, availability, human oversight, model behavior, data governance, and organizational accountability**.

---

### 2. AI Third-Party Risk as an Enterprise Risk Domain

An AI third party can become an extension of the organization's technology and decision-making environment.

Traditional supplier risk assessments often focus on:

* Information security
* Business continuity
* Privacy
* Financial stability
* Service availability
* Regulatory compliance
* Contractual performance

AI introduces additional risk dimensions, including:

* Model behavior
* Training-data provenance
* Model-data dependency
* Model integrity
* Model poisoning
* Prompt and instruction manipulation
* AI-generated content
* Model drift
* Evaluation reliability
* Bias and discriminatory outcomes
* Explainability limitations
* Human-oversight dependency
* AI-specific incident reporting
* Third-party model changes
* Hidden subcontractors
* Model and dataset licensing
* Intellectual-property exposure
* AI supply-chain compromise
* Autonomous or agentic behavior
* Tool-use permissions
* External system access
* Model concentration risk

Accordingly, AI third-party risk should be incorporated into the organization's broader **Enterprise Risk Management (ERM)** framework rather than treated exclusively as a procurement or cybersecurity activity.

---

### 3. Definition of an AI Third Party

For purposes of this knowledge base, an **AI third party** is an external organization, individual, service, technology component, or dependency that materially contributes to the organization's acquisition, development, deployment, operation, maintenance, monitoring, or retirement of an AI capability.

The definition should be interpreted functionally rather than solely according to procurement terminology.

Examples include:

* A provider supplying an LLM through an API
* A cloud provider hosting an AI workload
* A vendor providing an AI-powered customer-service platform
* A company supplying training datasets
* An external provider fine-tuning a foundation model
* A consultant developing an AI application
* A marketplace supplying downloadable models
* An open-source repository supplying model artifacts
* A vendor providing an AI-powered security product
* A subcontractor processing data for an AI provider

A party may therefore constitute an AI third party even when there is no conventional software-license relationship.

---

### 4. AI Supply-Chain Dependency

AI systems frequently contain multiple layers of third-party dependency.

A simplified AI supply chain may include:

**Cloud Infrastructure → AI Infrastructure → Foundation Model → Fine-Tuned Model → AI Application → API/Integration → Business Process → End User**

Additional dependencies can include:

**Datasets → Data Processing → Training → Model Artifact → Evaluation → Deployment → Monitoring**

The organization may therefore depend on parties that it does not directly perceive as its primary AI vendor.

For example, an AI SaaS provider may itself depend on:

* A hyperscale cloud provider
* A foundation-model provider
* A model-hosting service
* A dataset provider
* An external vector database
* A security provider
* An observability platform
* A content-moderation service
* A payment or identity provider

This creates **fourth-party and nth-party risk**.

---

### 5. AI Third-Party Risk Hierarchy

An AI-TPRM program should distinguish among different levels of dependency.

#### Tier 1 — Direct AI Provider

The organization directly contracts with the party providing the AI capability.

Examples:

* LLM API provider
* AI SaaS provider
* AI cloud service
* Dataset provider

#### Tier 2 — Critical Subcontractor

A subcontractor materially supports the service provided by the direct vendor.

Examples:

* Foundation-model provider used by an AI SaaS vendor
* Cloud infrastructure provider
* External data processor

#### Tier 3 — Fourth-Party Dependency

A supplier used by the third-party provider's subcontractor.

#### Tier 4 — Embedded or Transitive Dependency

A dependency incorporated into software, model artifacts, libraries, datasets, or infrastructure without necessarily appearing in the organization's commercial relationship.

This hierarchy should be represented in the organization's AI supply-chain inventory.

### 8. AI Third-Party Risk Classification

AI vendors should be classified according to the potential consequences of their failure, compromise, misuse, non-compliance, or unexpected behavior.

A practical classification may include:

**Critical**

The third party supports AI capabilities whose compromise or failure could materially affect safety, regulated decisions, critical operations, sensitive information, or major business processes.

**High**

The third party processes sensitive information, provides material AI functionality, influences consequential decisions, or creates significant regulatory, security, privacy, or operational exposure.

**Moderate**

The third party provides important but replaceable AI functionality with controlled data and limited decision impact.

**Low**

The third party provides non-critical AI functionality with limited access, limited data exposure, and minimal business impact.

The classification is an **internal risk-management methodology** and should not be represented as a statutory AI risk category unless a specific law or regulation establishes the classification.

### 21. AI Third-Party Governance Architecture

An effective AI-TPRM program normally requires coordination among several organizational functions.

A representative structure is:

**Board / Executive Management**

↓

**Enterprise Risk Management / AI Governance Committee**

↓

**AI Security Governance / GRC**

↓

**Procurement + Legal + Privacy + Information Security + Data Governance + Architecture**

↓

**Business Owner / Technical Owner**

↓

**AI Third Party / Supplier / Subprocessors**

The exact structure should be adapted to organizational size, regulatory obligations, risk profile, and operating model.

### 26. AI Third-Party Security Principles

An enterprise AI-TPRM program should be designed around several foundational principles:

1. **Visibility** — Know which external parties support AI.
2. **Accountability** — Assign an internal owner.
3. **Proportionality** — Apply controls according to risk.
4. **Traceability** — Maintain evidence of decisions and dependencies.
5. **Transparency** — Obtain sufficient information from suppliers.
6. **Security by Design** — Address AI-specific threats before deployment.
7. **Data Minimization** — Limit unnecessary third-party data exposure.
8. **Change Awareness** — Detect material supplier changes.
9. **Resilience** — Prepare for supplier failure or withdrawal.
10. **Exitability** — Maintain a viable termination and migration strategy.
11. **Regulatory Awareness** — Continuously assess changing obligations.
12. **Independent Assurance** — Test whether controls actually operate.

These principles provide the foundation for the more specialized AI vendor, model, dataset, API, software-supply-chain, contractual, and concentration-risk controls developed throughout this chapter.

### 28. Shadow AI and Unapproved Third Parties

AI-TPRM must address **shadow AI**—AI services acquired or used outside approved procurement, security, architecture, privacy, or AI-governance processes.

Shadow AI may arise through:

* Employees registering for public AI services
* Free AI SaaS tools
* Browser extensions
* Developer APIs
* AI coding assistants
* Public model repositories
* Model downloads
* AI plugins
* Personal accounts
* Unapproved cloud AI services
* Embedded AI features in existing SaaS

Shadow AI creates particular difficulty because the organization may not know:

* What data is being submitted
* Which model processes it
* Where data is processed
* Whether prompts are retained
* Whether data is used for training
* Which subprocessors are involved
* Which security controls exist
* Which legal terms apply

The AI-TPRM program should therefore operate alongside **AI discovery, application inventory, identity governance, data-loss prevention, cloud security, procurement monitoring, and acceptable-use controls**.

### 31. AI-TPRM-17 — Third-Party AI Intake Control

All proposed third-party AI services should enter a standardized intake process before procurement, technical integration, or production use.

The intake process should capture, at minimum:

* Supplier identity
* AI service name
* Business purpose
* Intended use
* AI capability

* Data processed

* Business process supported
* Integration architecture
* External interfaces
* Geographic processing
* Regulatory exposure
* Criticality
* Required availability
* Planned deployment environment
* Subprocessors
* Known fourth parties

The intake record should become the starting point for the subsequent AI-TPRM assessment.

**Control Type:** Preventive Governance Control
**Primary Objective:** Controlled entry into AI third-party risk management
**Evidence:** Supplier intake form, AI service request, architecture submission, initial risk classification

---

### 32. AI-TPRM-18 — AI Use-Case Registration Control

Each material third-party AI service should be associated with a documented business use case.

The registration should describe:

* Business objective
* Intended users
* Intended outputs
* Decision supported
* Degree of automation
* Human involvement
* Data categories
* Business process
* Expected benefits
* Known limitations
* Security considerations
* Privacy considerations
* Regulatory considerations

A single supplier may support multiple AI use cases with materially different risk profiles.

Accordingly, supplier-level approval should not automatically constitute approval for every possible use of the service.

**Control Type:** Use-Case Governance Control
**Primary Objective:** Prevent uncontrolled expansion of approved AI services
**Evidence:** Use-case records, approval records, application inventory

---

### 33. AI-TPRM-19 — Supplier-versus-Use-Case Risk Separation

The organization should distinguish:

**Supplier Risk**

from:

**AI Use-Case Risk**

A supplier may have strong security controls while a particular organizational use case remains high risk because of:

* Sensitive data
* Consequential decision-making
* Large affected populations
* Regulatory requirements
* Autonomous behavior
* External tool access
* Human-oversight limitations

Conversely, a high-risk supplier dependency may create exposure even when an individual use case appears relatively low risk.

Risk assessment should therefore operate at both the **supplier level** and the **use-case level**.

### 37. AI-TPRM-23 — Supplier Residual Risk Assessment

After evaluating supplier controls, the organization should determine residual risk.

The assessment should consider:

**Inherent Risk → Existing Controls → Control Effectiveness → Residual Risk**

Residual risk should be compared against organizational risk appetite.

Where residual risk is unacceptable, the organization should:

* Require remediation
* Add compensating controls
* Restrict the use case
* Reduce data exposure
* Modify architecture
* Require contractual protections
* Select an alternative supplier
* Reject the service

---

### 38. AI-TPRM-24 — Risk Tiering Control

AI suppliers should be assigned a risk tier using documented criteria.

A representative methodology may use:

**Tier 1 — Critical**

Material operational, regulatory, safety, security, or strategic dependency.

**Tier 2 — High**

Significant data, regulatory, security, or business-process exposure.

**Tier 3 — Moderate**

Meaningful but contained AI dependency.

**Tier 4 — Low**

Limited exposure and readily replaceable functionality.

The organization's methodology should define the assessment frequency and control requirements for each tier.

These tiers are an **internal GRC classification**, not a universal legal classification.
