# 16.04 — AI SaaS Risk — Part 1

## Foundations, SaaS Classification, Risk Architecture, and Governance

### 1 — AI-SAAS-001 — AI SaaS Risk Definition

AI SaaS risk is the risk arising from the use of externally hosted software services that incorporate artificial intelligence capabilities and process organizational data, perform business functions, make or support decisions, interact with enterprise systems, or provide AI-enabled functionality through a software-as-a-service delivery model.

### 2 — AI-SAAS-002 — AI SaaS Governance Objective

AI SaaS governance should ensure that externally hosted AI-enabled applications are identified, assessed, approved, configured, monitored, and retired according to their security, privacy, operational, regulatory, contractual, and business risks.

### 3 — AI-SAAS-003 — AI SaaS vs Traditional SaaS

AI SaaS should not automatically be treated as equivalent to conventional SaaS because AI capabilities may introduce additional risks involving model behavior, prompts, generated outputs, training-data use, inference processing, autonomous actions, model changes, and AI-specific dependencies.

### 4 — AI-SAAS-004 — AI SaaS Classification

Organizations should establish an internal classification scheme for AI SaaS services based on factors such as business criticality, data sensitivity, AI functionality, degree of automation, external connectivity, regulatory exposure, and potential impact.

### 5 — AI-SAAS-005 — AI SaaS Provider

An AI SaaS provider is an external organization that delivers software functionality through a hosted service while incorporating one or more AI capabilities into the product or service.

### 6 — AI-SAAS-006 — AI SaaS Provider Role

An AI SaaS provider may simultaneously act as application provider, AI model integrator, model developer, model host, data processor, infrastructure consumer, API intermediary, or marketplace participant.

### 7 — AI-SAAS-007 — Embedded Model Dependency

An AI SaaS product may depend on third-party LLMs, foundation models, machine-learning models, AI APIs, cloud AI platforms, datasets, or other external services that are not directly contracted by the customer.

### 8 — AI-SAAS-008 — Hidden AI Dependency

Organizations should consider whether an AI SaaS provider relies on upstream AI providers whose identity, jurisdiction, processing location, or security characteristics may materially affect organizational risk.

### 9 — AI-SAAS-009 — AI SaaS Dependency Mapping

Material AI SaaS services should be mapped to relevant applications, users, business processes, data flows, AI models, APIs, cloud infrastructure, subprocessors, and upstream providers.

### 10 — AI-SAAS-010 — AI SaaS Business Criticality

AI SaaS criticality should reflect the potential effect of service unavailability, incorrect outputs, data exposure, security compromise, regulatory noncompliance, or provider failure on business operations.

### 11 — AI-SAAS-011 — AI SaaS Data Sensitivity

The organization should determine what categories of organizational information may be processed by each AI SaaS service.

### 12 — AI-SAAS-012 — AI SaaS Use-Case Classification

Each material AI SaaS deployment should have an identified business use case, intended users, authorized functions, expected outputs, and relevant risk characteristics.

### 13 — AI-SAAS-013 — AI SaaS Risk Dimensions

AI SaaS risk assessment should consider cybersecurity, privacy, data governance, AI model, application security, identity, availability, resilience, regulatory, contractual, intellectual-property, supply-chain, concentration, financial, and exit risks.

### 14 — AI-SAAS-014 — Cybersecurity Risk

AI SaaS introduces cybersecurity risks involving application vulnerabilities, identity compromise, unauthorized access, insecure integrations, data leakage, malicious inputs, account takeover, and provider-side compromise.

### 15 — AI-SAAS-015 — Application Security Risk

AI SaaS applications should be assessed for application-security risks appropriate to the service architecture and organizational use.

### 16 — AI-SAAS-016 — AI-Specific Application Risk

AI functionality should be evaluated for risks that may not exist in conventional SaaS, including prompt injection, unsafe outputs, hallucination, model manipulation, excessive agency, and unintended tool execution.

### 17 — AI-SAAS-017 — Identity Dependency

AI SaaS security depends significantly on the effectiveness of identity and access controls used to administer and consume the service.

### 18 — AI-SAAS-018 — Authentication Risk

Weak authentication mechanisms can expose AI SaaS accounts, administrative interfaces, integrations, data, and AI configurations to unauthorized access.

### 19 — AI-SAAS-019 — Strong Authentication

Administrative access to material AI SaaS services should use authentication mechanisms appropriate to organizational security requirements.

### 20 — AI-SAAS-020 — Federated Identity

Where technically and commercially feasible, enterprise identity federation should be considered for organizational access to AI SaaS services.

### 21 — AI-SAAS-021 — Single Sign-On

Single sign-on may improve centralized identity governance and reduce unmanaged authentication dependencies for enterprise AI SaaS services.

### 22 — AI-SAAS-022 — Privileged Access

Administrative privileges within AI SaaS platforms should be restricted according to least-privilege principles.

### 23 — AI-SAAS-023 — Administrative Role Separation

AI SaaS administrative roles should be separated where practical to reduce excessive concentration of privileges.

### 24 — AI-SAAS-024 — Privileged Access Review

Administrative and privileged access to material AI SaaS services should be periodically reviewed.

### 25 — AI-SAAS-025 — User Lifecycle Integration

AI SaaS access should integrate with organizational joiner, mover, and leaver processes where appropriate.

### 26 — AI-SAAS-026 — Orphaned Account Risk

Inactive or orphaned AI SaaS accounts should be identified and removed or disabled.

### 27 — AI-SAAS-027 — Service Account Governance

Service accounts used to integrate enterprise applications with AI SaaS should have defined ownership, purpose, privileges, lifecycle, and monitoring requirements.

### 28 — AI-SAAS-028 — API Credential Governance

AI SaaS API credentials should be securely generated, stored, rotated, monitored, and revoked.

### 29 — AI-SAAS-029 — Secret Management

API keys, tokens, client secrets, certificates, and other AI SaaS credentials should be stored using approved secret-management mechanisms.

### 30 — AI-SAAS-030 — Credential Scope

AI SaaS credentials should be restricted to the minimum privileges and service scope required.

### 31 — AI-SAAS-031 — Credential Environment Separation

Where practical, credentials should be separated across development, testing, staging, and production environments.

### 32 — AI-SAAS-032 — Credential Exposure Response

The organization should maintain procedures for responding to suspected compromise or exposure of AI SaaS credentials.

### 33 — AI-SAAS-033 — API Access Monitoring

Material AI SaaS API activity should be monitored for anomalous or unauthorized behavior where technically feasible.

### 34 — AI-SAAS-034 — Data Processing Model

The organization should understand how AI SaaS processes organizational data, including input, inference, storage, logging, output generation, analytics, support, and deletion processes.

### 35 — AI-SAAS-035 — Prompt Data

Prompts submitted to AI SaaS applications should be treated as potentially sensitive organizational information and governed accordingly.

### 36 — AI-SAAS-036 — Input Data Classification

Data submitted to AI SaaS services should be classified according to organizational data-classification requirements.

### 37 — AI-SAAS-037 — Output Data Classification

AI-generated outputs should be classified according to their content and intended use rather than automatically treated as non-sensitive.

### 38 — AI-SAAS-038 — Data Minimization

AI SaaS services should receive only the information necessary to perform the approved business function.

### 39 — AI-SAAS-039 — Sensitive Data Restriction

Organizations should define categories of sensitive information that may not be submitted to AI SaaS services unless explicitly authorized.

### 40 — AI-SAAS-040 — Personal Data Processing

Where AI SaaS processes personal data, applicable privacy requirements and organizational privacy controls should be assessed.

### 41 — AI-SAAS-041 — Special-Category Data

Where sensitive or specially protected personal data is processed, additional legal, privacy, security, and governance requirements should be evaluated as applicable.

### 42 — AI-SAAS-042 — Confidential Business Information

Confidential business information submitted to AI SaaS should be subject to appropriate authorization and data-protection controls.

### 43 — AI-SAAS-043 — Intellectual Property Data

Source code, proprietary designs, trade secrets, research, and other intellectual-property-sensitive information should be governed before submission to external AI SaaS.

### 44 — AI-SAAS-044 — Data Residency

Where organizational, contractual, or regulatory requirements impose geographic processing restrictions, the AI SaaS service and configuration should be evaluated against those requirements.

### 45 — AI-SAAS-045 — Geographic Processing

The organization should determine relevant jurisdictions in which AI SaaS data may be processed or stored where such information is available.

### 46 — AI-SAAS-046 — Cross-Border Data Transfer

Where AI SaaS processing involves international data transfers, applicable legal and privacy requirements should be assessed.

### 47 — AI-SAAS-047 — Data Transfer Mechanisms

Appropriate contractual or legal mechanisms should be established where required for applicable cross-border personal-data transfers.

### 48 — AI-SAAS-048 — Data Retention

The organization should determine how long AI SaaS providers retain prompts, uploaded files, outputs, logs, metadata, and related customer information.

### 49 — AI-SAAS-049 — Retention Configuration

Available AI SaaS retention settings should be configured according to approved organizational requirements.

### 50 — AI-SAAS-050 — Retention Verification

Critical retention settings should be periodically verified.

### 51 — AI-SAAS-051 — Provider Training Use

The organization should determine whether AI SaaS providers use customer prompts, files, outputs, or other information for model training, service improvement, analytics, or other secondary purposes.

### 52 — AI-SAAS-052 — Training-Use Restriction

Where organizational, contractual, privacy, or legal requirements prohibit provider use of customer information for model training or other secondary purposes, applicable restrictions should be established and verified.

### 53 — AI-SAAS-053 — Training Policy Evidence

Claims concerning customer-data training restrictions should be supported by applicable contractual, technical, or service documentation.

### 54 — AI-SAAS-054 — Secondary Data Use

Secondary use of organizational information by AI SaaS providers should be identified and assessed.

### 55 — AI-SAAS-055 — Human Review of Customer Data

Organizations should determine whether provider personnel may access customer prompts, files, outputs, logs, or other information for support, safety, abuse prevention, quality assurance, or other purposes.

### 56 — AI-SAAS-056 — Provider Personnel Access

Provider personnel access to organizational information should be governed according to the service's contractual and security arrangements.

### 57 — AI-SAAS-057 — Subprocessor Dependency

AI SaaS providers may use subprocessors for hosting, storage, analytics, support, AI inference, security monitoring, or other services.

### 58 — AI-SAAS-058 — Subprocessor Inventory

Material subprocessors should be identified where relevant information is available.

### 59 — AI-SAAS-059 — Subprocessor Change Monitoring

Material subprocessor changes should be evaluated where they may affect data processing, security, privacy, or contractual obligations.

### 60 — AI-SAAS-060 — Fourth-Party Dependency

Dependencies beyond direct subprocessors should be considered where upstream services materially affect the AI SaaS service.

### 61 — AI-SAAS-061 — AI Model Dependency

AI SaaS providers should be assessed for dependence on external models and AI infrastructure where such dependency materially affects risk.

### 62 — AI-SAAS-062 — Model Provider Transparency

Where material to risk assessment, organizations should seek appropriate information concerning the external models supporting an AI SaaS service.

### 63 — AI-SAAS-063 — Model Change Dependency

Changes to underlying AI models may affect application behavior, accuracy, security, privacy, and compliance.

### 64 — AI-SAAS-064 — Model Version Transparency

For material AI SaaS use cases, organizations should seek sufficient information to understand significant changes in underlying model versions where available.

### 65 — AI-SAAS-065 — Model Change Notification

Contracts or service arrangements should address appropriate notification or information rights for material model changes where commercially and technically feasible.

### 66 — AI-SAAS-066 — Model Regression Risk

Material model changes should be evaluated for potential regression in security, reliability, functionality, or business performance.

### 67 — AI-SAAS-067 — AI Output Reliability

Organizations should assess the reliability limitations of AI-generated outputs when outputs support business processes or consequential decisions.

### 68 — AI-SAAS-068 — Hallucination Risk

AI SaaS applications should account for the possibility of inaccurate, fabricated, incomplete, or misleading outputs.

### 69 — AI-SAAS-069 — Output Validation

Outputs used in consequential business processes should be subject to appropriate validation.

### 70 — AI-SAAS-070 — Human Oversight

Human oversight should be incorporated where risk assessment determines that AI-generated outputs or actions require human review.

### 71 — AI-SAAS-071 — Automated Decision Risk

Where AI SaaS contributes to automated or semi-automated decisions, the organization should assess the legal, ethical, operational, security, and business implications applicable to the use case.

### 72 — AI-SAAS-072 — AI Agency

AI SaaS services capable of performing actions, invoking tools, sending communications, modifying records, or interacting with enterprise systems should be assessed for increased risk arising from agency.

### 73 — AI-SAAS-073 — Tool Invocation

AI SaaS capabilities that can invoke external tools or enterprise APIs should use explicit authorization boundaries.

### 74 — AI-SAAS-074 — High-Impact Action Control

Actions capable of producing material financial, legal, operational, security, privacy, or customer impact should have appropriate authorization and validation controls.

### 75 — AI-SAAS-075 — Human Approval Gate

Material AI SaaS actions may require human approval where organizational risk assessment determines that autonomous execution is inappropriate.

### 76 — AI-SAAS-076 — AI SaaS Integration Risk

Integrations between AI SaaS and enterprise systems should be assessed for data leakage, privilege escalation, unauthorized actions, insecure APIs, and excessive permissions.

### 77 — AI-SAAS-077 — Integration Inventory

Material integrations between AI SaaS services and enterprise applications should be inventoried.

### 78 — AI-SAAS-078 — Integration Ownership

Each material AI SaaS integration should have an accountable technical or business owner.

### 79 — AI-SAAS-079 — Integration Authorization

AI SaaS integrations should be formally authorized before being connected to enterprise systems where organizational policy requires approval.

### 80 — AI-SAAS-080 — Integration Least Privilege

AI SaaS integrations should receive only the permissions necessary for their approved functionality.

### 81 — AI-SAAS-081 — Integration Segmentation

Where feasible, AI SaaS integrations should be logically or technically separated according to application, environment, data sensitivity, or business function.

### 82 — AI-SAAS-082 — Integration Monitoring

Material AI SaaS integrations should be monitored for anomalous access, excessive activity, unexpected destinations, and unauthorized behavior where technically feasible.

### 83 — AI-SAAS-083 — Webhook and Callback Security

AI SaaS webhooks, callbacks, and event-driven interfaces should use appropriate authentication, integrity validation, authorization, and replay-protection mechanisms.

### 84 — AI-SAAS-084 — API Input Validation

Applications consuming AI SaaS APIs should validate relevant inputs and enforce expected data formats and security constraints.

### 85 — AI-SAAS-085 — API Output Validation

Applications consuming AI SaaS outputs should validate data before passing it into sensitive or consequential downstream processes.

### 86 — AI-SAAS-086 — Untrusted AI Output

AI-generated content should be treated as potentially untrusted when it can influence software, commands, queries, configurations, workflows, or security-sensitive operations.

### 87 — AI-SAAS-087 — Code Execution Boundary

AI SaaS output should not be allowed to execute privileged code or commands without appropriate security controls and authorization.

### 88 — AI-SAAS-088 — Prompt Injection

AI SaaS applications should be assessed for prompt-injection risks where user-controlled, external, or retrieved content can influence model behavior.

### 89 — AI-SAAS-089 — Indirect Prompt Injection

Applications that process external documents, websites, emails, or other untrusted content through AI SaaS should consider indirect prompt-injection risks.

### 90 — AI-SAAS-090 — Retrieval-Augmented AI SaaS

Where AI SaaS uses retrieval-augmented generation, the organization should assess the security and integrity of connected knowledge sources.

### 91 — AI-SAAS-091 — Knowledge-Source Authorization

AI SaaS retrieval systems should enforce appropriate access controls on enterprise knowledge sources.

### 92 — AI-SAAS-092 — Knowledge-Source Segmentation

Sensitive knowledge repositories should be appropriately segmented when exposed to AI SaaS retrieval functionality.

### 93 — AI-SAAS-093 — Retrieval Data Leakage

Organizations should assess whether retrieval mechanisms could expose information to users who are not authorized to access the underlying source.

### 94 — AI-SAAS-094 — AI SaaS Configuration Baseline

Material AI SaaS services should have an approved configuration baseline covering relevant security, privacy, identity, data, integration, and AI settings.

### 95 — AI-SAAS-095 — Configuration Governance

Material configuration changes should be subject to appropriate change-management controls.

### 96 — AI-SAAS-096 — Configuration Drift

Organizations should detect and address material deviations from approved AI SaaS configurations where technically feasible.

### 97 — AI-SAAS-097 — Configuration Evidence

Critical AI SaaS configuration states should be retained as evidence where they materially support security or compliance requirements.

### 98 — AI-SAAS-098 — Feature Governance

New AI SaaS features should not automatically become approved enterprise capabilities without appropriate assessment where organizational governance requires review.

### 99 — AI-SAAS-099 — Experimental Feature Restriction

Preview, beta, experimental, or otherwise immature AI SaaS features should be subject to appropriate restrictions before use in material production processes.

### 100 — AI-SAAS-100 — Feature Change Risk

Material changes to AI SaaS capabilities should be evaluated for security, privacy, operational, compliance, and business impact.

### 101 — AI-SAAS-101 — Feature Retirement

Organizations should monitor retirement or deprecation of material AI SaaS features that support business processes.

### 102 — AI-SAAS-102 — Service Availability

AI SaaS availability should be assessed according to the business criticality of dependent processes.

### 103 — AI-SAAS-103 — Provider Outage Risk

AI SaaS outages may affect multiple business functions and should be incorporated into appropriate continuity assessments.

### 104 — AI-SAAS-104 — Business Continuity Dependency

Critical AI SaaS services should be identified within relevant business-continuity planning.

### 105 — AI-SAAS-105 — Recovery Requirement

Recovery requirements should be defined for critical processes dependent on AI SaaS.

### 106 — AI-SAAS-106 — Outage Fallback

Where justified by business impact, critical AI SaaS processes should have an alternative operating procedure, fallback service, or manual process.

### 107 — AI-SAAS-107 — Fallback Feasibility

Fallback arrangements should be evaluated for security, functionality, data compatibility, cost, operational feasibility, and regulatory suitability.

### 108 — AI-SAAS-108 — Fallback Testing

Critical fallback mechanisms should be tested periodically where justified by risk.

### 109 — AI-SAAS-109 — Provider Concentration

Organizations should assess concentration arising from dependence on a single AI SaaS provider across multiple applications or business functions.

### 110 — AI-SAAS-110 — Correlated Failure

Concentration should consider correlated failure where multiple services depend on the same AI SaaS provider, cloud infrastructure, model provider, or upstream dependency.

### 111 — AI-SAAS-111 — Geographic Concentration

Geographic concentration of AI SaaS processing or infrastructure should be assessed where regional disruption could materially affect operations.

### 112 — AI-SAAS-112 — Jurisdictional Dependency

AI SaaS dependencies across jurisdictions should be assessed for applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, privacy, and operational implications.

### 113 — AI-SAAS-113 — Provider Ownership Change

Material provider ownership, acquisition, restructuring, or control changes should be evaluated for potential effects on security, privacy, service continuity, and contractual risk.

### 114 — AI-SAAS-114 — Commercial Dependency

Significant dependence on a provider's pricing, licensing, service tier, usage limits, or commercial model should be considered in provider-risk assessments.

### 115 — AI-SAAS-115 — Cost Governance

AI SaaS consumption should be monitored where usage-based pricing or AI inference costs could create material financial exposure.

### 116 — AI-SAAS-116 — Usage Monitoring

Material AI SaaS services should have appropriate usage monitoring.

### 117 — AI-SAAS-117 — Cost Anomaly Detection

Significant deviations from expected AI SaaS consumption or expenditure should be detectable and investigated.

### 118 — AI-SAAS-118 — Service-Tier Governance

Organizations should understand differences between AI SaaS service tiers that may affect security, privacy, retention, availability, support, model access, or contractual protections.

### 119 — AI-SAAS-119 — Service-Tier Change Assessment

Changes to service tiers should be assessed where they could materially alter organizational risk.

### 120 — AI-SAAS-120 — Provider Support Risk

Provider support personnel and support processes may have access to organizational data, configurations, logs, or administrative functions and should therefore be considered in the AI SaaS risk assessment.

### 121 — AI-SAAS-121 — Support Access Governance

Provider support access should be appropriately restricted and governed according to contractual and security arrangements.

### 122 — AI-SAAS-122 — Support Escalation

Critical AI SaaS services should have documented support escalation mechanisms appropriate to business impact.

### 123 — AI-SAAS-123 — Provider Security Assurance

Organizations should obtain appropriate security assurance regarding material AI SaaS providers based on service criticality and risk.

### 124 — AI-SAAS-124 — Assurance Scope

Provider assurance should be evaluated for scope, service coverage, testing period, control objectives, exceptions, and applicability to the organization's actual implementation.

### 125 — AI-SAAS-125 — Assurance Limitation

A provider certification, audit report, questionnaire, or security attestation should not automatically be treated as complete assurance for every AI SaaS feature, configuration, integration, or use case.

### 126 — AI-SAAS-126 — AI SaaS Governance Principle

AI SaaS governance should treat externally hosted AI-enabled applications as a distinct third-party risk category requiring integrated management of traditional SaaS risks and AI-specific risks, including model dependency, prompt and output data, AI behavior, automated actions, provider changes, hidden upstream dependencies, security, privacy, resilience, contractual obligations, and lifecycle management.

# Part 2 — Due Diligence, Security Assurance, Data Governance, Privacy, and Technical Controls

### 127 — AI-SAAS-127 — AI SaaS Due Diligence

Organizations should perform documented due diligence before approving material AI SaaS services for organizational use.

### 128 — AI-SAAS-128 — Due Diligence Scope

Due diligence should address cybersecurity, privacy, data processing, AI functionality, model dependencies, identity, integrations, resilience, regulatory exposure, contractual obligations, supply-chain dependencies, and exit considerations.

### 129 — AI-SAAS-129 — Use-Case-Specific Due Diligence

The provider assessment should reflect the intended business use case rather than relying solely on generic provider-level security information.

### 130 — AI-SAAS-130 — Business Owner Assessment

The business owner should document the business purpose, expected users, critical processes, expected benefits, and consequences of service failure or misuse.

### 131 — AI-SAAS-131 — Technical Owner Assessment

The technical owner should document the intended architecture, integrations, authentication mechanisms, data flows, administrative interfaces, and dependencies.

### 132 — AI-SAAS-132 — Data Owner Assessment

The relevant data owner should determine what categories of organizational data may be processed by the AI SaaS service.

### 133 — AI-SAAS-133 — Security Assessment

Security functions should assess the AI SaaS service according to organizational third-party and application-security requirements.

### 134 — AI-SAAS-134 — Privacy Assessment

Where personal data is involved, the appropriate privacy assessment should be completed before material processing begins.

### 135 — AI-SAAS-135 — Legal Assessment

Material contractual, regulatory, intellectual-property, jurisdictional, and liability issues should be reviewed by appropriate legal or compliance personnel.

### 136 — AI-SAAS-136 — Regulatory Applicability Assessment

The organization should determine which legal and regulatory requirements may apply to the AI SaaS service based on jurisdiction, industry, role, data, and use case.

### 137 — AI-SAAS-137 — Provider Questionnaire

A structured questionnaire may be used to obtain information concerning provider security, privacy, resilience, AI governance, data handling, and operational controls.

### 138 — AI-SAAS-138 — Questionnaire Validation

Material questionnaire responses should be validated against supporting evidence where the provider's risk warrants additional assurance.

### 139 — AI-SAAS-139 — Provider Security Documentation

Relevant provider security documentation should be collected and evaluated.

### 140 — AI-SAAS-140 — Trust-Center Evidence

Provider trust-center materials may provide useful assurance information but should be assessed for scope, currency, and applicability.

### 141 — AI-SAAS-141 — Independent Assurance

Where appropriate, independent audit reports, certifications, attestations, penetration-testing summaries, or comparable assurance evidence should be evaluated.

### 142 — AI-SAAS-142 — Assurance Scope Review

Assurance evidence should be reviewed for the actual service, environment, geographic scope, control period, exceptions, and applicable features.

### 143 — AI-SAAS-143 — Certification Limitation

Possession of a security certification should not automatically be interpreted as proof that every AI SaaS feature or customer configuration satisfies organizational requirements.

### 144 — AI-SAAS-144 — Assurance Gap

Material gaps between required controls and available provider evidence should be documented.

### 145 — AI-SAAS-145 — Compensating Control

Where provider controls cannot satisfy an organizational requirement, feasible compensating controls should be evaluated.

### 146 — AI-SAAS-146 — Evidence Currency

Material provider evidence should be sufficiently current to support the risk decision.

### 147 — AI-SAAS-147 — Evidence Reliability

Provider evidence should be evaluated according to source reliability, independence, scope, completeness, and applicability.

### 148 — AI-SAAS-148 — Marketing Claim Validation

Security, privacy, compliance, AI safety, data-residency, retention, and training claims should be validated against authoritative provider evidence where material.

### 149 — AI-SAAS-149 — Security Architecture Review

Material AI SaaS services should undergo an appropriate security architecture review.

### 150 — AI-SAAS-150 — Trust Boundary Identification

The architecture review should identify trust boundaries between users, enterprise systems, AI SaaS components, provider infrastructure, models, subprocessors, and external data sources.

### 151 — AI-SAAS-151 — Data-Flow Mapping

Material AI SaaS services should have documented data flows showing relevant inputs, processing activities, storage, outputs, integrations, and external dependencies.

### 152 — AI-SAAS-152 — Data-Flow Validation

Documented data flows should be validated against actual implementation where practical.

### 153 — AI-SAAS-153 — Data Inventory

Data categories processed through AI SaaS should be identified and documented.

### 154 — AI-SAAS-154 — Data Classification

Data submitted to AI SaaS should be classified according to the organization's information-classification scheme.

### 155 — AI-SAAS-155 — Data Handling Rules

Data-classification rules should define which information may be processed through specific AI SaaS services.

### 156 — AI-SAAS-156 — Prohibited Data

The organization should define categories of information that may not be submitted to AI SaaS services unless explicitly authorized.

### 157 — AI-SAAS-157 — Data Minimization

Only information necessary for the approved business purpose should be transmitted to the AI SaaS service.

### 158 — AI-SAAS-158 — Prompt Minimization

Prompts should contain only the information required to obtain the intended service outcome.

### 159 — AI-SAAS-159 — File Upload Governance

Uploading documents or files to AI SaaS services should be governed according to data sensitivity and approved use-case requirements.

### 160 — AI-SAAS-160 — File-Type Restrictions

Where appropriate, technical controls should restrict prohibited or high-risk file types from being uploaded to AI SaaS platforms.

### 161 — AI-SAAS-161 — Data Redaction

Sensitive information not required for the intended AI operation should be redacted where feasible.

### 162 — AI-SAAS-162 — Data Masking

Masking or tokenization may be used to reduce exposure of sensitive information where functionality permits.

### 163 — AI-SAAS-163 — Pseudonymization

Where appropriate, pseudonymization may reduce direct exposure of identifiable information while preserving required analytical or processing functionality.

### 164 — AI-SAAS-164 — Personal Data Inventory

Personal-data categories processed by the AI SaaS service should be identified where applicable.

### 165 — AI-SAAS-165 — Processing Purpose

The purpose for processing personal or organizational data through the AI SaaS service should be documented.

### 166 — AI-SAAS-166 — Lawful Processing Assessment

Where personal data is involved, the organization should establish the applicable legal basis or other required processing justification according to applicable privacy law.

### 167 — AI-SAAS-167 — Privacy Impact Assessment

Where required or appropriate, the organization should conduct a privacy impact assessment or equivalent privacy-risk assessment.

### 168 — AI-SAAS-168 — Data Processor Assessment

Where the AI SaaS provider processes personal data on behalf of the organization, the relevant processor relationship and obligations should be assessed.

### 169 — AI-SAAS-169 — Data Processing Agreement

Where required by applicable privacy law, an appropriate data-processing agreement or equivalent contractual arrangement should be established.

### 170 — AI-SAAS-170 — Processing Instructions

Where a provider acts as a processor, relevant processing instructions should be appropriately defined.

### 171 — AI-SAAS-171 — Subprocessor Controls

Applicable requirements concerning provider subprocessors should be addressed through appropriate contractual and governance mechanisms.

### 172 — AI-SAAS-172 — Subprocessor Transparency

The organization should seek sufficient information about material subprocessors to assess relevant security and privacy risks.

### 173 — AI-SAAS-173 — Subprocessor Objection

Where applicable contractual or legal arrangements provide customer rights concerning subprocessors, the organization should maintain a process for exercising and managing those rights.

### 174 — AI-SAAS-174 — International Transfer Assessment

International transfers of personal data through AI SaaS services should be assessed according to applicable legal requirements.

### 175 — AI-SAAS-175 — Transfer Mechanism

Where required, an appropriate legal mechanism should support applicable international data transfers.

### 176 — AI-SAAS-176 — Transfer Risk Assessment

Where required by applicable law or organizational policy, relevant transfer risks should be assessed and documented.

### 177 — AI-SAAS-177 — Data Residency Configuration

Where the AI SaaS platform provides geographic processing controls, those controls should be configured according to approved requirements.

### 178 — AI-SAAS-178 — Residency Verification

Configured geographic restrictions should be periodically verified for critical services.

### 179 — AI-SAAS-179 — Provider Processing Location

The organization should maintain sufficient information regarding relevant provider processing locations where geographic processing materially affects risk.

### 180 — AI-SAAS-180 — Data Retention Policy

AI SaaS data retention should be defined according to applicable security, privacy, legal, regulatory, contractual, and business requirements.

### 181 — AI-SAAS-181 — Retention Configuration

Available provider retention controls should be configured according to approved requirements.

### 182 — AI-SAAS-182 — Retention Verification

Critical AI SaaS retention configurations should be periodically verified.

### 183 — AI-SAAS-183 — Deletion Requirement

Where deletion is required by applicable law, contract, policy, or business requirements, the relevant provider obligations should be established.

### 184 — AI-SAAS-184 — Deletion Capability

The organization should determine whether the AI SaaS service provides practical mechanisms for deleting relevant customer data.

### 185 — AI-SAAS-185 — Deletion Verification

Where deletion commitments are material, appropriate evidence of deletion should be obtained where feasible.

### 186 — AI-SAAS-186 — Backup Retention

Provider backup and disaster-recovery retention should be considered where it affects organizational deletion or retention requirements.

### 187 — AI-SAAS-187 — Data Export

The organization should determine whether relevant customer data can be exported in usable formats.

### 188 — AI-SAAS-188 — Export Completeness

Data-export assessments should consider documents, prompts, outputs, metadata, configurations, records, user data, and other information required for business continuity or migration.

### 189 — AI-SAAS-189 — Provider Data Ownership

Contracts should clearly address applicable rights and responsibilities concerning customer data.

### 190 — AI-SAAS-190 — AI Output Rights

Where generated outputs have material commercial or intellectual-property significance, applicable provider terms concerning output rights should be reviewed.

### 191 — AI-SAAS-191 — Customer Content

The organization should understand how the provider defines and handles customer content, prompts, uploaded files, outputs, and associated metadata.

### 192 — AI-SAAS-192 — Secondary Use

Potential provider secondary use of customer information should be identified and assessed.

### 193 — AI-SAAS-193 — Model Training Use

The organization should determine whether customer data may be used for model training or service improvement.

### 194 — AI-SAAS-194 — Training Restriction

Where training use is prohibited by organizational, contractual, privacy, or legal requirements, appropriate restrictions should be established.

### 195 — AI-SAAS-195 — Training Configuration

Where the service provides customer-controlled training or data-use settings, those settings should be configured according to approved requirements.

### 196 — AI-SAAS-196 — Training-Use Verification
