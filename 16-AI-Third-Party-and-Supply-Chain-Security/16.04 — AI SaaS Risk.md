# 16.04 — AI SaaS Risk

## Part 1 — Foundations, AI SaaS Risk, Service Boundaries, Data Governance, Architecture and Accountability

### 1. AI SaaS Risk

AI Software-as-a-Service (AI SaaS) risk is the risk that an organization is exposed to security, privacy, operational, compliance, legal, financial, or business consequences because it consumes artificial intelligence capabilities through an externally operated SaaS service.

Unlike a traditional software product installed and operated entirely by the organization, AI SaaS generally combines:

* externally operated application software;
* externally managed infrastructure;
* AI or machine-learning capabilities;
* provider-controlled data processing;
* provider-managed security controls;
* external APIs and integrations;
* and potentially additional model, cloud, dataset, and subprocessor dependencies.

The organization therefore depends on the SaaS provider for more than software availability.

### 2. AI SaaS Risk Objective

The objective of AI SaaS risk management is to ensure that externally operated AI applications are appropriately identified, assessed, approved, secured, monitored, and governed throughout their lifecycle.

Governance should address:

* what AI SaaS service is being used;
* who provides it;
* what AI capabilities it contains;
* what data it processes;
* where processing occurs;
* which models support the service;
* what external dependencies exist;
* what security controls are provided;
* what controls remain the customer's responsibility;
* how the provider manages changes;
* how incidents are handled;
* what contractual protections exist;
* and how the organization can exit the service.

### 4. AI SaaS Versus Traditional SaaS

AI SaaS introduces additional considerations beyond conventional SaaS because the service may dynamically generate, transform, classify, summarize, predict, retrieve, or act upon information.

Potential AI-specific risks include:

* model hallucination;
* prompt injection;
* sensitive-data disclosure;
* model changes;
* AI-generated code;
* automated decision support;
* tool invocation;
* agentic behavior;
* retrieval-based data exposure;
* model-provider dependency;
* and unpredictable output.

Traditional SaaS controls therefore may be necessary but not always sufficient for AI SaaS.

### 7. AI SaaS Use-Case Classification

The same AI SaaS platform may create different risk levels depending on its use.

* public-content generation;
* internal knowledge search;
* customer support;
* software development;
* security operations;
* financial analysis;
* employee management;
* or automated business workflows.

Risk should therefore be evaluated according to the actual use case rather than the product name.

### 8. Business Criticality

AI SaaS criticality should be determined according to the business services that depend on it.

Potential factors include:

* business impact;
* customer impact;
* revenue dependency;
* regulatory significance;
* security impact;
* data sensitivity;
* operational dependency;
* availability requirements;
* recovery requirements;
* and availability of alternatives.

A SaaS service used for an optional productivity function should not automatically receive the same governance treatment as an AI SaaS platform supporting a critical business service.

### 15. Shared Responsibility

AI SaaS generally operates under some form of shared responsibility.

The provider may control:

* application infrastructure;
* SaaS platform;
* provider-side security;
* model integration;
* provider operations;
* and service availability.

The customer may control:

* users;
* identities;
* configurations;
* data;
* integrations;
* permissions;
* and business-process use.

The precise responsibility boundary depends on the service.

### 16. Customer Responsibility Boundary

Customer responsibilities may include:

* selecting approved use cases;
* configuring security settings;
* managing user access;
* protecting credentials;
* classifying data;
* controlling integrations;
* monitoring use;
* and governing business decisions based on AI output.

The provider's security controls do not automatically secure the customer's configuration.

### 17. Provider Responsibility Boundary

Provider responsibilities may include, depending on the service:

* infrastructure security;
* SaaS application security;
* vulnerability management;
* service availability;
* provider-side access control;
* model integration;
* incident response;
* and platform maintenance.

The organization should validate the actual responsibility model rather than relying on assumptions.

### 19. Responsibility Gap

A responsibility gap occurs when an important control is assumed to be owned by the other party.

Examples include:

* logging;
* data deletion;
* model change notification;
* incident evidence;
* identity integration;
* tool authorization;
* or configuration monitoring.

Responsibility gaps should be identified during due diligence and architecture review.

### 23. Data Minimization

The organization should minimize information submitted to an AI SaaS provider to what is necessary for the approved purpose.

Potential controls include:

* field filtering;
* redaction;
* masking;
* pseudonymization;
* tokenization;
* document filtering;
* context reduction;
* and retrieval restrictions.

Minimization can reduce the impact of provider compromise, unauthorized access, inappropriate retention, or unintended secondary use.

### 24. Data Ownership

The organization should establish ownership and control expectations for data submitted to the AI SaaS provider.

Relevant questions include:

* Who owns the information?
* Who is authorized to submit it?
* Can the provider use it for other purposes?
* Can the provider retain it?
* Can the provider use it for training?
* Can the organization retrieve it?
* Can it be deleted?
* Can derived information remain after termination?

Ownership, custody, processing rights, and contractual rights should be treated as distinct concepts.

### 25. Data Retention

The organization should determine how long information is retained by the AI SaaS provider.

Retention may apply to:

* user inputs;
* prompts;
* uploaded documents;
* outputs;
* logs;
* telemetry;
* backups;
* support records;
* and derived information.

Retention requirements may differ by service tier or configuration.

### 26. Data Deletion

Where deletion is required by contract, policy, or applicable law, the organization should understand how the provider performs deletion.

Relevant considerations may include:

* primary storage;
* backups;
* replicated data;
* caches;
* logs;
* derived data;
* embeddings;
* and subprocessors.

Deletion should be evaluated for both technical capability and evidence.

### 27. Data Return

The organization should establish what information can be returned when the AI SaaS relationship ends.

Potential information includes:

* source data;
* uploaded files;
* outputs;
* configurations;
* metadata;
* logs;
* workflow definitions;
* prompts;
* evaluation datasets;
* and other customer-controlled information.

Data return should be evaluated for completeness and usability.

### 28. Data Portability

Data export does not automatically establish practical portability.

Portability should consider:

* format;
* completeness;
* metadata;
* relationships;
* timestamps;
* access controls;
* application compatibility;
* migration tooling;
* and operational usability.

A provider may technically export data while still creating substantial migration difficulty.

### 29. Provider Data Training Use

The organization should determine whether information submitted to the AI SaaS provider may be used for:

* model training;
* fine-tuning;
* evaluation;
* service improvement;
* abuse detection;
* or other secondary purposes.

The organization should verify whether controls differ between:

* free and paid plans;
* consumer and enterprise products;
* API and interactive interfaces;
* or different geographic regions.

### 30. Secondary Data Use

Secondary use should be explicitly assessed.

Potential uses include:

* analytics;
* product improvement;
* model evaluation;
* marketing;
* abuse monitoring;
* research;
* or service optimization.

The organization should determine whether such uses are consistent with applicable contractual, privacy, security, and governance requirements.

### 31. Provider Personnel Access

AI SaaS provider personnel may require access to customer information for:

* support;
* troubleshooting;
* security operations;
* incident investigation;
* or service administration.

The organization should understand:

* who may access data;
* under what conditions;
* whether approval is required;
* whether access is logged;
* whether access is monitored;

Provider administrative access should be governed through appropriate controls.

Potential controls include:

* MFA;
* privileged-access management;
* just-in-time access;
* role separation;
* approval;
* session monitoring;
* and audit logging.

The organization should assess provider assurance regarding these controls where direct verification is not possible.

### 33. Tenant Isolation

AI SaaS is frequently multi-tenant.

The organization should understand how tenants are logically or technically isolated.

Potential mechanisms include:

* tenant identifiers;
* logical data separation;
* access-control boundaries;
* encryption;
* application-layer isolation;
* database isolation;
* and administrative separation.

The required level of assurance should be proportionate to the sensitivity of the data.

Potential cross-tenant risks may arise from:

* authorization errors;

* shared caches;
* logging failures;

Where material, the organization should evaluate the provider's controls for preventing cross-tenant exposure.

### 35. AI SaaS Authentication

Authentication controls should ensure that only authorized users, applications, and services can access the AI SaaS platform.

Potential mechanisms include:

* SSO;
* MFA;
* federation;
* service identities;
* API authentication;
* workload identity;
* and conditional access.

The organization's authentication architecture should be integrated with its enterprise identity system where appropriate.

### 36. Authorization

Authorization should control:

* who can use the AI SaaS;
* which features they can use;
* what data they can access;
* which integrations they can activate;
* what administrative actions they can perform;
* and which AI agents or tools they can invoke.

Authentication establishes identity; authorization determines permitted actions.

### 37. Role-Based Access Control

RBAC may be used to separate responsibilities such as:

* end user;
* power user;
* developer;
* administrator;
* security administrator;
* auditor;
* and service owner.

Roles should follow least privilege.

### 38. Privileged Access

Administrative functions should be protected through enhanced controls.

Potential requirements include:

* MFA;
* PAM;
* separate administrative accounts;
* approval;
* session logging;
* periodic review;
* and rapid revocation.

Privileged access should be limited to authorized personnel.

### 39. Service Accounts

AI SaaS integrations may use service accounts or machine identities.

These identities should have:

* defined ownership;
* limited permissions;
* credential protection;
* rotation;
* monitoring;
* and lifecycle management.

Shared credentials should be avoided where individual or workload identities are feasible.

### 40. API Access

AI SaaS platforms may expose APIs for:

* data ingestion;
* automation;
* model interaction;
* administration;
* workflow integration;
* and reporting.

API governance should address:

* authentication;

* input validation;

### 41. API Credential Protection

API credentials should be stored in controlled secrets-management systems where feasible.

They should not be:

* embedded in source code;
* stored in public repositories;
* placed in client-side applications without appropriate protection;
* included in prompts;
* or shared between unrelated applications.

Credential exposure can provide both data-access and financial-abuse capabilities.

### 42. Network Security

The organization should assess how users and applications connect to the AI SaaS service.

Relevant controls may include:

* TLS;
* secure DNS;
* web filtering;
* proxy controls;
* egress filtering;
* private connectivity where supported;
* firewall policy;

Uncontrolled outbound access may increase shadow-AI and data-exfiltration risk.

* unauthorized browser tools;

* browser extensions;
* AI meeting tools;

Shadow AI can bypass:

* security review;
* privacy assessment;
* contracts;
* logging;
* data classification;
* and incident-response mechanisms.

### 50. AI SaaS Governance Principle

**AI-SAAS-050 — AI SaaS Governance Principle**

An organization should govern AI SaaS as a third-party technology and AI dependency whose risk depends on the service architecture, enabled AI capabilities, business use case, data processed, permissions, integrations, provider dependencies, and operational criticality.

Effective governance should establish the provider and service identity, define the responsibility boundary, map data flows and downstream dependencies, classify permitted data, control authentication and authorization, manage API access, address shadow AI, evaluate provider security and assurance, and maintain appropriate lifecycle, resilience, and exit controls.

Provider security controls do not eliminate the customer's responsibility for secure configuration, data governance, access management, business-process decisions, and residual risk.

### Core Principles — Part 1

1. **AI SaaS Dependency Principle** — AI SaaS should be governed as an external enterprise dependency rather than merely as an application subscription.
2. **Use-Case Principle** — AI SaaS risk depends on the actual business use case and enabled capabilities.
3. **Shared-Responsibility Principle** — Provider and customer responsibilities should be explicitly identified and verified.
4. **Data-Boundary Principle** — Data entering, leaving, or being generated by the AI SaaS service should be identified and governed.
5. **Data-Minimization Principle** — Only information necessary for the approved purpose should be submitted to the external service.
6. **Access-Control Principle** — Users, applications, service accounts, and administrators should receive only the access necessary for authorized activities.
7. **Tenant-Isolation Principle** — The organization should understand the provider's mechanisms for protecting customer environments from unauthorized cross-tenant access.
8. **API-Security Principle** — AI SaaS APIs should be governed as security-sensitive interfaces.
9. **Shadow-AI Principle** — Unauthorized AI SaaS usage can bypass established security, privacy, contractual, and governance controls.
10. **Provider-Assurance Principle** — Provider certifications, audit reports, questionnaires, and security claims should be evaluated for scope, currency, independence, and applicability.
11. **Dependency-Visibility Principle** — Material model, cloud, API, subprocessor, dataset, and infrastructure dependencies should be identified where they affect organizational risk.
12. **Portability Principle** — Data export should be distinguished from practical portability and migration capability.
13. **Lifecycle Principle** — AI SaaS governance should extend from discovery and onboarding through operation, monitoring, renewal, replacement, and verified offboarding.
14. **Accountability Principle** — Outsourcing an AI SaaS capability does not automatically transfer ownership of the organization's residual risk.
15. **Governance-Classification Principle** — Internal risk tiers and control identifiers are organizational governance constructs and should not be represented as statutory or regulatory classifications unless explicitly supported by an applicable external requirement.

## Part 2 — Due Diligence, Security Assurance, AI Controls, Configuration, Integration and Data Protection

### 51. AI SaaS Due-Diligence Scope

AI SaaS due diligence should evaluate the specific service being procured and the way the organization intends to use it. A provider may have strong general security controls while the particular AI capability introduces additional risks involving prompts, model outputs, retrieval systems, agents, integrations, training data, or administrative functions.

Due diligence should therefore cover:

* provider organization and ownership;
* service architecture;
* AI functionality;
* model dependencies;
* data processing;
* security controls;
* privacy controls;
* identity and access management;
* integrations;
* logging and monitoring;
* incident management;
* resilience;
* regulatory applicability;
* contractual commitments;
* subcontractors and subprocessors;
* service changes;
* exit and portability.

The assessment should be proportionate to business criticality and data sensitivity.

### 56. AI SaaS Model-Change Dependency

AI SaaS applications may change underlying models without requiring a customer software deployment.

The organization should determine:

* whether model changes are provider-controlled;
* whether customers receive advance notice;
* whether model versions can be pinned;
* whether behavioral changes are documented;
* whether regression testing is possible;
* whether customers can reject material changes;
* whether model changes can affect security controls;
* whether model changes can alter data-processing behavior.

Model substitution should therefore be treated as a potential service-change dependency.

### Core Principles

1. **Due-Diligence Principle** — Assess the actual AI SaaS service and intended use case.
2. **Architecture Principle** — Understand the service's material technical and AI dependencies.
3. **Model-Transparency Principle** — Identify material model dependencies where information is available.
4. **Change Principle** — Treat material model and service changes as potential risk changes.
5. **Configuration Principle** — Establish and maintain secure SaaS configurations.
6. **Identity Principle** — Integrate AI SaaS with enterprise identity governance.
7. **Least-Privilege Principle** — Minimize user, administrator, API, OAuth, and integration permissions.
8. **Data-Boundary Principle** — Define what information may enter and leave the AI SaaS boundary.
9. **Prompt-Governance Principle** — Treat prompts as potentially sensitive enterprise data.
10. **Output-Governance Principle** — Govern AI outputs according to their content and consequences.
11. **RAG-Security Principle** — Enforce authorization before protected information is retrieved for AI processing.
12. **Agent-Control Principle** — Constrain agent permissions, tools, actions, and execution scope.
13. **Auditability Principle** — Maintain sufficient evidence to reconstruct material AI SaaS activity.
14. **Assurance Principle** — Evaluate provider claims according to scope, currency, independence, and evidence.
15. **Resilience Principle** — Understand business dependence on the AI SaaS service and available alternatives.
16. **Incident-Integration Principle** — Integrate provider incidents into organizational incident response.
17. **Lifecycle Principle** — Govern AI SaaS from assessment through termination.
18. **Evidence Principle** — Do not interpret assurance evidence beyond its documented scope.
19. **Risk-Acceptance Principle** — Document and authorize residual risk rather than leaving it implicit.
20. **Accountability Principle** — Assign clear ownership for every material customer-side AI SaaS control.

### Core Principles

1. **Continuous-Monitoring Principle** — AI SaaS risk should be monitored throughout the service lifecycle.
2. **Change-Detection Principle** — Material service, model, data, integration, and provider changes should be identified.
3. **Regression Principle** — Material AI behavior should be tested against an established baseline where feasible.
4. **Access-Review Principle** — User, privileged, service-account, API, and OAuth access should be periodically reviewed.
5. **Configuration-Compliance Principle** — Actual configurations should be compared with approved security baselines.
6. **Incident-Integration Principle** — AI SaaS incidents should integrate with enterprise incident response.
7. **Evidence-Preservation Principle** — Relevant evidence should be preserved during material incidents.
8. **Corrective-Action Principle** — Material findings should result in tracked corrective actions.
9. **Resilience Principle** — Critical AI SaaS dependencies should have practical continuity and recovery arrangements.
10. **Concentration Principle** — Shared underlying providers and infrastructure should be considered when assessing diversification.
11. **Exit-Readiness Principle** — Exit should be assessed before operational dependency becomes excessive.
12. **Portability Principle** — Data, knowledge, workflows, prompts, and AI behavior should be considered when assessing portability.
13. **Assurance-Currency Principle** — Provider assurance evidence should remain sufficiently current and relevant.
14. **Traceability Principle** — Governance decisions should be traceable to evidence and applicable requirements.
15. **Automation Principle** — Automated governance should be auditable and monitored for failure.
16. **Human-Oversight Principle** — Material governance decisions should retain accountable human ownership.
17. **Uncertainty Principle** — Unknown dependencies and incomplete evidence should be explicitly recorded.
18. **Lifecycle Principle** — Renewal, reassessment, migration, and decommissioning are governance events.
19. **Integration Principle** — AI SaaS governance should integrate with cybersecurity, privacy, procurement, architecture, and third-party risk management.
20. **Continuous-Assurance Principle** — Assurance should reflect the service actually operating in production, not merely the service originally approved.

The objective is to ensure that the organization's risk assessment remains aligned with the service that actually operates in production.

**Control ID: `AI-SAAS-210 — AI SaaS Continuous-Assurance Principle`**

### Core Principles

1. **Continuous-Monitoring Principle** — AI SaaS risk should be monitored throughout the service lifecycle.
2. **Change-Detection Principle** — Material service, model, data, integration, and provider changes should be identified.
3. **Regression Principle** — Material AI behavior should be tested against an established baseline where feasible.
4. **Access-Review Principle** — User, privileged, service-account, API, and OAuth access should be periodically reviewed.
5. **Configuration-Compliance Principle** — Actual configurations should be compared with approved security baselines.
6. **Incident-Integration Principle** — AI SaaS incidents should integrate with enterprise incident response.
7. **Evidence-Preservation Principle** — Relevant evidence should be preserved during material incidents.
8. **Corrective-Action Principle** — Material findings should result in tracked corrective actions.
9. **Resilience Principle** — Critical AI SaaS dependencies should have practical continuity and recovery arrangements.
10. **Concentration Principle** — Shared underlying providers and infrastructure should be considered when assessing diversification.
11. **Exit-Readiness Principle** — Exit should be assessed before operational dependency becomes excessive.
12. **Portability Principle** — Data, knowledge, workflows, prompts, and AI behavior should be considered when assessing portability.
13. **Assurance-Currency Principle** — Provider assurance evidence should remain sufficiently current and relevant.
14. **Traceability Principle** — Governance decisions should be traceable to evidence and applicable requirements.
15. **Automation Principle** — Automated governance should be auditable and monitored for failure.
16. **Human-Oversight Principle** — Material governance decisions should retain accountable human ownership.
17. **Uncertainty Principle** — Unknown dependencies and incomplete evidence should be explicitly recorded.
18. **Lifecycle Principle** — Renewal, reassessment, migration, and decommissioning are governance events.
19. **Integration Principle** — AI SaaS governance should integrate with cybersecurity, privacy, procurement, architecture, and third-party risk management.
20. **Continuous-Assurance Principle** — Assurance should reflect the service actually operating in production, not merely the service originally approved.
