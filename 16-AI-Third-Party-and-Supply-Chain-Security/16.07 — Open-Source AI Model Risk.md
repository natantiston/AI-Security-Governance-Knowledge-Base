# 16.07 — Open-Source AI Model Risk

## Part 1 — Foundations, Open-Source Model Classification, Provenance, Dependency and Risk Architecture

### 1 — Open-Source AI Model Risk Definition

Open-source AI model risk is the risk arising from acquiring, downloading, modifying, deploying, integrating, or relying on AI models distributed with source code, model weights, configuration, documentation, or other artifacts under an open or publicly accessible licensing and distribution model.

### 2 — Governance Objective

The objective of open-source AI model governance is to enable appropriate use of openly distributed AI models while controlling risks involving:

* cybersecurity;
* model integrity;
* provenance;
* licensing;
* data;
* privacy;
* intellectual property;
* vulnerabilities;
* malicious modification;
* supply-chain compromise;
* unsupported dependencies;
* model behavior;
* operational resilience.

### 3 — Open-Source AI Model Definition

An open-source AI model should not be treated simply as a model that can be downloaded without payment.

Assessment should distinguish among:

* open-source software;
* open-weight models;
* freely downloadable models;
* source-available models;
* research releases;
* restricted-license models;
* community models.

### 4 — Open-Source Versus Open-Weight

Open-weight availability does not necessarily mean that:

* training data is available;
* training methodology is available;
* source code is available;
* commercial use is unrestricted;
* redistribution is unrestricted.

The organization's classification should therefore be based on the actual license and available artifacts.

### 5 — Open-Source Model Governance Classification

Organizations should establish an internal classification scheme for models obtained from public or community sources.

Possible classifications include:

* approved open-source model;
* approved open-weight model;
* restricted-license model;
* research-only model;
* unverified model;
* prohibited model.

### 6 — Model Source Identification

The organization should identify the original source from which the model was obtained.

Sources may include:

* official project repository;
* official model repository;
* recognized model hub;
* package repository;
* institutional repository;
* community distribution channel.

### 7 — Official Source Preference

Where possible, production models should be obtained from authoritative project or publisher sources rather than unverified mirrors.

### 8 — Model Publisher Identification

The organization should identify the entity or community responsible for publishing the model.

### 9 — Model Maintainer Identification

Where applicable, the organization should identify:

* project maintainers;
* publishing organization;
* repository maintainers;
* model contributors.

### 10 — Maintainer Accountability

The absence of an identifiable maintainer should be considered when evaluating the sustainability and supportability of a model dependency.

### 11 — Model Repository Assessment

The repository hosting a model should be assessed for relevant indicators of:

* authenticity;
* activity;
* maintenance;
* security;
* documentation;
* version history.

### 12 — Repository Ownership

Organizations should establish whether the repository is controlled by:

* the original model developer;
* a recognized organization;
* a community;
* an unknown party.

### 13 — Repository Integrity

Where technically feasible, the integrity of the repository and model artifacts should be validated before production use.

### 14 — Model Artifact Identification

The organization should identify the actual model artifacts being deployed.

Artifacts may include:

* model weights;
* tokenizer files;
* configuration files;
* adapters;
* inference code;
* preprocessing components;
* post-processing components.

### 15 — Artifact Completeness

Organizations should determine whether all components required to operate the model are known and controlled.

### 16 — Model Package Dependency

A downloaded model may depend on external:

* libraries;
* runtimes;
* packages;
* APIs;
* system components.

These dependencies should be identified where material.

### 17 — Model Dependency Mapping

The organization should maintain an appropriate dependency relationship:

**Application → Model → Model Artifacts → Runtime → Libraries → Infrastructure**

### 18 — Open-Source Model Criticality

Model criticality should be determined according to the impact of model failure, compromise, or unavailability on the organization.

### 19 — Business Criticality

A model should receive greater governance attention when it supports:

* critical business processes;
* customer-facing services;
* security operations;
* regulated activities;
* financial processes.

### 20 — Data Criticality

The sensitivity of information processed by the model should be incorporated into its risk assessment.

### 21 — Security Criticality

Models used in:

* security monitoring;
* threat detection;
* identity systems;
* privileged operations;

may require enhanced controls because model compromise could affect security decisions.

### 22 — Operational Criticality

Operational criticality should consider the consequences of model:

* outage;
* degradation;
* corruption;
* replacement difficulty.

### 23 — Regulatory Criticality

Where a model supports processes subject to legal or regulatory requirements, applicable requirements should be incorporated into the assessment.

### 24 — Model Risk Dimensions

Open-source model risk should be assessed across multiple dimensions, including:

* provenance;
* integrity;
* cybersecurity;
* licensing;
* privacy;
* data;
* model behavior;
* vulnerabilities;
* operational resilience;
* supply chain;
* governance.

### 25 — Provenance Risk

Provenance risk arises when the organization cannot establish where a model originated or how the artifact reached the deployment environment.

### 26 — Model Authenticity

The organization should establish reasonable confidence that the model artifact corresponds to the intended publisher or release.

### 27 — Artifact Tampering

Model artifacts may be altered before reaching the organization.

Potential threats include:

* malicious replacement;
* unauthorized modification;
* compromised repositories;
* malicious mirrors.

### 28 — Model Integrity

Model integrity means maintaining confidence that the deployed artifact has not been altered without authorization.

### 29 — Cryptographic Integrity

Where supported, organizations should use:

* cryptographic hashes;
* digital signatures;
* trusted checksums;

to validate model artifacts.

### 30 — Hash Verification

A known-good hash should be compared with the hash of the downloaded model artifact where a trustworthy reference exists.

### 31 — Signature Verification

Digitally signed model artifacts should be verified where the publisher provides trustworthy signing mechanisms.

### 32 — Trusted Distribution

Production model artifacts should preferably be acquired through controlled and trusted distribution channels.

### 33 — Model Mirror Risk

Unofficial mirrors may introduce uncertainty regarding:

* authenticity;
* integrity;
* version;
* modifications.

### 34 — Mirror Governance

Where a mirror is required, the organization should establish controls for validating the provenance and integrity of the mirrored artifact.

### 35 — Version Identification

The organization should record the exact model version or release used in production.

### 36 — Immutable Version Reference

Where feasible, production deployments should reference immutable versions rather than ambiguous labels such as:

* latest;
* stable;
* current.

### 37 — Model Version Traceability

The organization should be able to determine which model version is associated with a production application.

### 38 — Model Change Risk

Changes to open-source model artifacts may affect:

* functionality;
* security;
* performance;
* behavior;
* licensing.

### 39 — Model Update Governance

Model updates should be subject to appropriate change-management procedures.

### 40 — Automatic Model Updates

Automatic retrieval of updated model artifacts should be controlled where uncontrolled changes could create material risk.

### 41 — Model Pinning

Critical applications should consider pinning approved model versions.

### 42 — Model Rollback

Where feasible, organizations should maintain the ability to return to a previously approved model version.

### 43 — Model Lifecycle

The lifecycle of an open-source model should include:

**Discovery → Assessment → Approval → Acquisition → Validation → Deployment → Monitoring → Update → Retirement**

### 44 — Model Discovery

Models discovered through public repositories should undergo appropriate preliminary screening before organizational adoption.

### 45 — Preliminary Screening

Screening may consider:

* publisher;
* license;
* popularity;
* maintenance;
* documentation;
* security concerns;
* known vulnerabilities.

### 46 — Model Approval

Production use should require appropriate approval according to organizational policy and risk.

### 47 — Approved Model Registry

Organizations should maintain an inventory or registry of approved open-source models where material use exists.

### 48 — Model Registry Information

The registry may contain:

* model name;
* version;
* publisher;
* source;
* license;
* hash;
* owner;
* use case;
* environment;
* approval status.

### 49 — Model Ownership

Each material open-source model deployment should have an accountable owner.

### 50 — Technical Ownership

Technical ownership should address:

* deployment;
* maintenance;
* patching;
* configuration;
* monitoring.

### 51 — Business Ownership

Business ownership should address:

* intended use;
* business value;
* acceptable risk;
* operational dependency.

### 52 — Security Ownership

Security responsibility should be explicitly assigned for material open-source model deployments.

### 53 — Legal Ownership

Legal or appropriate compliance functions should be engaged when licensing, intellectual property, privacy, or contractual questions are material.

### 54 — Open-Source License Identification

The organization should identify the license governing the model and relevant associated artifacts.

### 55 — License Version Identification

Where the license has multiple versions, the organization should identify the specific version applicable to the acquired artifact.

### 56 — License Scope

License assessment should determine what rights and restrictions apply to:

* model weights;
* source code;
* documentation;
* datasets;
* derivatives.

### 57 — Commercial Use Assessment

The organization should determine whether commercial use is permitted where commercial deployment is intended.

### 58 — Redistribution Assessment

Where model artifacts may be distributed to customers, partners, or other parties, redistribution rights should be assessed.

### 59 — Modification Rights

Organizations should determine whether the applicable license permits modification of the model or associated components.

### 60 — Derivative Model Assessment

Where a model is modified, fine-tuned, merged, or otherwise transformed, the organization should assess applicable licensing requirements.

### 61 — License Obligation Tracking

Material license obligations should be documented and assigned to accountable owners.

### 62 — License Compatibility

Licenses associated with the model and its dependencies should be evaluated for compatibility with the intended deployment.

### 63 — License Conflict

Potential conflicts between model licenses, software licenses, and organizational distribution requirements should be escalated for appropriate review.

### 64 — License Evidence

Organizations should retain evidence of the license applicable to the model version actually used.

### 65 — License Change Risk

Changes in licensing terms between releases should be identified before upgrading where material.

### 66 — License Compliance Monitoring

Material open-source model deployments should be periodically reviewed for continued compliance with applicable license obligations.

### 67 — Intellectual Property Risk

Open-source model use may create intellectual-property risks involving:

* model artifacts;
* training data;
* generated outputs;
* software dependencies;
* trademarks;
* documentation.

### 68 — Training Data Transparency

The organization should assess the extent to which information about training data is available and relevant to its risk assessment.

### 69 — Training Data Provenance

Where information is available, training-data provenance should be evaluated for material use cases.

### 70 — Training Data Licensing Risk

Where training-data licensing is material, organizations should assess available evidence concerning rights and restrictions.

### 71 — Dataset Dependency

A model may depend on datasets that create separate:

* privacy;
* licensing;
* security;
* provenance;

risks.

### 72 — Dataset-to-Model Traceability

Where feasible, organizations should maintain visibility into material datasets used to create or customize a model.

### 73 — Data Poisoning Risk

Open-source model development may be exposed to malicious or compromised training data.

### 74 — Training Data Integrity

Where relevant information is available, organizations should assess controls protecting training data against unauthorized modification.

### 75 — Model Poisoning Risk

Model poisoning may alter model behavior in ways that are difficult to identify through ordinary functional testing.

### 76 — Backdoor Risk

Organizations should consider the possibility that a model contains behavior triggered by specific inputs or conditions.

### 77 — Behavioral Integrity

Model evaluation should consider whether behavior remains consistent with approved expectations.

### 78 — Malicious Model Risk

Models obtained from untrusted or unknown sources may contain malicious functionality or artifacts.

### 79 — Model Security Screening

Before production deployment, organizations should perform appropriate security screening of model packages and associated components.

### 80 — Model Package Scanning

Where technically feasible, model packages should be scanned for:

* malware;
* suspicious files;
* malicious scripts;
* unexpected executables.

### 81 — Serialization Risk

Some model formats or supporting libraries may introduce risks when loading serialized objects or executable components.

### 82 — Safe Model Loading

Organizations should use secure loading mechanisms and avoid unsafe deserialization patterns where applicable.

### 83 — Model Runtime Isolation

Models obtained from external sources should execute within appropriately controlled runtime environments where feasible.

### 84 — Model Sandbox

Where the model runtime can execute code or interact with the operating system, sandboxing should restrict unnecessary capabilities.

### 85 — Runtime Privilege Restriction

Model-serving processes should operate with the minimum privileges required.

### 86 — Network Isolation

Self-hosted open-source models should use network controls appropriate to their exposure and data sensitivity.

### 87 — Outbound Network Restriction

Where external connectivity is unnecessary, model-serving environments should restrict outbound network access.

### 88 — Inbound Exposure Control

Model endpoints should not be exposed publicly unless the exposure is required and appropriately secured.

### 89 — Model API Authentication

Model APIs should require appropriate authentication.

### 90 — Model API Authorization

Model API access should be restricted according to:

* user;
* application;
* environment;
* function.

### 91 — Model Endpoint Segmentation

Model endpoints should be segmented according to risk and deployment environment.

### 92 — Development Model Environment

Development environments should be appropriately separated from production model environments.

### 93 — Testing Model Environment

Testing environments should provide sufficient isolation to prevent uncontrolled access to production information or infrastructure.

### 94 — Production Model Environment

Production model deployments should use stronger controls appropriate to their criticality.

### 95 — Model Secrets Management

Credentials required by model-serving environments should be protected using approved secrets-management mechanisms.

### 96 — Model Service Identity

Where supported, model services should use dedicated service identities.

### 97 — Least Privilege

Model-serving processes should receive only the permissions necessary for their intended operation.

### 98 — Privileged Model Operations

Permissions to:

* replace model artifacts;
* modify configurations;
* change serving infrastructure;
* alter model routing;

should be restricted.

### 99 — Separation of Duties

Where risk warrants, model acquisition, approval, deployment, and security validation should not all be controlled by a single individual.

### 100 — Model Artifact Access

Access to model artifacts should be restricted according to:

* business need;
* security requirements;
* licensing;
* confidentiality.

### 101 — Model Repository Access

Internal model repositories should enforce appropriate:

* authentication;
* authorization;
* logging;
* integrity controls.

### 102 — Internal Model Registry

Organizations may maintain an internal repository or registry containing validated model artifacts.

### 103 — Trusted Model Repository

Production deployments should preferably retrieve approved models from controlled internal repositories rather than repeatedly downloading directly from public sources.

### 104 — Model Artifact Promotion

Models may follow controlled promotion through:

**External Source → Validation → Internal Repository → Testing → Production**

### 105 — Artifact Quarantine

New model artifacts may be quarantined until security and provenance checks are completed.

### 106 — Artifact Approval Status

Artifacts should have explicit status such as:

* pending;
* approved;
* restricted;
* rejected;
* retired.

### 107 — Model Artifact Integrity Record

The registry should retain integrity information such as:

* hash;
* signature;
* source;
* acquisition date;
* version.

### 108 — Model Acquisition Logging

Acquisition of material model artifacts should be traceable.

### 109 — Model Source Logging

The organization should record where each production model artifact was obtained.

### 110 — Model Deployment Logging

Production deployment should generate appropriate records identifying:

* model;
* version;
* environment;
* deployment date;
* deploying entity.

### 111 — Model Provenance Chain

The organization should maintain an appropriate provenance chain:

**Publisher → Repository → Artifact → Validation → Internal Repository → Deployment**

### 112 — Provenance Gap

A material inability to establish model provenance should be treated as a risk requiring appropriate disposition.

### 113 — Unverified Model Handling

Unverified models should not automatically be permitted in production.

### 114 — Restricted Model Handling

Models with unresolved security, licensing, or provenance concerns should be subject to appropriate restrictions.

### 115 — Prohibited Model Criteria

Organizations should define circumstances under which a model cannot be used.

Potential criteria include:

* malicious artifacts;
* unverifiable provenance;
* unacceptable license restrictions;
* unresolved critical vulnerabilities;
* prohibited use conditions.

### 116 — Model Exception Process

Exceptions to approved model requirements should require documented authorization.

### 117 — Exception Risk Assessment

Model exceptions should identify:

* reason;
* risk;
* compensating controls;
* owner;
* expiration or review date.

### 118 — Model Vulnerability Dependency

Open-source models may depend on software components with known vulnerabilities.

### 119 — Dependency Inventory

Organizations should maintain visibility into material software dependencies supporting model deployment.

### 120 — Vulnerability Monitoring

Relevant vulnerabilities affecting:

* model runtimes;
* inference frameworks;
* libraries;
* operating systems;
* container images;

should be monitored.

### 121 — Model Runtime Patching

Model-serving infrastructure should be patched according to applicable vulnerability-management requirements.

### 122 — Unsupported Dependency Risk

Unsupported or abandoned dependencies should be considered in the model's operational risk assessment.

### 123 — Project Maintenance Risk

The organization should assess whether the project receives sufficient maintenance for its intended use.

### 124 — Maintenance Indicators

Relevant indicators may include:

* recent releases;
* issue resolution;
* security advisories;
* maintainer activity;
* community support.

### 125 — Abandoned Model Risk

Models that are no longer maintained may create increased:

* vulnerability;
* compatibility;
* lifecycle;
* support;

risk.

### 126 — Model Community Dependency

Community-supported models should be evaluated for the reliability and sustainability of their development ecosystem.

### 127 — Open-Source Model Dependency Principle

Open-source model adoption should be governed according to the actual model artifact, license, provenance, dependencies, deployment architecture, and intended use rather than the assumption that publicly available models are inherently low risk.

### 128 — Open-Source Model Risk Decision Principle

The decision to approve an open-source AI model should be based on **verified provenance, appropriate licensing, security validation, model integrity, dependency visibility, operational supportability, intended-use risk, and documented accountability**.

Internal `AI-OS-*` identifiers used in this knowledge base, where introduced in subsequent controls, are organizational taxonomy identifiers and should not be represented as provisions of external law or regulation unless an authoritative source explicitly establishes the corresponding requirement.

* security;
* dependencies;

* privacy;
* intellectual property;
* maintenance;

* model criticality;

* autonomy;
* dependency concentration.
