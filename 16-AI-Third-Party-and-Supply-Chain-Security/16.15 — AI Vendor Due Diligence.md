# 16.15 — AI Vendor Due Diligence

## Part 1 — Foundations, Vendor Due-Diligence Scope, Classification, Risk Assessment, Evidence and Governance Architecture

### 1. AI Vendor Due Diligence Definition

AI vendor due diligence is the structured process through which an organization evaluates a vendor before, during, and where necessary after engagement to determine whether the vendor's AI-related products, services, software, models, data processing, security controls, governance practices, and contractual commitments are appropriate for the intended use.

AI vendor due diligence should establish sufficient information to support an informed procurement, security, privacy, compliance, risk, and business decision.

---

### 2. AI Vendor Due-Diligence Objective

The objective is to determine whether a vendor can be used within the organization's defined risk tolerance and control environment.

Due diligence should establish an understanding of:

* What the vendor provides
* How the service operates
* What data it processes
* What AI capabilities it provides
* What dependencies exist
* What security controls exist
* What contractual commitments apply
* What risks remain

---

### 3. Due Diligence Is Not Vendor Approval

Due diligence provides evidence for a decision.

It does not automatically mean that the vendor is approved.

### 4. AI Vendor Risk Context

AI vendor risk depends heavily on the intended use of the vendor.

The same vendor may represent materially different risks depending on whether it provides:

* A low-risk productivity tool
* An internal AI assistant
* A customer-facing AI service
* A model API
* An autonomous agent
* A system supporting consequential decisions

---

### 5. Use-Case Dependency

Vendor due diligence should evaluate the vendor in the context of the actual organizational use case.

**Vendor risk ≠ vendor name alone.**

### 8. Vendor versus AI System Classification

Vendor classification should not be confused with the legal or technical classification of an AI system.

A vendor may provide multiple services with different risk characteristics.

---

### 9. High-Risk Vendor Internal Classification

An organization may classify a vendor as internally "high risk" because of:

* Data access
* Privilege
* Criticality
* AI autonomy
* Regulatory exposure
* Business dependency

This internal classification should not automatically be represented as the legal classification of a high-risk AI system under external legislation.

---

### 10. Vendor Criticality

Vendor criticality should reflect the consequences of vendor failure, compromise, or unavailability.

Factors may include:

* Business criticality
* Security dependency
* Data dependency
* Operational dependency
* Recovery difficulty
* Replacement difficulty

---

### 11. Vendor Materiality

A vendor may be material because it:

* Supports a critical business service
* Processes sensitive information
* Provides a critical AI capability
* Has privileged access
* Creates significant concentration risk

### 13. Tier 1 Vendor

A standard vendor may have:

* Limited data access
* Low business criticality
* Low privilege
* Limited AI impact

Due diligence may therefore be proportionate and streamlined.

---

### 14. Tier 2 Vendor

An elevated-risk vendor may have:

* Greater data access
* Broader integration
* Greater business dependency
* Increased security exposure

Enhanced due diligence may be appropriate.

---

### 15. Tier 3 Vendor

A high-risk vendor may involve:

* Sensitive data
* Significant business processes
* Privileged access
* Material AI functionality
* Significant external exposure

More extensive assurance may be required.

---

### 16. Tier 4 Vendor

A critical vendor may support:

* Critical AI infrastructure
* Critical business services
* Highly sensitive data
* Material autonomous capabilities
* Strategic enterprise functions

The organization may require enhanced governance and executive oversight.
