# 16.19 — AI Vendor Exit and Concentration Risk

## Part 1 — Foundations, Exit Risk, Concentration Risk, Dependency Architecture, Criticality and Governance

### 1. AI Vendor Exit Risk

AI vendor exit risk is the risk that an organization cannot safely, economically, or operationally discontinue or replace an AI vendor when required.

Exit risk may arise from:

* technical lock-in;
* proprietary interfaces;
* proprietary data formats;
* model-specific dependencies;
* contractual restrictions;
* insufficient alternatives;
* migration complexity;
* data portability limitations;
* operational dependency;
* concentration.

### 2. AI Vendor Concentration Risk

AI vendor concentration risk is the risk created when a material portion of AI capability depends on a limited number of providers, platforms, models, infrastructure providers, or related dependencies.

Concentration can affect:

* availability;
* resilience;
* security;
* negotiating leverage;
* regulatory exposure;
* operational continuity.

### 3. Exit and Concentration Relationship

Exit and concentration are related but distinct risks.


A highly concentrated environment may have elevated exit risk, but concentration does not automatically mean that exit is impossible.

### 4. Exit Risk Objective

The objective of exit governance is not necessarily to eliminate every vendor dependency.

The objective is to ensure that material dependencies are:

* understood;
* risk assessed;
* governed;
* monitored;
* recoverable where required;
* replaceable or containable where justified.

### 5. Concentration Risk Objective

The objective of concentration governance is to identify dependencies that could create unacceptable common-mode exposure.

### 6. Vendor Dependency

A vendor dependency exists when an organizational capability, service, process, application, model, dataset, or infrastructure component relies materially on an external provider.

### 7. Direct Vendor Dependency

A direct dependency exists when the organization obtains an AI capability directly from the provider.

Examples include:

* AI SaaS;
* model API;
* cloud AI service;
* managed AI platform.

### 8. Indirect Vendor Dependency

An indirect dependency exists when an AI capability depends on another service through an intermediary.

Examples include:

* AI application using an API aggregator;
* SaaS platform using a foundation-model provider;
* AI service deployed through a cloud marketplace.

### 9. Hidden Vendor Dependency

A hidden dependency may exist where the provider is not obvious from the primary service relationship.

Examples include:

* embedded models;
* subcontracted infrastructure;
* fourth-party APIs;
* external identity providers;
* managed vector databases.

### 10. Fourth-Party Dependency

A fourth-party dependency is a dependency beyond the organization's direct contractual provider.

The organization should determine whether material fourth-party dependencies can affect:

* service availability;
* data processing;
* security;
* resilience;
* exit.

### 13. Business-Service Dependency

Vendor dependency should be mapped to the business services that rely on it.

A provider supporting a non-critical experiment may present materially different exit requirements from a provider supporting a critical business process.

### 14. Critical Vendor Dependency

A vendor dependency becomes particularly significant when failure or exit would materially affect:

* critical business operations;
* customer services;
* regulated processes;
* sensitive data;
* safety-relevant functions;
* financial operations.

### 15. Vendor Criticality

Vendor criticality should be determined using the organization's risk methodology.

Potential factors include:

* business impact;
* data sensitivity;
* service dependency;
* substitutability;
* recovery time;
* regulatory exposure;
* operational complexity.

### 16. Criticality Is Not Provider Classification

An organization's internal classification of a vendor as critical is not automatically equivalent to a regulatory classification.

Internal risk tiers should not be represented as statutory categories unless the applicable law or regulation explicitly establishes such a category.

### 17. Exit Trigger

An exit requirement may arise from:

* contract termination;
* material security failure;
* repeated service failure;
* unacceptable risk;
* regulatory requirement;
* strategic decision;
* provider failure;
* insolvency;
* acquisition;
* unacceptable concentration.

### 18. Exit Trigger Governance

Exit triggers should be defined before a crisis occurs.

The organization should identify:

* triggering condition;
* decision authority;
* assessment process;
* migration process;
* communication;
* evidence requirements.

### 19. Contractual Exit Right

A contractual exit right establishes a legal or contractual mechanism for terminating or transitioning a service.

It does not by itself establish that the organization can technically migrate.

### 20. Practical Exit Capability

Practical exit capability requires the organization to have sufficient:

* knowledge;
* data access;
* technical capability;
* alternative services;
* migration procedures;
* resources.

### 22. Exit Readiness

Exit readiness is the degree to which the organization can execute a planned transition away from a vendor within an acceptable timeframe and risk boundary.

### 23. Exit Readiness Assessment

Exit readiness should consider:

* data portability;
* application portability;
* model portability;
* integration portability;
* identity dependencies;
* operational skills;
* alternative-provider availability;
* migration duration.

### 24. Exit Readiness Evidence

Evidence may include:

* migration plans;
* tested exports;
* architecture documentation;
* alternative-provider assessments;
* migration exercises;
* recovery exercises.

### 25. Exit Planning

Exit planning should begin during vendor onboarding for material dependencies rather than only after termination becomes imminent.

### 26. Exit-by-Design

Exit-by-design means incorporating portability and replacement considerations into the architecture and contract from the beginning of the relationship.

### 27. Exit Architecture

Exit architecture may include:

* abstraction layers;
* standardized interfaces;
* portable data formats;
* provider-independent orchestration;
* documented dependencies.

### 28. Provider-Specific Architecture

Provider-specific architecture can increase exit complexity.

Examples include:

* proprietary APIs;
* proprietary orchestration;
* proprietary embeddings;
* provider-specific agents;
* provider-specific security controls.

### 29. Technical Lock-In

Technical lock-in occurs when provider-specific technology materially increases the difficulty or cost of migration.

### 30. Data Lock-In

Data lock-in may arise when:

* data is stored in proprietary formats;
* export capabilities are limited;
* metadata is not portable;
* deletion and extraction are difficult to verify.

### 31. Model Lock-In

Model lock-in may arise when applications depend heavily on:

* proprietary model APIs;
* provider-specific prompts;
* model-specific behavior;
* proprietary fine-tuning;
* provider-specific tools.

### 32. Embedding Lock-In

Embedding-based applications may experience migration complexity because embeddings may depend on:

* model architecture;
* embedding dimensions;
* preprocessing;
* tokenizer;
* similarity behavior.

### 33. Agent Lock-In

Agentic systems may create additional lock-in through:

* proprietary orchestration;
* tool definitions;
* memory systems;
* workflow configurations;
* provider-specific agent runtimes.

### 34. API Lock-In

API lock-in may arise when applications depend on provider-specific:

* endpoints;
* schemas;
* authentication;
* streaming behavior;
* error handling;
* tool interfaces.

### 35. Configuration Lock-In

Configuration lock-in can occur when critical service behavior depends on provider-specific configurations that cannot easily be reproduced elsewhere.

### 36. Identity Lock-In

Identity dependencies may complicate exit where the provider is deeply integrated with:

* SSO;
* federation;
* service accounts;
* privileged access;
* identity lifecycle processes.

### 37. Security-Control Lock-In

A provider may supply security capabilities that become embedded in the organization's architecture.

Exit planning should determine how those controls will be replaced.

### 38. Monitoring Lock-In

Exit planning should consider dependencies on provider-specific:

* logs;
* telemetry;
* monitoring APIs;
* security analytics;
* alerting.

### 39. Operational Lock-In

Operational lock-in may arise when internal processes, skills, documentation, and support arrangements become heavily dependent on one provider.

### 40. Knowledge Lock-In

Knowledge lock-in occurs when only the provider or a small number of individuals understand critical service dependencies.

### 41. Skills Dependency

The organization should determine whether sufficient internal or external expertise exists to execute migration.

### 42. Documentation Dependency

Critical vendor configurations and integration dependencies should be documented sufficiently to support transition.

### 43. Contractual Lock-In

Contractual terms may increase exit difficulty through:

* long commitments;
* termination restrictions;
* migration limitations;
* data-export restrictions;
* proprietary licensing.

### 44. Economic Lock-In

Economic lock-in may arise from:

* high migration costs;
* sunk implementation costs;
* volume discounts;
* specialized integrations;
* retraining requirements.

### 45. Organizational Lock-In

Organizational lock-in may occur when business processes become dependent on vendor-specific capabilities.

### 46. User Lock-In

Users may become dependent on provider-specific:

* workflows;
* interfaces;
* prompts;
* configurations;
* automation.

### 50. Cloud Concentration

Cloud concentration exists when material AI workloads rely on a single cloud provider or cloud ecosystem.

### 51. Infrastructure Concentration

Infrastructure concentration exists when multiple services rely on shared underlying infrastructure.

### 52. Geographic Concentration

Geographic concentration may arise when material AI processing depends on a limited number of regions or locations.

### 53. Jurisdictional Concentration

Jurisdictional concentration may create common exposure to:

* legal changes;
* regulatory restrictions;
* geopolitical disruption;
* cross-border constraints.

### 54. Identity Concentration

Multiple AI services may depend on the same identity provider.

A failure or compromise could therefore affect multiple services simultaneously.

### 55. Network Concentration

Common network dependencies may create systemic availability or security exposure.

### 56. API Concentration

Multiple applications may rely on the same external API provider.

### 57. Dataset Concentration

Multiple AI systems may depend on the same third-party dataset or data provider.

### 58. Software Dependency Concentration

Multiple AI services may rely on the same:

* framework;
* runtime;
* library;
* package;
* inference engine.

### 59. Model Supply-Chain Concentration

Concentration may also exist when multiple services rely on a common model distribution or model supply chain.

### 60. Common-Mode Failure

Common-mode failure occurs when multiple services fail because they share a dependency or failure mechanism.

### 61. Common-Mode Security Failure

A compromised shared provider, model, software dependency, or infrastructure component may create simultaneous exposure across multiple AI services.

### 62. Common-Mode Availability Failure

A provider outage may affect multiple applications simultaneously where they share the same service dependency.

### 63. Common-Mode Data Risk

A shared provider may create common exposure to data-processing failures.

### 64. Common-Mode Control Failure

Multiple services may inherit the same provider control weakness.

* defined control;
* operational capability;
* test evidence;
* accountable ownership.

### 997. Governance Limitation

No governance framework can eliminate all vendor concentration or exit risk.

The objective is to establish risk visibility, practical resilience, informed decision-making, and proportionate control.

### 998. Enterprise Exit Principle

An enterprise should not assume that a vendor is replaceable merely because another provider offers a superficially similar AI service.

Practical replaceability requires sufficient:

* technical compatibility;
* data portability;
* security capability;
* privacy compatibility;
* operational capacity;
* contractual feasibility;
* financial feasibility;
* organizational capability.

### 999. AI Vendor Exit and Concentration Assurance Principle

AI vendor exit and concentration assurance should establish evidence that the organization can identify material dependencies, understand correlated exposure, execute appropriate migration or fallback, preserve required security and governance controls, and make informed decisions about residual dependency.

The strongest assurance combines:

* accurate dependency inventories;
* enterprise dependency mapping;
* concentration analysis;
* contractual analysis;
* portability testing;
* migration exercises;
* resilience testing;
* security validation;
* evidence review;
* accountable governance.

No individual artifact should be treated as sufficient assurance by itself.

### 1000. AI Vendor Exit and Concentration Governance Principle

AI vendor exit and concentration governance should be maintained as a continuous enterprise capability spanning procurement, architecture, security, privacy, AI governance, third-party risk, business continuity, legal, contracts, operations, and internal assurance.

The organization should continuously distinguish between:

* **Vendor Count ≠ Effective Diversification**
* **Alternative Provider ≠ Independent Resilience**
* **Termination Right ≠ Practical Exit Capability**
* **Portability Clause ≠ Tested Portability**
* **Data Export Right ≠ Usable Data Portability**
* **Migration Plan ≠ Executable Migration**
* **Exit Runbook ≠ Tested Exit Capability**
* **Provider Assurance ≠ Independent Assurance**
* **Certification ≠ Universal Security**
* **Questionnaire ≠ Control Effectiveness**
* **Contractual Protection ≠ Complete Resilience**
* **Concentration Threshold ≠ Universal Legal Requirement**
* **Internal Control ≠ Statutory Requirement**
* **Regulatory Mapping ≠ Regulatory Applicability**
* **Risk Acceptance ≠ Risk Elimination**
