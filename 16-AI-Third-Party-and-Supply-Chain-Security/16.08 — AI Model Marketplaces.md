# 16.08 — AI Model Marketplaces

## Part 1 — Foundations, Marketplace Classification, Model Acquisition, Provenance, Trust and Risk Architecture

### 1 — AI Model Marketplace Risk Definition

AI model marketplace risk is the risk arising from discovering, evaluating, acquiring, deploying, integrating, or relying on AI models distributed through platforms that aggregate models from multiple publishers, developers, organizations, or communities.

### 2 — Governance Objective

The objective of AI model marketplace governance is to enable controlled acquisition of AI models while managing risks involving:

* model provenance;
* publisher trust;
* artifact integrity;
* licensing;
* vulnerabilities;
* malicious models;
* dependencies;
* data;
* privacy;
* intellectual property;
* model behavior;
* supply-chain compromise;
* operational resilience.

### 3 — AI Model Marketplace Definition

An AI model marketplace is a platform through which users can discover, evaluate, obtain, deploy, or integrate AI models or related model artifacts from one or more publishers.

A marketplace may provide:

* model weights;
* APIs;
* model endpoints;
* deployment packages;
* adapters;
* fine-tuned models;
* embeddings;
* datasets;
* inference components.

### 4 — Marketplace Versus Model Repository

A model repository primarily stores or distributes model artifacts, while a marketplace may additionally provide:

* discovery;
* ranking;
* commercial transactions;
* deployment services;
* publisher information;
* usage interfaces.

The governance requirements depend on the actual capabilities of the platform.

### 5 — Marketplace Provider

The marketplace provider operates the platform through which models are discovered or distributed.

### 6 — Model Publisher

The model publisher is the party responsible for publishing or distributing a particular model.

### 7 — Model Maintainer

The maintainer is responsible for maintaining the model or associated project where that responsibility exists.

### 8 — Marketplace Consumer

The marketplace consumer is the organization or individual acquiring or using a model through the marketplace.

### 9 — Marketplace Operator Versus Model Publisher

The marketplace operator and model publisher may be different entities.

The organization should not automatically treat marketplace-level assurances as assurance over every individual model.

### 10 — Marketplace Trust Boundary

The marketplace creates a supply-chain trust boundary between:

**Organization → Marketplace → Model Publisher → Model Artifact**

Additional dependencies may exist beyond the publisher.

### 11 — Marketplace Dependency Mapping

Material marketplace dependencies should be mapped across:

* marketplace;
* publisher;
* model;
* artifact;
* runtime;
* dependencies;
* infrastructure.

### 12 — Marketplace Distribution Models

Model marketplaces may distribute models through:

* direct download;
* API;
* hosted inference;
* managed deployment;
* container;
* package;
* cloud integration.

### 13 — Hosted Marketplace Model

A hosted marketplace may provide inference without transferring model weights to the customer.

### 14 — Downloadable Marketplace Model

A downloadable marketplace allows customers to obtain model artifacts for local or cloud deployment.

### 15 — Marketplace API Model

An API marketplace may provide access to multiple models through standardized interfaces.

### 16 — Managed Deployment Marketplace

A marketplace may deploy models directly into customer-controlled or provider-controlled environments.

### 17 — Hybrid Marketplace

A marketplace may combine:

* downloadable artifacts;
* hosted models;
* APIs;
* deployment automation.

Each service type should be assessed according to its actual control boundary.

### 18 — Marketplace Model Criticality

Model criticality should reflect the potential impact of model failure, compromise, or unavailability.

### 19 — Marketplace Criticality

The marketplace itself may become a critical dependency when it is used for:

* model acquisition;
* production deployment;
* model updates;
* runtime access;
* dependency management.

### 20 — Model Risk Dimensions

Marketplace-acquired model risk should consider:

* provenance;
* integrity;
* security;
* licensing;
* privacy;
* data;
* reliability;
* behavior;
* dependencies;
* resilience.

### 21 — Marketplace Risk Dimensions

Marketplace-specific risks include:

* fraudulent publishers;
* counterfeit artifacts;
* malicious uploads;
* inaccurate metadata;
* misleading ratings;
* compromised accounts;
* insecure distribution;
* platform compromise.

### 22 — Publisher Identity Risk

The identity of a model publisher may be difficult to verify in open marketplaces.

### 23 — Publisher Verification

Organizations should determine whether the marketplace provides meaningful mechanisms for verifying publisher identity.

### 24 — Verified Publisher Status

Marketplace indicators such as verified accounts should not automatically be treated as comprehensive security assurance.

### 25 — Publisher Reputation

Publisher reputation may provide a useful risk signal but should not replace technical and security validation.

### 26 — Publisher History

Where relevant, organizations should consider:

* publication history;
* maintenance activity;
* security response;
* project continuity.

### 27 — Publisher Accountability

The organization should identify who is responsible for the model where such information is available.

### 28 — Anonymous Publisher Risk

Models published without meaningful attribution may present increased provenance and supportability uncertainty.

### 29 — Marketplace Metadata

Model marketplace metadata may include:

* model name;
* publisher;
* version;
* license;
* description;
* capabilities;
* intended use;
* limitations.

### 30 — Metadata Reliability

Marketplace metadata should be treated as information requiring validation where material to a risk decision.

### 31 — Model Identity

The organization should establish the exact model artifact being acquired.

### 32 — Model Version

The exact model version or immutable release identifier should be recorded where available.

### 33 — Model Variant

Organizations should distinguish among:

* base model;
* fine-tuned model;
* quantized model;
* distilled model;
* merged model;
* adapter-enhanced model.

### 34 — Model Artifact Identity

Model artifacts should be uniquely identifiable through available information such as:

* version;
* hash;
* signature;
* repository reference.

### 35 — Artifact Integrity

The organization should establish reasonable assurance that the acquired artifact has not been modified without authorization.

### 36 — Cryptographic Hash

Where a trustworthy hash is available, it may be used to verify artifact integrity.

### 37 — Digital Signature

Where trusted digital signatures are available, they should be verified according to organizational procedures.

### 38 — Artifact Comparison

Where appropriate, acquired artifacts may be compared against trusted reference artifacts.

### 39 — Marketplace Mirror Risk

Marketplace platforms may mirror or redistribute models originating elsewhere.

The organization should establish whether the marketplace copy corresponds to the intended source.

### 40 — Distribution Chain

The model distribution chain should be understood where material:

**Publisher → Marketplace → Acquisition → Internal Repository → Deployment**

### 41 — Distribution Integrity

Each material transition in the distribution chain should be subject to appropriate integrity controls.

### 42 — Model Provenance

Model provenance should establish, to a reasonable degree, where the model originated and how it was modified or distributed.

### 43 — Provenance Record

A provenance record may include:

* publisher;
* source repository;
* marketplace;
* version;
* artifact hash;
* acquisition date;
* modifications.

### 44 — Provenance Gap

A material inability to establish model provenance should be treated as a risk requiring appropriate disposition.

### 45 — Model Lineage

Where a marketplace model has been modified, lineage should identify relevant relationships:

**Base Model → Fine-Tuning → Adapter → Quantization → Marketplace Distribution**

### 46 — Derivative Model

A marketplace model may be derived from another model.

Organizations should assess the implications of derivative status for:

* licensing;
* provenance;
* security;
* behavior.

### 47 — Model Merge

Merged models should be evaluated for:

* component provenance;
* licensing;
* behavioral changes;
* security.

### 48 — Fine-Tuned Marketplace Model

Fine-tuned models should be assessed separately from their underlying base model where material.

### 49 — Adapter Dependency

Adapters and similar components should be treated as part of the model dependency chain.

### 50 — Quantized Model Dependency

Quantized variants should be uniquely identified because their behavior and security characteristics may differ from the original model.

### 51 — Model Wrapper Dependency

Wrappers may modify:

* input handling;
* output handling;

* tool access;
* model routing.

### 52 — Marketplace Runtime Dependency

A marketplace may provide or require a specific inference runtime.

### 53 — Runtime Identification

Material runtime dependencies should be identified.

### 54 — Runtime Version

### 55 — Software Dependency Identification

### 56 — Transitive Dependency

### 57 — Dependency Inventory

### 70 — Model File-Type Validation

### 73 — Runtime Isolation

Marketplace-acquired models should execute within appropriately controlled environments.

### 75 — Model Runtime Privilege

### 76 — Network Isolation

### 77 — Outbound Network Restriction

Unnecessary outbound network connectivity should be restricted.

### 78 — Inbound Exposure Restriction

Model endpoints should not be publicly exposed unless required and appropriately secured.
