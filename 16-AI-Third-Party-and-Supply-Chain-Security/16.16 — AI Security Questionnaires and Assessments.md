# 16.16 — AI Security Questionnaires and Assessments

## Part 1 — Foundations, Questionnaire Scope, Assessment Architecture, AI Risk Domains, Evidence and Governance

### 1. AI Security Questionnaire Definition

An AI security questionnaire is a structured set of questions used to collect information about an organization's, vendor's, product's, model's, service's, or technology component's security and governance practices.

AI security questionnaires can support third-party risk management, procurement, security assessment, AI governance, supply-chain assurance, and regulatory analysis.

---

### 2. AI Security Assessment Definition

An AI security assessment is a structured evaluation of security risks, controls, evidence, and residual exposure associated with an AI system, service, vendor, model, dataset, platform, or dependency.

A questionnaire collects information.

An assessment evaluates that information.

```text id="aiq16-01"

     |
     v

     |

     |

     |
     v

```

---

### 3. Questionnaire Versus Assessment

A questionnaire should not be treated as equivalent to an assessment.

A completed questionnaire may contain:

* Vendor assertions
* Descriptions
* Control responses
* Supporting references
* Evidence

The assessment determines whether those responses are sufficient for the intended risk decision.

---

### 4. Questionnaire Objective

The objective of an AI security questionnaire is to obtain structured information necessary to evaluate relevant security and governance risks.

---

### 5. Assessment Objective

The objective of an AI security assessment is to determine whether identified risks are appropriately understood and treated based on available evidence.

## Part 2 — Questionnaire Design, Assessment Methodology, Control Mapping, Evidence Requests and Response Validation

### 211. Questionnaire Design Principle

An AI security questionnaire should be designed as a risk-assessment instrument rather than as a generic list of security questions. The questionnaire should obtain information that enables the organization to determine whether the assessed AI service, provider, component, model, dataset, software dependency, or integration satisfies defined security, privacy, governance, resilience, and assurance expectations.

Questionnaire design should therefore be driven by the AI use case, trust boundaries, data flows, architecture, business criticality, regulatory exposure, supplier dependency, and potential impact of failure or misuse.

### 212. Question Relevance Principle

Every questionnaire question should have a defined assessment purpose.

A question should exist because its answer contributes to one or more of the following:

* risk identification;
* control evaluation;
* regulatory or contractual assessment;
* security architecture understanding;
* evidence validation;
* residual-risk determination;
* remediation planning;
* procurement decision-making;
* assurance planning.

Questions that do not contribute to an assessment objective should be removed, consolidated, or placed into an optional information section.

### 213. Risk-Based Question Selection

Question selection should be proportional to the assessed AI risk.

A low-risk internal AI service may require a limited questionnaire covering:

* data classification;
* access control;
* provider security;
* retention;
* incident management;
* basic AI functionality;
* business continuity.

A critical AI service may require substantially more detailed assessment of:

* model architecture;
* model and dataset provenance;
* supply-chain dependencies;
* privileged access;
* agent autonomy;
* tool execution;
* software dependencies;
* model-change management;
* security testing;
* incident response;
* resilience;
* regulatory applicability;
* independent assurance;
* exit capability.

### 214. Questionnaire Tiering Principle

The organization should establish questionnaire tiers corresponding to defined risk categories.

A practical structure may include:

```text
Standard Assessment
        |
        v
Enhanced Assessment
        |
        v
Critical AI Assessment
        |
        v
Specialized Technical / Regulatory Assessment
```

The tier should determine questionnaire depth, evidence requirements, reviewer expertise, validation activities, approval authority, and reassessment frequency.

### 215. Standard AI Questionnaire

A standard questionnaire should address baseline domains applicable to ordinary AI services.

Typical domains include:

* organization and service profile;
* information security governance;
* access control;
* data protection;
* privacy;
* vulnerability management;
* incident management;
* business continuity;
* supplier management;
* AI governance;
* model management;
* security testing;
* change management.

The organization should not assume that every question applies to every AI deployment.

### 216. Enhanced AI Questionnaire

An enhanced questionnaire should be used where the AI service introduces elevated risk.

Additional domains may include:

* sensitive or regulated data;
* high-impact business processes;
* external-facing AI;
* material model dependency;
* autonomous or semi-autonomous agents;
* external tool access;
* privileged integrations;
* extensive API exposure;
* critical infrastructure dependencies;
* cross-border processing;
* significant supplier concentration;
* material regulatory exposure.

### 217. Critical AI Assessment

A critical AI assessment should be substantially more evidence-driven than a standard questionnaire.

It should generally require:

* detailed architecture documentation;
* data-flow documentation;
* control evidence;
* security testing evidence;
* incident history;
* independent assurance where appropriate;
* model and software dependency information;
* resilience evidence;
* access-control evidence;
* change-management evidence;
* contractual commitments;
* documented limitations;
* identified exceptions;
* remediation commitments.

A critical designation should not be based solely on the provider's description of its service.

### 218. Question Dependency Principle

Questions should reflect dependencies between control domains.

For example:

```text
AI Service
    |
    +-- Data
    |     |
    |     +-- Classification
    |     +-- Retention
    |     +-- Access
    |
    +-- Model
    |     |
    |     +-- Provenance
    |     +-- Version
    |     +-- Change
    |
    +-- Integration
          |
          +-- API
          +-- Identity
          +-- Tools
```

A response regarding model security may therefore depend on answers concerning the underlying provider, software, data, identity architecture, and integration model.

### 219. Conditional Questioning

Questionnaires should use conditional logic where appropriate.

Examples include:

* If the provider uses customer data for model training, request additional data-governance information.
* If the service supports autonomous agents, request agent-control information.
* If external tools can be invoked, request tool authorization and execution-control information.
* If customer data leaves the European Economic Area, request applicable transfer and residency information.
* If models are downloadable, request artifact integrity and supply-chain information.
* If the provider uses subprocessors, request relevant subprocessor governance information.

Conditional questioning reduces unnecessary respondent burden while increasing assessment depth where risk requires it.

### 220. Question Traceability Principle

Each material question should be traceable to its assessment objective and, where applicable, to:

* an internal control;
* a policy requirement;
* a contractual requirement;
* a regulatory requirement;
* a recognized security framework;
* a risk scenario;
* an evidence requirement.

The traceability relationship should be documented rather than assumed.

### 221. Control-to-Question Mapping

A questionnaire should maintain a mapping between questions and assessed controls.

Example:

```text
Control
   |
   +-- Question
   |
   +-- Evidence Request
   |
   +-- Validation Method
   |
   +-- Assessment Result
   |
   +-- Finding
   |
   +-- Remediation
```

This prevents questionnaires from becoming disconnected from the organization's control framework.

### 222. Question Identifier Principle

Questions should have stable identifiers.

An identifier should remain associated with the same control objective even when wording changes.

Example:

```text
AI-Q-AC-001
AI-Q-DATA-004
AI-Q-MODEL-012
AI-Q-AGENT-006
AI-Q-SUPPLY-015
```

These identifiers are internal assessment taxonomy identifiers and should not be represented as statutory or regulatory identifiers.

### 223. Question Versioning

Questionnaires should be version-controlled.

Version changes may result from:

* new threats;
* new AI capabilities;
* architectural changes;
* regulatory changes;
* policy changes;
* lessons learned;
* assessment findings;
* new supplier risks;
* emerging attack techniques.

Historical questionnaire versions should remain identifiable so that assessment results can be interpreted in their original context.

### 224. Question Wording Principle

Questions should be specific enough to produce an assessable response.

Weak question:

> Do you have adequate AI security?

Stronger question:

> Describe the technical and organizational controls used to restrict privileged access to production AI model infrastructure and provide evidence demonstrating periodic access review.

The second formulation defines both the information requirement and the expected assurance direction.

### 225. Avoiding Ambiguous Questions

Questions containing vague terms should be avoided unless those terms are explicitly defined.

Examples of potentially ambiguous wording include:

* adequate;
* appropriate;
* robust;
* secure;
* regularly;
* significant;
* sensitive;
* critical;
* timely;
* comprehensive.

Where such terminology is necessary, the questionnaire should define the assessment interpretation.

### 226. Open-Ended Question Principle

Open-ended questions should be used when qualitative information is required.

Appropriate uses include:

* architecture descriptions;
* AI use-case descriptions;
* model-change processes;
* incident explanations;
* dependency descriptions;
* known limitations;
* compensating controls;
* planned remediation.

Open-ended responses should not automatically be treated as evidence of control effectiveness.

### 227. Closed-Ended Question Principle

Closed-ended questions are useful for structured assessment.

Examples include:

* Yes;
* No;
* Partially;
* Not Applicable;
* Unknown;
* Planned.

However, a "Yes" response should generally identify the supporting evidence when the question concerns a material control.

### 228. Yes/No Limitation

A binary answer should not be treated as sufficient assurance for complex controls.

For example:

> Does the organization perform AI security testing?

A "Yes" answer does not establish:

* testing scope;
* testing frequency;
* testing methodology;
* independence;
* coverage;
* findings;
* remediation;
* retesting;
* applicability to the assessed AI system.

Material questions should therefore be supported by evidence or additional explanatory fields.

### 229. Partial Compliance

Questionnaires should provide a mechanism for partial compliance.

Partial compliance may occur when:

* a control exists only for production;
* a control covers only certain models;
* a control applies only to certain regions;
* a control is implemented through a compensating mechanism;
* a control is partially deployed;
* implementation is in progress.

Partial compliance should not automatically be converted into full compliance.

### 230. Not Applicable Principle

"Not Applicable" should require justification where the underlying control is material.

For example:

```text
Question: Are AI model artifacts cryptographically verified before deployment?

Answer: N/A

Required justification:
The service is fully provider-hosted and no model artifacts are
downloaded, transferred, or deployed by the customer.
```

The assessment should distinguish genuine non-applicability from inability or unwillingness to answer.

### 231. Unknown Response

"Unknown" should be treated differently from "No."

An unknown response may indicate:

* insufficient provider visibility;
* missing documentation;
* decentralized ownership;
* inadequate internal knowledge;
* incomplete assessment;
* dependency on a fourth party.

Unknown responses should be evaluated according to the risk of the underlying control area.

### 232. Evidence-Required Questions

Questions concerning material security controls should identify expected evidence.

Examples:

```text
Question
   |
   v
Control Response
   |
   v
Evidence Request
   |
   v
Evidence Validation
   |
   v
Assessment Conclusion
```

This creates a distinction between assertion and assurance.

### 233. Evidence Request Principle

Evidence requests should be proportionate to the risk and control being assessed.

Possible evidence includes:

* policies;
* procedures;
* architecture diagrams;
* data-flow diagrams;
* access-review records;
* penetration-test reports;
* vulnerability reports;
* audit reports;
* certifications;
* control test results;
* incident summaries;
* business-continuity test results;
* model documentation;
* security testing results.

### 234. Evidence Specificity

Evidence requests should identify the expected scope.

Instead of:

> Provide your security documentation.

Use:

> Provide the most recent independent security assessment covering the production AI service, including its assessment period, scope, exclusions, material findings, and remediation status.

Specific requests improve evidence quality and reduce irrelevant submissions.

### 235. Evidence Currency

Evidence should be assessed for currency.

The acceptable age of evidence should depend on:

* control volatility;
* AI model change frequency;
* system criticality;
* threat environment;
* contractual requirements;
* regulatory expectations;
* assessment objectives.

Older evidence may remain relevant for stable governance controls but may be insufficient for rapidly changing technical environments.

### 236. Evidence Scope

Evidence should be checked against the scope of the assessed service.

A certification for a parent organization does not automatically establish that:

* the AI service is within scope;
* the relevant production environment is covered;
* the relevant region is covered;
* the relevant subsidiary is covered;
* the relevant model is covered;
* the relevant service version is covered.

### 237. Evidence Authenticity

Evidence should be reasonably attributable to the claimed source.

Assessment teams should consider:

* issuer identity;
* document integrity;
* reporting period;
* scope;
* authorization;
* version;
* publication source;
* consistency with other evidence.

Screenshots and self-generated documents may provide useful information but should not automatically be treated as independent assurance.

### 238. Evidence Traceability

Evidence should be traceable to:

* the question;
* the control;
* the assessed service;
* the relevant time period;
* the assessment conclusion.

A useful evidence record may contain:

```text
Evidence ID
   |
   +-- Question ID
   +-- Control ID
   +-- Provider / Service
   +-- Evidence Date
   +-- Scope
   +-- Source
   +-- Reviewer
   +-- Assessment Result
```

### 239. Evidence Conflict Principle

Conflicting evidence should be investigated.

Examples include:

* questionnaire states MFA is mandatory while configuration evidence shows exceptions;
* provider documentation states annual testing while the test report indicates a longer interval;
* contract prohibits training use while product documentation describes an optional training feature;
* architecture documentation shows no autonomous tools while technical configuration enables tool execution.

The assessment should not silently select the more favorable statement.

### 240. Evidence Gap Principle

An evidence gap should be explicitly recorded when a material assertion cannot be sufficiently validated.

Possible classifications include:

* evidence not provided;
* evidence incomplete;
* evidence outdated;
* evidence out of scope;
* evidence contradictory;
* evidence not independently validated;
* evidence unavailable due to confidentiality restrictions.

### 241. Compensating Evidence

Where primary evidence is unavailable, alternative evidence may be considered if it provides reasonable support for the assessment objective.

Examples may include:

* independent audit reports;
* control test results;
* technical configuration evidence;
* contractual commitments;
* external assurance reports;
* validated operational records.

Compensating evidence should not automatically be treated as equivalent to the unavailable primary evidence.

### 242. Assessment Methodology Principle

An assessment methodology should define how questionnaire responses are converted into risk conclusions.

A methodology should specify:

* scope;
* assessment criteria;
* evidence expectations;
* validation procedures;
* finding classification;
* risk evaluation;
* remediation requirements;
* approval authority;
* reassessment requirements.

### 246. Shared Responsibility Assessment

The questionnaire should identify which party is responsible for each relevant control.

Example:

| Control Area                    | Provider | Customer | Shared |

### 247. Control Applicability

Controls should be assessed for applicability before scoring or concluding.

Applicability may depend on:

* architecture;
* deployment model;
* data;
* AI capability;
* integration;
* user population;
* regulatory context;
* service configuration.

An inapplicable control should not be treated as a failed control.

### 248. Assessment Criteria

Assessment criteria should be defined before responses are reviewed.

Possible criteria include:

* control absent;
* control partially implemented;
* control implemented;
* control implemented and independently validated;
* control not applicable;
* evidence insufficient;
* control effectiveness uncertain.

The organization should document the meaning of each classification.

### 249. Control Effectiveness

Implementation of a documented control does not automatically establish effectiveness.

Assessment should consider:

* design;
* implementation;
* operating effectiveness;
* evidence;
* exceptions;
* historical performance;
* test results;
* scope.

### 250. Assessment Independence

Where assessment results may materially influence risk acceptance or procurement decisions, independence should be considered.

The individual performing the assessment should have sufficient objectivity and expertise to challenge unsupported assertions.

### 252. Coverage Gap

A coverage gap exists when a relevant control objective has no effective questionnaire or assessment mechanism.

Coverage gaps may arise because:

* the questionnaire was designed before a new AI capability was introduced;
* a new threat was not incorporated;
* a control was omitted;
* a third-party dependency was overlooked;
* the questionnaire focused on conventional cybersecurity only.

### 253. Redundant Question Analysis

Questionnaires should periodically be reviewed for redundant questions.

Multiple questions may request substantially identical information using different terminology.

Reducing redundancy improves:

* respondent efficiency;
* assessment consistency;
* data quality;
* review efficiency;
* questionnaire maintainability.

### 254. Question Quality Review

Questions should undergo quality review before deployment.

Review should evaluate:

* clarity;
* relevance;
* ambiguity;
* evidence requirements;
* applicability;
* duplication;
* technical accuracy;
* control mapping;
* regulatory mapping where applicable.

### 255. Technical Review

AI security questionnaires containing technical questions should be reviewed by personnel with appropriate technical expertise.

Relevant expertise may include:

* application security;
* cloud security;
* AI/ML security;
* identity;
* data security;
* API security;
* software supply-chain security;
* infrastructure security;

### 256. GRC Review

Questions involving legal or privacy obligations should be reviewed by appropriate legal or privacy functions where necessary.

The questionnaire should distinguish:

* legal requirements;
* contractual requirements;
* internal policy;
* recommended practices.

A questionnaire question should not transform an internal preference into a claimed statutory obligation.

* jurisdiction;

* relevant requirement;
* applicability basis;
* assessment purpose.

* NIST AI RMF;

### 262. Evidence Validation Method

Each material evidence item should have an identified validation method.

Possible methods include:

* document review;
* interview;
* configuration review;
* observation;
* technical testing;
* independent assurance review;
* sampling;
* cross-evidence comparison.

### 263. Interview Evidence

Interviews may provide valuable contextual information but should generally be distinguished from documentary or technical evidence.

An interview can explain:

* process ownership;
* control operation;
* exceptions;
* architectural decisions;
* operational practices.

The assessment should avoid treating an unsupported verbal statement as equivalent to independent technical evidence.

### 264. Configuration Evidence

Configuration evidence may provide stronger assurance for technical controls.

Examples include:

* MFA configuration;
* RBAC assignments;
* API gateway policies;
* network rules;
* logging configuration;
* DLP policies;
* model deployment settings;
* agent permissions.

Configuration evidence should be scoped to the assessed environment and time period.

### 265. Sampling Principle

Where full population testing is impractical, sampling may be used.

Sampling should consider:

* population;
* risk;
* materiality;
* selection method;
* sample size;
* exceptions;
* confidence requirements.

The limitations of sampling should be recorded.

### 266. Technical Testing

Technical testing may be required where questionnaire responses cannot establish sufficient assurance.

Examples include:

* API security testing;
* access-control testing;
* configuration validation;
* vulnerability assessment;
* model security testing;
* prompt-injection testing;
* agent authorization testing;
* network testing;
* logging validation.

Testing should be authorized and appropriately scoped.

### 267. AI-Specific Testing

AI assessments may require testing beyond conventional cybersecurity controls.

Examples include:

* prompt injection;
* jailbreak resistance;
* sensitive-data leakage;
* model extraction exposure;
* insecure tool invocation;
* unauthorized agent actions;
* retrieval poisoning;
* malicious document handling;
* output validation;
* unsafe code execution;
* model-change regression.

### 268. AI Behavioral Evidence

AI behavior should be assessed where security depends on model behavior.

Evidence may include:

* evaluation results;
* adversarial test results;
* red-team reports;
* benchmark results;
* safety test results;
* production monitoring;
* incident history.

Behavioral evidence should identify test conditions and limitations.

### 269. Model Documentation Review

Where applicable, assessment should review available model documentation.

Relevant information may include:

* model identity;
* version;
* intended use;
* limitations;
* known risks;
* training or fine-tuning information;
* evaluation methodology;
* security considerations;
* change history.

Documentation should be treated as an information source rather than automatically as independent assurance.

### 270. Model Change Assessment

Assessment should determine how model changes are governed.

Relevant questions include:

* Who authorizes model changes?
* How are changes communicated?
* Are model versions identifiable?
* Is regression testing performed?
* Can customers pin a version?
* Can changes alter security behavior?
* Can customers revert to a previous version?

### 271. Provider Change Transparency

Where material changes may affect the organization's risk, the assessment should determine whether the provider supplies sufficient change information.

Relevant changes may include:

* model replacement;
* model upgrade;
* architecture changes;
* new subprocessors;
* new data uses;
* new tool capabilities;
* new geographic processing;

* fine-tuning;

* analytics;
* product improvement.

* remediation;
* retesting.

A penetration-test report should not be interpreted as evidence that no vulnerabilities exist outside its defined scope.

### 298. Incident Management Assessment

Questionnaires should assess the provider's ability to:

* detect incidents;
* classify incidents;
* contain incidents;
* investigate;
* preserve evidence;
* notify affected parties;
* recover;
* perform root-cause analysis;
* implement corrective actions.

### 299. AI Incident Assessment

AI-specific incident questions may address:

* model compromise;
* model poisoning;
* prompt-injection exploitation;
* data leakage;
* unauthorized tool execution;
* agent misuse;
* malicious model artifacts;
* compromised dependencies;
* unsafe model changes.

### 300. Incident Notification Assessment

Where contractual or legal notification obligations exist, the assessment should identify:

* trigger;
* notification recipient;
* notification timeline;
* required information;
* escalation process;
* update obligations;
* evidence requirements.

The questionnaire should distinguish contractual notification commitments from statutory notification duties.

### 301. Business Continuity Assessment

Assessment should determine whether the AI service has continuity arrangements appropriate to its criticality.

Relevant areas include:

* redundancy;
* backup;
* recovery;
* alternate processing;
* provider resilience;
* capacity;
* dependency recovery;
* recovery testing.

### 302. AI Dependency Resilience

An AI service may depend on multiple providers.

For example:

```text
Application
    |
    +-- AI API
    |     |
    |     +-- Model Provider
    |
    +-- Vector Database
    |
    +-- Cloud Platform
    |
    +-- Identity Provider
    |
    +-- External Tools
```

Assessment should consider the resilience implications of these dependencies collectively.

### 303. Concentration Risk Assessment

Questionnaires should identify material concentration across:

* AI providers;
* cloud providers;
* model providers;
* software frameworks;
* repositories;
* datasets;
* identity providers;
* geographic regions.

A large number of services may still share the same underlying provider or technology dependency.

### 304. Fourth-Party Assessment

Where material services depend on subcontractors or other fourth parties, assessment should determine:

* who they are;
* what functions they perform;
* what data they process;
* where they operate;
* how they are governed;
* how changes are communicated.

The organization should avoid assuming that first-tier vendor assurance automatically covers every downstream dependency.

### 305. Contractual Assurance Assessment

Questionnaires should identify whether contracts address relevant assurance requirements.

Potential areas include:

* security obligations;
* confidentiality;
* data use;
* incident notification;
* audit rights;
* subprocessor governance;
* model changes;
* service continuity;
* deletion;
* exit;
* regulatory cooperation.

### 306. Audit Rights Assessment

Where audit rights are material, assessment should determine:

* whether audit rights exist;
* who may exercise them;
* scope;
* frequency;
* notice;
* confidentiality restrictions;
* remediation obligations.

A contractual audit right is not the same as having exercised the audit right.

### 307. Independent Assurance

Independent assurance may include:

* external audit reports;
* independent certifications;
* independent penetration testing;
* third-party assessments;
* regulatory assessments where applicable.

The assessment should determine whether the independent assurance covers the relevant service and control objectives.

### 308. Certification Scope

A certification should be assessed for:

* standard;
* certification period;
* certified entity;
* certified locations;
* certified services;
* exclusions;
* applicable controls.

Certification should not be interpreted as universal evidence of security across every product, model, deployment, or configuration.

### 309. Assurance Triangulation

Material assessment conclusions should be supported through multiple evidence sources where appropriate.

Example:

```text
Questionnaire Response
        |
        +---- Contract
        |
        +---- Technical Evidence
        |
        +---- Independent Assurance
        |
        +---- Operational Record
        |
        v
Assessment Conclusion
```

Triangulation reduces reliance on a single potentially incomplete source.

### 310. Response Validation Principle

Every material questionnaire response should be evaluated for:

* completeness;
* consistency;
* applicability;
* evidence;
* currency;
* scope;
* plausibility;
* contradictions.

Validation effort should be proportional to risk.

### 311. Completeness Validation

The assessment team should determine whether required questions were answered.

Incomplete responses should identify:

* unanswered questions;
* missing evidence;
* pending responses;
* unavailable information;
* excluded domains.

### 312. Consistency Validation

Responses should be compared across the questionnaire.

For example, a provider may state:

> Customer data is never retained.

Elsewhere, the questionnaire may identify:

> Logs are retained for 30 days.

The assessment should determine whether the statements refer to different data categories or represent a contradiction.

### 313. Cross-Document Validation

Questionnaire responses should be compared against:

* contracts;
* privacy documentation;
* security documentation;
* architecture;
* assurance reports;
* technical configuration;
* product documentation.

Material discrepancies should be investigated.

### 314. Cross-Question Validation

Related questions should be evaluated together.

For example:

```text
Q1: Are models version-controlled?
Q2: Can customers identify model versions?
Q3: Are model changes communicated?
Q4: Can customers revert changes?
```

A "Yes" response to Q1 does not automatically establish that Q2–Q4 are also satisfied.

### 315. Provider Assertion Principle

Provider assertions should be treated according to their evidentiary strength.

A provider statement may establish that the provider claims a control exists, but it may not independently establish:

* operating effectiveness;
* complete scope;
* absence of exceptions;
* current implementation;
* customer-specific configuration.

### 316. Marketing Claim Validation

Marketing statements should not automatically be treated as assessment evidence.

Examples include claims such as:

* secure by design;
* enterprise-grade security;
* privacy-first;
* zero-trust architecture;
* military-grade encryption;
* fully autonomous security.

Assessment should seek specific control evidence supporting material claims.

### 317. Unsupported Assertion

An assertion should be flagged as unsupported when the provider:

* provides no evidence;
* provides irrelevant evidence;
* provides expired evidence;
* provides evidence outside scope;
* refuses reasonable clarification;
* provides contradictory information.

### 318. Assessment Finding Principle

A finding should describe the difference between the expected condition and the observed condition.

A useful finding structure is:

```text
Criteria
   |
   v

   |
   v

   |
   v
Evidence
   |
   v
Required Action
```

### 319. Finding Classification

Findings may be classified according to organizational methodology, such as:

* critical;
* high;
* medium;
* low;
* observation;
* information.

The definitions should be documented and applied consistently.

### 320. Risk-Based Finding Classification

Finding severity should consider more than control status.

Relevant factors may include:

* business impact;
* data sensitivity;
* exploitability;
* exposure;
* privilege;
* AI autonomy;
* regulatory relevance;
* dependency criticality;
* likelihood;
* existing compensating controls.

### 321. AI-Specific Risk Factors

AI assessments should consider additional factors such as:

* model autonomy;
* tool permissions;
* external action capability;
* retrieval scope;
* training-data exposure;
* model supply-chain dependency;
* model change frequency;
* output influence;
* human oversight;
* attack surface.

### 322. Compensating Control Assessment

A failed or incomplete control may be partially mitigated by compensating controls.

Assessment should determine:

* what the compensating control does;
* how independently it operates;
* what risk remains;
* whether it is sustainable;
* whether it is tested.

### 323. Residual Risk

Assessment conclusions should distinguish inherent risk from residual risk.

```text
Inherent Risk
      |
      v
Controls
      |
      v
Compensating Controls
      |
      v
Residual Risk
```

A questionnaire should support this analysis rather than simply produce a compliance percentage.

### 324. Risk Acceptance

Where residual risk exceeds normal control expectations, formal risk acceptance may be required under organizational policy.

Risk acceptance should identify:

* risk;
* owner;
* rationale;
* compensating controls;
* expiration or review date;
* approval authority.

Risk acceptance does not eliminate the underlying risk.

### 325. Remediation Principle

Material findings should have documented remediation actions.

Each action should identify:

* owner;
* required outcome;
* target date;
* dependency;
* verification method;
* status.

### 326. Remediation Evidence

A remediation statement such as "fixed" should not automatically close a finding.

Closure should be supported by appropriate evidence, such as:

* configuration;
* test result;
* revised procedure;
* retest;
* independent validation.

### 327. Remediation Verification

Remediation verification should confirm that:

1. the corrective action was implemented;
2. the relevant control now operates as intended;
3. the original risk is reduced;
4. no material new risk was introduced.

### 328. Exception Management

Exceptions should be formally documented when assessment requirements cannot be met.

An exception should identify:

* requirement;
* reason;
* affected service;
* risk;
* compensating controls;
* owner;
* approval;
* expiry/review date.

### 329. Temporary Exception

Temporary exceptions should have defined expiration or review dates.

An exception without review can gradually become an undocumented permanent control weakness.

### 330. Assessment Approval

Assessment approval should be performed by an authorized role appropriate to the risk.

Potential approval roles include:

* service owner;
* business owner;
* security owner;
* risk owner;
* privacy owner;
* procurement;
* legal;
* executive risk authority.

The exact model should follow organizational governance.

Where multiple vendors are assessed, responses should be normalized sufficiently to permit meaningful comparison.

Assessment outputs may support vendor comparison based on defined organizational criteria.

* scope differences;

* residual risk;
* uncertainty.

### 382. Assessment Scoring Principle

Where scoring is used, scoring criteria should be explicitly defined.

A scoring model may evaluate:

* control implementation;
* evidence strength;
* control effectiveness;
* residual risk;
* material findings.

The organization should avoid presenting an arbitrary numerical score as objective security truth.

### 383. Weighted Scoring

Where different controls have different materiality, weighted scoring may be used.

```text
AI Security             25%

```

The weighting should reflect organizational risk priorities and should be documented.

### 384. Score Limitation

A high overall score should not automatically offset a critical unresolved weakness.

For example:

```text
Overall Score: High
Critical Agent Privilege Finding: Open
```

The critical finding may require separate treatment regardless of the aggregate score.

### 385. Hard-Fail Criteria

Organizations may establish specific conditions requiring escalation regardless of aggregate assessment score.

Potential examples include:

* unacceptable privileged access;
* material unresolved data exposure;
* inability to meet mandatory contractual requirements;
* critical security vulnerability;
* unacceptable incident history;
* absence of required regulatory controls.

The criteria should be formally approved rather than improvised during an individual assessment.

### 386. Scoring Calibration

Scoring should be periodically calibrated against historical assessment results.

Calibration can identify:

* inconsistent reviewers;
* overly generous scoring;
* overly restrictive scoring;
* questions with weak discrimination;
* scoring categories that do not correspond to actual risk.

### 387. Assessment Consistency

Organizations should periodically compare assessments performed by different reviewers to determine whether materially similar evidence produces materially similar conclusions.

### 388. Reviewer Guidance

Reviewer guidance should explain how to interpret common response patterns.

Guidance may address:

* "Yes" without evidence;
* "Partially implemented";
* "N/A";
* "Planned";
* "Inherited control";
* "Provider-managed";

### 389. Inherited Controls

Where a provider relies on inherited controls, the assessment should determine:

* what control is inherited;
* from whom;
* what environment it covers;
* whether it applies to the assessed service;

* interview;
* technical observation.

### 393. Finding Ownership

An assessment should only be closed when defined closure conditions are satisfied.

Possible conditions include:

* material questions answered;

### 400. Questionnaire and Assessment Governance Principle

AI security questionnaires should function as structured evidence-collection and risk-assessment mechanisms integrated with third-party risk management, procurement, security architecture, privacy, legal, compliance, and enterprise risk processes. Questionnaire completion alone should not be treated as equivalent to control assurance, and a favorable response should not substitute for evidence where the assessed risk requires validation.
