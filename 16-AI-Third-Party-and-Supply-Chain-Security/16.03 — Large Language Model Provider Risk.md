# 16.03 — Large Language Model Provider Risk

## Part 1 — Foundations, Provider Risk, LLM Dependency, Trust Boundaries and Governance

### 1. Large Language Model Provider Risk

Large Language Model Provider Risk is the risk that an organization is exposed to security, privacy, operational, compliance, legal, financial, or business consequences because it depends on an external provider for access to a large language model (LLM).

An LLM provider may supply a model through an API, hosted application, cloud service, managed platform, embedded capability, model marketplace, or other service arrangement. The organization may therefore depend on the provider for model availability, model behavior, security controls, data processing, model updates, infrastructure, logging, support, and other capabilities.

The risk does not arise simply because an organization uses an LLM. It arises from the **external dependency and the organization's reliance on the provider's capabilities and controls**.

A provider may be technically sophisticated and still create material organizational risk if the organization's use case, data, architecture, contractual position, or operational dependency is not appropriately governed.

### 2. LLM Provider Risk Objective

The primary objective of LLM provider risk management is to ensure that the organization understands, evaluates, controls, monitors, and governs its dependence on external LLM providers throughout the service lifecycle.

The governance objective should address:

* what model or service is being consumed;
* who provides it;
* how the organization accesses it;
* what data is sent to it;
* where processing occurs;
* how the provider uses the data;
* what security controls protect the service;
* how model behavior is governed;
* how model changes are controlled;
* what downstream providers are involved;
* what contractual protections exist;
* what happens when the provider experiences an incident;
* what happens when the provider changes or withdraws the service;
* whether the organization can migrate away from the provider;
* whether multiple organizational services depend on the same provider;
* and whether residual risk is acceptable to the appropriate risk owner.

### 4. Direct and Indirect LLM Provider Dependency

A **direct LLM provider dependency** exists when the organization contracts with or directly consumes the provider's LLM service.

An **indirect dependency** exists when the organization consumes an LLM through another service that itself depends on an external model provider.

For example:

```text

     |
     v

     |

```

Indirect dependencies can make risk assessment more difficult because the organization may have limited visibility into the underlying provider, model, infrastructure, or subprocessors.

### 5. LLM Provider Risk Is Use-Case Dependent

The risk associated with an LLM provider cannot be determined solely by the provider's reputation, size, certification portfolio, or published security documentation.

The same provider can present different levels of risk depending on how the organization uses the service.

For example:

* public marketing content may create relatively limited confidentiality risk;
* internal business analysis may create greater confidentiality risk;
* processing customer information may create privacy and regulatory risk;
* processing confidential source code may create intellectual-property and security risk;
* security operations may create operational and integrity risks;
* autonomous agents connected to enterprise systems may create significantly greater authorization and impact risks.

Therefore:

**Provider risk must be assessed in the context of the specific use case, data, architecture, permissions, and business process.**

### 7. LLM Provider Classification

An organization should classify LLM providers according to the service actually consumed rather than relying only on vendor marketing terminology.

Potential classifications include:

* hosted LLM provider;
* API-based LLM provider;
* cloud-hosted model service;
* AI SaaS provider using third-party LLMs;
* foundation model provider;
* open-weight model provider;
* model marketplace provider;
* model aggregator;
* AI platform provider;
* managed AI service provider;
* embedded LLM provider;
* or hybrid provider.

A provider may belong to more than one category.

The classification should identify the actual technical and contractual dependency.

### 8. Provider Identity and Accountability

The organization should establish who is accountable for the external LLM service.

The provider assessment should identify, where relevant:

* legal entity;
* service provider;
* model provider;
* infrastructure provider;
* relevant subprocessors;
* service operator;
* model publisher;
* contractual counterparty;
* support organization;
* security contact;
* incident contact;
* privacy contact;
* and escalation authority.

A common governance weakness occurs when the organization knows the name of the AI product but cannot identify the underlying entities responsible for the model, infrastructure, processing, or support.

### 10. Model Substitution Risk

An organization may believe that it has approved a specific model while the provider may route requests to different models, versions, regions, or infrastructure according to service configuration.

Model substitution can create differences in:

* security characteristics;
* output behavior;
* data processing;
* performance;
* latency;
* availability;
* content filtering;
* tool-calling behavior;
* context handling;
* privacy characteristics;
* and regulatory risk.

Therefore, model substitution capability should be understood and governed where it can materially affect the organization's risk.

### 13. Provider Data Processing

The organization should understand how the provider processes information submitted to the service.

Relevant questions may include:

* Is submitted information retained?
* For how long?
* Is it stored persistently?
* Is it used for service operation?
* Is it used for model training?
* Is it used for model improvement?
* Is it used for abuse monitoring?
* Is it accessible by provider personnel?
* Is it processed by subprocessors?
* Is it replicated?
* Is it backed up?
* Can the organization delete it?
* Can the organization obtain evidence of deletion?

These questions are governance and due-diligence considerations. Whether a particular processing activity is legally permitted or legally required to be controlled depends on the applicable law, contractual arrangement, data type, jurisdiction, and role of the parties.

### 14. Provider Training-Use Risk

A significant LLM provider risk is the possibility that organizational information may be used for provider model training, fine-tuning, evaluation, service improvement, or other secondary purposes.

The organization should determine:

* whether submitted data may be used for training;
* whether the setting is configurable;
* whether the configuration is tenant-specific;
* whether the restriction is contractual;
* whether the provider's personnel can access the data;
* whether the restriction applies to all service tiers;
* whether the restriction applies to subprocessors;
* and whether the organization can obtain evidence supporting the stated restriction.

A provider's statement that customer data is "not used for training" should be evaluated against the actual service configuration and contractual terms.

### 15. Data Classification Before LLM Use

The organization should classify information before permitting it to be submitted to an external LLM provider.

Potential categories include:

* public;
* internal;
* confidential;
* restricted;
* personal data;
* sensitive personal data where applicable;
* regulated information;
* authentication information;
* credentials and secrets;
* proprietary source code;
* intellectual property;
* security-sensitive information;
* and legally privileged information where applicable.

The classification should determine whether the data may be sent to the provider and under what conditions.

### 16. Data Minimization for LLM Services

Organizations should avoid sending unnecessary information to external LLM providers.

Data minimization may include:

* removing unnecessary identifiers;
* reducing prompt content;
* filtering sensitive fields;
* masking confidential information;
* pseudonymization where appropriate;
* redacting credentials;
* limiting retrieved documents;
* restricting tool parameters;
* and limiting context to what is necessary for the approved task.

The objective is to reduce the potential impact of provider compromise, unauthorized access, inappropriate retention, accidental disclosure, or unintended secondary use.

A prompt should not automatically be treated as harmless text.

A prompt may contain:

* confidential business information;
* personal data;
* source code;
* credentials;
* architecture information;
* security configurations;
* customer information;
* proprietary algorithms;
* or information retrieved from internal repositories.

In an enterprise AI architecture:

### 18. Output as a Security Boundary

LLM output should also be treated as an information-security boundary.

Outputs may contain:

* confidential information;
* personal information;
* generated code;
* security recommendations;
* privileged instructions;
* malicious content;
* hallucinated information;
* sensitive data retrieved through connected systems;
* or instructions capable of influencing downstream systems.

Output handling should therefore be governed according to the use case.

### 19. LLM Hallucination Risk

An LLM may generate inaccurate, incomplete, misleading, or fabricated information.

This creates risks when outputs are used for:

* legal analysis;
* financial decisions;
* security operations;
* customer communications;
* software development;
* compliance decisions;
* medical or safety-related processes;
* automated business decisions;
* or other high-impact activities.

The organization should determine whether human validation, grounding, retrieval, deterministic controls, or other validation mechanisms are required for the intended use.

### 21. Vector and Embedding Provider Dependency

Where an external provider supplies embeddings or vector-processing capabilities, the organization may have an additional third-party dependency.

The assessment should consider:

* embedding provider;
* embedding model;
* vector database;
* storage location;
* access controls;
* data retention;
* indexing;
* deletion;
* tenant isolation;
* encryption;
* retrieval security;
* and migration capability.

A change in the embedding model can affect retrieval behavior even when the primary LLM remains unchanged.

### 22. Tool and Agent Dependency

Modern LLM applications may allow models to invoke external tools or operate as agents.

Examples include:

* databases;
* APIs;
* email systems;
* ticketing platforms;
* cloud services;
* file repositories;
* code execution environments;
* identity systems;
* financial systems;
* and enterprise applications.

The risk therefore increases when the provider-controlled model can influence external actions.

### 23. Human Oversight

Human oversight should be proportionate to the impact of the LLM use case.

The organization should establish where human approval is required before:

* external communication;
* security action;
* financial transaction;
* privileged administrative action;
* production code deployment;
* access modification;
* sensitive data disclosure;
* or other material business action.

Human oversight should be meaningful rather than merely nominal.

A human who cannot reasonably understand, review, or reject the proposed action does not necessarily provide effective control.

### 24. LLM Provider Authentication

The organization should establish secure authentication between its applications and the external LLM provider.

Potential controls include:

* API keys;
* OAuth or equivalent mechanisms;
* workload identity;
* service identities;
* mutual authentication where supported;
* short-lived credentials;
* centralized secrets management;
* credential rotation;
* and access restrictions.

Credentials should not be embedded directly in source code, prompts, notebooks, client-side applications, or publicly accessible repositories.

### 25. Authorization and Least Privilege

Access to the LLM provider should follow least-privilege principles.

The organization should control:

* who can invoke the service;
* which applications may invoke it;
* which models may be accessed;
* which endpoints may be used;
* what data may be submitted;
* what tools may be invoked;
* what administrative functions are available;
* and which environments may use the service.

A user authorized to use an LLM does not automatically need authorization to use every model, dataset, tool, or administrative capability associated with the provider.

### 26. Provider API Security

Where an LLM is consumed through an API, the organization should assess:

* authentication;
* authorization;
* TLS;
* endpoint security;
* API keys;
* token management;
* rate limits;
* quotas;
* request validation;
* response validation;
* error handling;
* logging;
* monitoring;
* and abuse detection.

API security is particularly important because compromise of the organization's API credentials may provide unauthorized access to the external AI service and potentially expose sensitive data or generate significant financial charges.

### 27. Rate Limiting and Resource Abuse

LLM services can create substantial computational and financial consumption.

Risks may include:

* excessive token consumption;
* automated abuse;
* denial of service;
* credential compromise;
* denial-of-wallet attacks;
* uncontrolled agent loops;
* excessive tool invocation;
* and runaway workloads.

Controls may include:

* request quotas;
* token limits;
* concurrency limits;
* rate limits;
* budget thresholds;
* anomaly detection;
* workload restrictions;
* and automated shutdown or suspension mechanisms.

### 28. Provider Availability Risk

An organization may become operationally dependent on the availability of an external LLM provider.

Availability risk may result from:

* provider outage;
* regional outage;
* API failure;
* network failure;
* authentication failure;
* capacity limitation;
* service degradation;
* model retirement;
* provider incident;
* or dependency failure.

The impact depends on how deeply the LLM is embedded in the organization's business processes.

### 29. Business Criticality of LLM Dependency

The organization should classify LLM dependencies according to business criticality.

A useful assessment may consider:

* affected business service;
* number of users;
* transaction volume;
* revenue dependency;
* regulatory significance;
* customer impact;
* security impact;
* data sensitivity;
* operational dependency;
* recovery requirements;
* and availability of alternatives.

An LLM used for an optional productivity assistant should generally not be governed identically to an LLM embedded in a critical customer-facing or security operation.

### 31. Provider Concentration Assessment

Concentration assessment should consider more than the number of vendors.

Relevant dimensions include:

* number of providers;
* percentage of AI workloads per provider;
* critical services dependent on each provider;
* common underlying cloud infrastructure;
* common foundation models;
* common APIs;
* common identity systems;
* common security services;
* common geographic regions;
* common jurisdictions;
* and common fourth parties.

Two apparently separate AI services may still have a common underlying dependency.

### 32. Fourth-Party LLM Dependencies

An LLM provider may itself depend on:

* cloud infrastructure providers;
* data-center operators;
* model licensors;
* subprocessors;
* security providers;
* observability providers;
* content-filtering services;
* storage providers;
* networking providers;
* or other technology suppliers.

These dependencies may become relevant when they materially affect the organization's risk.

The organization should seek sufficient visibility into material fourth-party dependencies rather than assuming that the direct vendor is the only relevant dependency.

### 33. Provider Security Assurance

The organization should evaluate available evidence regarding the provider's security controls.

Evidence may include:

* security documentation;
* independent audit reports;
* certifications;
* penetration-testing summaries;
* vulnerability-management information;
* incident-management documentation;
* privacy documentation;
* architectural information;
* contractual commitments;
* and other relevant assurance evidence.

However:

**Provider evidence is evidence about the provider's controls; it is not automatically evidence that the organization's own implementation is secure.**

### 34. Scope of Provider Assurance

Provider assurance should be evaluated for:

* scope;
* service covered;
* geographic coverage;
* reporting period;
* control objectives;
* exclusions;
* exceptions;
* complementary customer controls;
* testing methodology;
* independence;
* and evidence currency.

For example, a certification covering a provider's general corporate environment does not automatically establish that every specific AI service, configuration, model, endpoint, or customer deployment is secure.

### 35. Provider Security Claims

Provider marketing statements should not automatically be treated as independent assurance.

Claims such as:

* "enterprise-grade security";
* "secure by design";
* "privacy-first";
* "zero trust";
* "military-grade security";
* or "fully compliant"

should be evaluated against objective evidence, contractual commitments, technical documentation, and the organization's actual configuration.

Marketing language may provide useful information, but it should not replace evidence-based risk assessment.

### 36. Provider Incident Risk

An incident affecting the LLM provider may affect the organization even when the organization's own infrastructure has not been directly compromised.

Examples include:

* provider credential compromise;
* unauthorized provider access;
* model compromise;
* data exposure;
* provider API compromise;
* malicious model update;
* supply-chain compromise;
* provider ransomware;
* cloud infrastructure failure;
* security-control failure;
* or compromise of a provider subprocessor.

The organization should understand how such events would affect its data, services, users, and business processes.

### 37. Incident Notification and Cooperation

The contractual and operational relationship should establish appropriate mechanisms for incident notification and cooperation.

Relevant considerations may include:

* notification channels;
* responsible contacts;
* severity classification;
* required information;
* investigation cooperation;
* evidence preservation;
* forensic cooperation;
* containment support;
* remediation information;
* root-cause reporting;
* and recovery coordination.

Whether a provider notification satisfies an organization's external legal reporting obligation depends on the applicable legal requirements and should not be assumed.

### 38. LLM Provider Change Risk

External LLM providers may change:

* models;
* versions;
* APIs;
* endpoints;
* pricing;
* data-processing terms;
* retention policies;
* security controls;
* content filters;
* safety mechanisms;
* model behavior;
* infrastructure;
* subprocessors;
* geographic processing;
* or service availability.

The organization should therefore establish a mechanism for identifying changes that could materially affect risk.

### 39. Model Behavior Change

A model can remain available while its behavior changes.

Behavioral changes may affect:

* accuracy;
* refusal behavior;
* output format;
* safety controls;
* hallucination patterns;
* instruction following;
* tool invocation;
* prompt sensitivity;
* security characteristics;
* latency;
* and application compatibility.

A provider's version or change-management process should therefore be evaluated according to the criticality of the organization's use case.

### 40. LLM Version Governance

Where practical and appropriate, organizations should maintain sufficient model-version information to support:

* change analysis;
* incident investigation;
* application testing;
* rollback decisions;
* auditability;
* regulatory evidence;
* and reproducibility.

If the provider does not permit customer-controlled version pinning, the organization should understand the resulting risk and implement compensating controls where appropriate.

### 44. LLM Provider Exit Dependency

The organization should understand how difficult it would be to replace the provider.

Potential lock-in may arise from:

* proprietary APIs;
* proprietary model behavior;
* prompt dependencies;
* proprietary embeddings;
* provider-specific tool calling;
* proprietary agent frameworks;
* provider-specific safety controls;
* proprietary logging;
* proprietary evaluation systems;
* proprietary data formats;
* application integration;
* user workflows;
* and accumulated operational knowledge.

Exit risk should therefore be assessed before the dependency becomes critical.

### 47. Three Lines and LLM Provider Risk

The Three Lines model can provide a governance structure for LLM provider risk.

The first line generally manages and operates the service.

The second line provides risk, compliance, security, privacy, or governance oversight.

The third line provides independent internal assurance.

The exact organizational implementation may differ, but accountability should remain clear.

### 50. LLM Provider Governance Principle

**AI-LLM-050 — LLM Provider Governance Principle**

An organization should govern an external LLM provider as a material third-party dependency when the provider can affect organizational data, systems, business processes, security, privacy, compliance, or operational resilience.

Effective governance should establish the provider and model identity, understand direct and indirect dependencies, map trust boundaries and data flows, classify the use case and data, evaluate provider security and assurance evidence, govern authentication and authorization, assess model and service change risk, consider concentration and exit dependencies, establish accountable ownership, and maintain an evidence-based risk decision.

Provider documentation, certifications, contractual commitments, and security claims should be evaluated according to their scope, currency, independence, and applicability to the organization's actual service and configuration. An external LLM provider's controls do not eliminate the organization's responsibility to govern its own implementation and residual risk.

### Core Principles — Part 1

1. **LLM Provider Dependency Principle** — An external LLM provider is an organizational dependency and should be governed according to the impact of that dependency.
2. **Use-Case Context Principle** — Provider risk depends on how the organization uses the service, what data it processes, and what authority it receives.
3. **Trust-Boundary Principle** — The boundary between organizational systems and the provider should be understood and documented.
4. **Data-Flow Principle** — Prompts, retrieved information, metadata, outputs, logs, and tool parameters may all form part of the AI data flow.
5. **Model-Identity Principle** — The organization should maintain sufficient information to identify the model and service relevant to the risk assessment.
6. **Provider-Assurance Principle** — Provider assurance evidence should be evaluated for scope, currency, independence, applicability, and limitations.
7. **Least-Privilege Principle** — Access to external LLM services and connected tools should be limited to authorized users, applications, data, and actions.
8. **Change-Risk Principle** — Provider, API, model, infrastructure, data-processing, and service changes can alter the organization's risk profile.
9. **Concentration Principle** — Multiple AI applications may still share the same provider or underlying dependency.
10. **Exit-Capability Principle** — A contractual termination or portability right does not by itself demonstrate practical ability to exit the provider.
11. **Accountability Principle** — Provider outsourcing does not automatically transfer ownership of the organization's residual risk.
12. **Evidence Principle** — Provider claims should be supported and evaluated through appropriate evidence rather than accepted solely as assertions.
13. **Governance-Decision Principle** — LLM provider approval should be based on documented risk criteria, applicable requirements, evidence, and accountable risk ownership.

## Part 2 — Provider Due Diligence, Data Governance, Security Controls, Model Assurance and Contractual Risk

### 51. LLM Provider Due Diligence

LLM provider due diligence is the structured process of evaluating whether an external LLM provider can support the organization's intended use case within its defined security, privacy, operational, legal, regulatory, and risk requirements.

Due diligence should evaluate the **actual service being consumed**, not merely the provider's overall corporate security posture.

The assessment should consider:

* provider identity;
* service identity;
* model identity;
* use case;
* data processed;
* processing locations;
* technical architecture;
* security controls;
* privacy controls;
* model governance;
* incident management;
* resilience;
* contractual protections;
* fourth-party dependencies;
* concentration;
* portability;
* exit capability;
* and available assurance evidence.

### 52. Due-Diligence Scope

The scope of due diligence should be proportional to the organization's dependency and risk.

A higher level of scrutiny may be appropriate where the LLM:

* processes confidential or regulated information;
* supports critical business services;
* influences material decisions;
* performs security operations;
* accesses privileged systems;
* invokes external tools;
* operates autonomously;
* processes large volumes of personal information;
* or creates significant provider concentration.

The assessment should document why the selected scope is appropriate.

### 53. Provider Corporate Due Diligence

Corporate due diligence may consider:

* legal entity;
* ownership;
* organizational structure;
* operating jurisdictions;
* financial stability;
* service maturity;
* security governance;
* incident history where reliable information is available;
* material litigation or regulatory issues where relevant;
* acquisition or ownership-change risk;
* strategic direction;
* and service continuity.

Corporate information should be treated as one component of the overall assessment rather than as evidence of technical security.

### 54. Provider Service Due Diligence

The specific service should be assessed separately from the provider's corporate environment.

Relevant characteristics include:

* service architecture;
* API endpoints;
* model access;
* supported models;
* hosting arrangement;
* geographic processing;
* service dependencies;
* authentication;
* authorization;
* logging;
* monitoring;
* data retention;
* support;
* and service-level commitments.

A provider may operate multiple AI services with materially different architectures and controls.

### 55. Model Capability Assessment

The organization should understand the capabilities of the model relevant to the intended use.

Relevant characteristics may include:

* language capabilities;
* multimodal capabilities;
* context window;
* structured output;
* function calling;
* tool use;
* code generation;
* retrieval integration;
* agentic capabilities;
* memory;
* fine-tuning;
* system instruction support;
* and external service integration.

Capabilities that increase the model's authority or connectivity may also increase the security impact of misuse or compromise.

### 56. Model Capability Versus Model Risk

Model capability should not be treated as synonymous with model quality or security.

A more capable model may introduce additional risk when it can:

* access more data;
* invoke more tools;
* execute more complex workflows;
* generate executable code;
* interact with external systems;
* or operate with less human intervention.

### 57. Model Evaluation Evidence

The organization should evaluate available evidence regarding model performance and security characteristics.

Evidence may include:

* provider evaluations;
* independent evaluations;
* benchmark results;
* red-team findings;
* security testing;
* robustness testing;
* safety evaluations;
* adversarial testing;
* application-specific testing;
* and internal validation.

Benchmark performance should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of security or suitability for the organization's particular use case.

### 58. Model Behavioral Baseline

For material use cases, the organization should establish an appropriate behavioral baseline.

The baseline may include:

* representative prompts;
* expected outputs;
* refusal behavior;
* structured-output behavior;
* tool-calling behavior;
* retrieval behavior;
* security restrictions;
* response latency;
* accuracy expectations;
* and known limitations.

The baseline can provide a reference point for detecting material provider or model changes.

### 59. Model Change Management

The organization should understand how the provider manages model changes.

Relevant questions include:

* Are model versions explicitly identified?
* Can versions be pinned?
* How are deprecated models communicated?
* How much notice is provided?
* Can the provider automatically substitute models?
* Are material behavior changes communicated?
* Are customers provided with testing opportunities?
* Can customers revert to an earlier version?
* What happens when a model reaches end-of-life?

The appropriate controls depend on the criticality of the use case.

### 60. Model Retirement Risk

A provider may retire a model even when the organization's application continues to depend on it.

Model retirement can create:

* availability risk;
* compatibility risk;
* security regression;
* behavioral changes;
* testing requirements;
* migration costs;
* retraining or prompt-engineering requirements;
* and operational disruption.

Model lifecycle risk should therefore be incorporated into provider governance.

### 62. Data Classification and Permitted Use

The organization should define which categories of data may be processed by the provider.

For example:

| Data Category                | Potential Governance Treatment                          |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Public information           | May be permitted subject to normal controls             |
| Internal information         | May require approved provider configuration             |
| Confidential information     | May require enhanced contractual and technical controls |
| Personal data                | Requires applicable privacy assessment and controls     |
| Regulated information        | Requires specific legal and control assessment          |
| Credentials/secrets          | Generally prohibited from prompt submission             |
| Highly sensitive information | May require restricted or dedicated processing          |

These are governance examples rather than universal legal classifications.

### 63. Data Minimization Control

The organization should implement data minimization before transmitting information to an external LLM.

Potential controls include:

* field filtering;
* redaction;
* masking;
* pseudonymization;
* tokenization;
* context reduction;
* retrieval filtering;
* document filtering;
* and sensitive-data detection.

Data minimization should occur as close as practical to the point where information leaves the organization's controlled environment.

### 64. Prompt Inspection

Where appropriate, the organization may inspect prompts before they are transmitted to an external provider.

Prompt inspection can identify:

* credentials;
* personal information;
* confidential information;
* source code;
* regulated information;
* prohibited content;
* and unauthorized data.

However, prompt inspection itself may create privacy or security considerations and should be designed accordingly.

### 65. Provider Data Retention

The organization should determine how long provider systems retain submitted information.

The assessment should consider:

* request retention;
* output retention;
* logs;
* backups;
* abuse-monitoring records;
* support records;
* diagnostic information;
* and data stored by subprocessors.

The organization should understand whether retention periods differ between service tiers, regions, APIs, and product configurations.

### 66. Data Deletion

Where deletion is required by contract, policy, or applicable law, the organization should understand how deletion is performed.

Relevant considerations include:

* primary storage deletion;
* backup deletion;
* replicated copies;
* caches;
* logs;
* derived data;
* embeddings;
* evaluation data;
* support records;
* and subprocessor deletion.

A provider's deletion statement should be evaluated according to its scope and evidence.

### 67. Data Return and Portability

The organization should determine what information can be retrieved when the relationship ends.

Potentially relevant information includes:

* prompts;
* outputs;
* conversation history;
* metadata;
* configurations;
* evaluation datasets;
* fine-tuning data;
* fine-tuned models where applicable;
* embeddings;
* logs;
* and audit records.

The organization should distinguish between **theoretical export capability** and **usable portability**.

### 68. Provider Access to Customer Data

The organization should understand whether provider personnel can access customer information.

Relevant controls may include:

* privileged-access management;
* just-in-time access;
* approval workflows;
* administrative logging;
* access reviews;
* segregation of duties;
* monitoring;
* and support-access restrictions.

The assessment should also consider whether access can occur through subprocessors or other downstream providers.

### 69. Tenant Isolation

For multi-tenant LLM services, the organization should understand how customer environments are separated.

Relevant controls may include:

* logical isolation;
* access controls;
* encryption;
* tenant identifiers;
* storage separation;
* request routing;
* administrative separation;
* and testing of isolation controls.

The exact technical architecture may vary by provider.

### 70. Provider Encryption

The organization should understand encryption controls applicable to:

* data in transit;
* data at rest;
* backups;
* logs;
* model-related artifacts;
* credentials;
* and administrative channels.

Where supported and appropriate, the organization should evaluate:

* key ownership;
* customer-managed keys;
* key rotation;
* key access;
* cryptographic standards;
* and separation of duties.

### 71. Provider Identity and Access Management

Provider IAM should be assessed where information is available and material to the risk.

Relevant areas include:

* workforce authentication;
* MFA;
* privileged access;
* role separation;
* administrative access;
* service identities;
* access reviews;
* credential lifecycle;
* session management;
* and privileged activity logging.

Customer IAM and provider IAM should be considered separately because the organization controls the former but generally does not directly administer the latter.

### 72. Customer-Side IAM

The organization should control its own access to the LLM provider.

Controls may include:

* centralized identity;
* SSO;
* MFA;
* RBAC;
* PAM;
* service accounts;
* API credential management;
* access reviews;
* separation of duties;
* and rapid access revocation.

User access should be linked to legitimate business need.

### 73. API Credential Governance

API credentials should be treated as security-sensitive credentials.

Controls may include:

* centralized secrets management;
* short-lived credentials;
* rotation;
* scoped permissions;
* environment separation;
* usage monitoring;
* anomaly detection;
* revocation;
* and prevention of credential exposure in source code.

Credential compromise can create both information-security and financial risk.

### 74. API Gateway Controls

Where feasible, organizations should place an API gateway or equivalent control layer between internal applications and external LLM services.

Potential functions include:

* authentication;
* authorization;
* rate limiting;
* data-loss prevention;
* request inspection;
* response inspection;
* logging;
* routing;
* provider abstraction;
* cost control;
* and policy enforcement.

A gateway can provide an additional governance boundary without eliminating the underlying provider dependency.

### 75. Network Security

The organization should assess network connectivity between its systems and the provider.

Relevant controls may include:

* TLS;
* egress filtering;
* DNS controls;
* firewall policy;
* private connectivity where supported;
* proxy controls;
* API gateway enforcement;
* network segmentation;
* and monitoring.

Unrestricted outbound access to external AI services can create shadow-AI and data-exfiltration risks.

### 76. Shadow AI Risk

Employees may use external LLM providers outside approved organizational channels.

Examples include:

* personal AI accounts;
* unapproved browser-based AI tools;
* public APIs;
* browser extensions;
* third-party AI plugins;
* and developer tools connected to external models.

Shadow AI can bypass:

* data classification;
* contractual controls;
* security monitoring;
* privacy review;
* approved provider restrictions;
* and organizational logging.

Organizations should establish governance mechanisms appropriate to their risk and environment.

A registry can contain:

The registry should be integrated with procurement and architecture processes where appropriate.

An LLM dependency may be considered internally critical where failure, compromise, or material degradation could significantly affect:

### 81. Inherent Risk

Inherent risk represents the level of risk associated with the LLM provider and use case before considering applicable controls.

Relevant factors may include:

* data sensitivity;
* business criticality;
* provider dependency;
* model capability;
* autonomy;
* external connectivity;
* concentration;
* jurisdiction;
* and potential impact.

### 82. Control Effectiveness

Controls should be evaluated for effectiveness rather than merely documented existence.

For example:

* a data-retention clause does not prove deletion;
* an MFA policy does not prove that privileged accounts use MFA;
* a security certification does not prove application-specific security;
* an incident-notification clause does not prove timely notification;
* an export clause does not prove usable portability.

The distinction between **control design** and **control operating effectiveness** should be maintained.

### 83. Residual Risk

Residual risk is the remaining risk after considering implemented controls.

Residual risk assessment should consider:

* control effectiveness;
* evidence quality;
* known limitations;
* provider dependency;
* uncertainty;
* concentration;
* remaining vulnerabilities;
* and potential impact.

The residual risk should be assigned to an appropriately authorized risk owner.

### 84. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance should be explicit and appropriately authorized.

An acceptance decision should identify:

* risk;
* business justification;
* affected service;
* existing controls;
* residual exposure;
* limitations;
* acceptance authority;
* duration;
* review date;
* and conditions.

Risk acceptance does not eliminate the underlying risk.

### 85. Exceptions and Compensating Controls

Where standard requirements cannot be met, the organization may establish a formal exception process.

An exception should identify:

* requirement;
* reason for exception;
* affected provider;
* affected service;
* risk impact;
* compensating controls;
* duration;
* owner;
* approval;
* and review date.

Exceptions should not become permanent substitutes for required security controls without appropriate governance.

### 86. Provider Vulnerability Management

The organization should evaluate how the provider manages vulnerabilities relevant to the service.

Where available, relevant evidence may include:

* vulnerability-management processes;
* patching practices;
* security testing;
* penetration testing;
* vulnerability disclosure mechanisms;
* remediation processes;
* and incident response.

The assessment should recognize the limits of customer visibility into provider internals.

### 87. Vulnerability Exposure in AI Services

LLM services may depend on:

* APIs;
* software libraries;
* model runtimes;
* containers;
* operating systems;
* cloud infrastructure;
* identity systems;
* storage;
* networking;
* and other software components.

The organization should therefore consider the provider's broader technology supply chain where it materially affects the service.

### 88. AI-Specific Security Testing

Where appropriate, provider or service assurance should consider AI-specific security testing.

Potential areas include:

* prompt injection;
* jailbreaks;
* data leakage;
* unauthorized tool use;
* malicious instructions;
* model extraction;
* sensitive information disclosure;
* adversarial inputs;
* output manipulation;
* and agent abuse.

The scope should reflect the actual capabilities enabled by the service.

### 89. Prompt Injection Risk

Prompt injection occurs when instructions embedded in input or retrieved content influence the model in unintended ways.

Where an LLM can access enterprise data or tools, prompt injection may create risks such as:

* unauthorized data retrieval;
* disclosure of sensitive information;
* manipulation of workflow;
* unauthorized tool invocation;
* or inappropriate external actions.

Provider security features may reduce some risks, but application-level controls remain important.

### 90. Jailbreak Risk

Jailbreak techniques attempt to bypass model restrictions or safety controls.

The organization should determine whether successful jailbreaks could affect its particular use case.

Risk increases when model output can:

* access sensitive information;
* execute code;
* invoke tools;
* influence high-impact decisions;
* or initiate external actions.

### 91. Output Validation

LLM output should be validated before being trusted by downstream systems.

Validation may include:

* schema validation;
* allowlisting;
* content filtering;
* authorization checks;
* code analysis;
* business-rule validation;
* human review;
* and security inspection.

A model-generated instruction should not automatically become an authorized enterprise action.

* unusual tool invocation;
* policy violations;
* data-loss indicators;
* service degradation;
* model changes;
* and provider incidents.

Monitoring should be proportionate to the use case.

### 99. Provider Incident Response Integration

The organization's incident-response process should include scenarios involving the external LLM provider.

Potential scenarios include:

* provider compromise;
* API credential compromise;
* provider data breach;
* model compromise;
* malicious model update;
* data leakage;
* provider outage;
* supply-chain compromise;
* or unauthorized provider access.

Incident playbooks should identify escalation paths, containment actions, provider contacts, evidence requirements, and recovery options.

### 100. Provider Incident Evidence

Evidence from provider incidents should be evaluated for:

* source;
* authenticity;
* scope;
* affected service;
* affected model;
* affected data;
* timeline;
* root cause;
* containment;
* remediation;
* and residual impact.

Provider incident reports should not automatically be treated as complete evidence of the organization's own impact.

### 101. Business Continuity for LLM Dependency

Business continuity planning should address material dependency on external LLM services.

Potential strategies include:

* alternative provider;
* alternative model;
* degraded functionality;
* manual processing;
* local model;
* cached results where appropriate;
* queued processing;
* or temporary suspension of dependent workflows.

The appropriate strategy depends on business criticality.

### 102. Recovery Time and Recovery Point

Where the LLM supports a critical business service, recovery requirements should be established.

Relevant concepts include:

* Recovery Time Objective (RTO);
* Recovery Point Objective (RPO);
* maximum tolerable downtime;
* maximum tolerable data loss;
* degraded-service capability;
* and recovery dependencies.

LLM recovery planning should account for the provider's own availability and the organization's ability to operate without it.

### 103. Alternative Provider Assessment

An alternative LLM provider should not be considered a proven resilience control merely because another vendor is available.

The alternative should be evaluated for:

* technical compatibility;
* model capability;
* security;
* privacy;
* data portability;
* application integration;
* operational readiness;
* contractual readiness;
* capacity;
* geographic availability;
* and independence from the primary provider.

### 104. Common-Mode Dependency

Two providers may appear independent while sharing common dependencies.

Examples include:

* same cloud infrastructure;
* same model provider;
* same API gateway;
* same identity provider;
* same geographic region;
* same software dependency;
* same dataset;
* or same downstream provider.

Therefore:

**Provider diversity does not automatically establish dependency independence.**

### 107. Regulatory Applicability

The organization should determine which laws and regulations apply to its specific use of an external LLM.

Relevant factors may include:

* jurisdiction;
* organizational role;
* type of data;
* purpose of processing;
* affected individuals;
* AI system function;
* sector;
* contractual relationships;
* and applicable regulatory regime.

Regulatory applicability should not be inferred solely from the fact that a provider describes itself as "compliant."

### 108. Legal Requirement Versus Internal Control

The knowledge base should distinguish between:

**Legal or regulatory requirements**

Requirements directly imposed by applicable law or regulation.

**Regulatory guidance**

Recommendations or expectations published by competent authorities or recognized regulatory bodies.

**Contractual requirements**

Obligations agreed between the organization and provider.

**Internal governance controls**

Controls established by the organization to manage risk.

**Recommended practices**

Practices that may improve governance but are not necessarily mandatory.

An internal control such as `AI-LLM-108` is therefore an organizational taxonomy identifier, not a statutory article or regulatory provision.

### 125. LLM Provider Assurance Principle

**AI-LLM-125 — LLM Provider Assurance Principle**

LLM provider due diligence should be evidence-based, use-case-specific, and proportionate to the organization's dependency and risk.

The assessment should evaluate provider identity, model identity, data processing, security architecture, IAM, API security, privacy, model behavior, model change, supply-chain dependencies, incidents, resilience, concentration, portability, contractual protections, and applicable legal or regulatory requirements.

* tool selection;

### 210. LLM Provider Continuous-Assurance Principle

**AI-LLM-210 — LLM Provider Continuous-Assurance Principle**

LLM provider risk should be managed continuously across the provider, model, data, application, contractual, operational, security, privacy, concentration, resilience, and exit dimensions.

Continuous assurance should combine provider monitoring, model and configuration change detection, security testing, incident intelligence, assurance evidence, contractual monitoring, concentration analysis, resilience testing, exit readiness, and documented risk reassessment.

Material incidents, provider changes, model changes, assurance deterioration, new use cases, increased autonomy, concentration increases, and other significant developments may justify internal reassessment. These reassessment triggers are **governance practices unless a specific law, regulation, contract, or authoritative requirement independently establishes an obligation**.

Provider monitoring and automated analysis can improve visibility but cannot guarantee detection of all provider-side changes or failures. Human accountability, evidence evaluation, and appropriately authorized risk decisions remain necessary.

### Core Principles — Part 3

1. **Continuous Monitoring Principle** — LLM provider risk should be monitored throughout the service lifecycle rather than assessed only during onboarding.
2. **Model Change Principle** — Model changes can materially alter security, behavior, performance, and business risk.
3. **Behavioral Baseline Principle** — Material use cases should have appropriate mechanisms for identifying significant behavioral change.
4. **Configuration Governance Principle** — Provider and customer configuration changes can materially affect risk.
5. **Incident Integration Principle** — Provider incidents should integrate with the organization's own incident-management and risk-governance processes.
6. **Evidence Preservation Principle** — Relevant provider and customer evidence should be preserved during material incidents and investigations.
7. **Concentration Principle** — Provider concentration should be assessed across direct and common underlying dependencies.
8. **Resilience Principle** — An alternative provider is not automatically an independent recovery capability.
9. **Exit Readiness Principle** — Exit capability should be evaluated before provider failure or termination becomes necessary.
10. **Portability Principle** — Data-export rights should be distinguished from usable technical and operational portability.
11. **Assurance-Gap Principle** — Material uncertainty or missing provider evidence should be explicitly recognized and managed.
12. **Regulatory Traceability Principle** — Regulatory developments should be assessed for applicability before being translated into internal controls.
13. **Governance-Trigger Principle** — Incident, complaint, enforcement, provider, model, or regulatory developments may be used as internal reassessment inputs, but such triggers are not automatically statutory requirements.
14. **Automation-Oversight Principle** — Automated provider monitoring and AI-assisted analysis should remain subject to appropriate human oversight.
15. **Continuous-Assurance Principle** — Continuous assurance requires an integrated lifecycle of monitoring, evidence, testing, reassessment, remediation, resilience, and governance decision-making.

### 400. LLM Provider Governance Principle

**AI-LLM-400 — LLM Provider Governance Principle**

An organization should treat external LLM providers as lifecycle-managed enterprise dependencies rather than isolated technology purchases.

Effective governance requires:

* accountable ownership;
* provider and model inventory;
* use-case-specific risk assessment;
* data and privacy governance;
* secure architecture;
* IAM and API security;
* model and configuration change management;
* incident integration;
* continuous monitoring;
* provider assurance;
* concentration management;
* resilience;
* portability;
* exit readiness;
* and continuous improvement.

The organization should distinguish:

* provider count from effective diversification;
* provider assurance from independent assurance;
* certification from universal security;
* contractual rights from demonstrated capability;
* data export from practical portability;
* documented recovery from tested recovery;
* alternative providers from independent resilience;
* provider notification from the organization's own legal reporting obligations;
* regulatory mapping from regulatory applicability;
* and internal governance controls from statutory or regulatory requirements.

Material governance triggers such as provider incidents, complaints, enforcement developments, regulatory changes, model changes, assurance deterioration, concentration increases, or failed resilience tests may justify internal reassessment. Such triggers should not be represented as universal legal obligations unless a specific law, regulation, contract, or authoritative requirement independently establishes them.

Internal `AI-LLM-*` identifiers are organizational taxonomy identifiers used to structure this knowledge base. They are not statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, or official legal control identifiers.

### Core Principles — Part 4

1. **Enterprise Governance Principle** — External LLM providers should be governed within the enterprise AI, security, privacy, risk, procurement, and resilience framework.
2. **Accountability Principle** — Business, technical, security, privacy, procurement, legal, and risk responsibilities should be clearly assigned.
3. **Dependency-Graph Principle** — Provider risk should be understood through dependencies connecting providers, models, applications, data, infrastructure, and business services.
4. **Effective-Diversity Principle** — The number of providers does not by itself demonstrate independent diversification.
5. **Common-Mode Principle** — Shared cloud, model, identity, network, software, geographic, or fourth-party dependencies can create correlated failure.
6. **Portability Principle** — Provider portability must include data, application, model behavior, security controls, operations, and other material dependencies.
7. **Exit-Assurance Principle** — Exit capability should be demonstrated through appropriate testing rather than inferred from contractual termination rights.
8. **Resilience Principle** — Recovery capability should be evaluated for independence from the primary provider's failure domain.
9. **Shared-Responsibility Principle** — Provider and customer responsibilities should be explicitly identified and verified.
10. **Control-Dependency Principle** — Provider-dependent security controls should be identified and assessed for resilience.
11. **Evidence-Triangulation Principle** — Important provider assertions should be evaluated against appropriate contractual, technical, audit, and independent evidence.
12. **Assurance-Limitation Principle** — Certifications, audit reports, questionnaires, and provider assertions have defined scopes and limitations.
13. **Automation-Governance Principle** — Automated provider governance should be auditable, monitored, and subject to appropriate human oversight.
14. **Regulatory-Applicability Principle** — External legal and regulatory requirements should be assessed for actual applicability before being translated into controls.
15. **Maturity Principle** — Governance maturity should be evaluated through demonstrated capability, not documentation volume alone.
16. **Continuous-Improvement Principle** — Incidents, provider changes, testing, assurance findings, metrics, and exit exercises should feed back into provider governance.
17. **Strategic-Dependency Principle** — Material provider dependencies should be managed as strategic enterprise risks when they can significantly affect business services.
18. **Risk-Ownership Principle** — Residual LLM provider risk should have an explicitly accountable and appropriately authorized owner.
19. **Lifecycle Principle** — LLM provider governance should extend from discovery and selection through operation, monitoring, reassessment, renewal, replacement, and verified exit.
20. **Governance-Integrity Principle** — Internal control identifiers, risk tiers, thresholds, maturity levels, and monitoring triggers should be clearly distinguished from external legal and regulatory requirements.
