# 16.02 — AI Vendor Risk Management

## Part 1 — Foundations, Vendor Risk Architecture, Scope, and Governance

### 1. AI Vendor Risk Management Definition

AI Vendor Risk Management (AI-VRM) is the structured process used to identify, assess, control, monitor, and govern security, privacy, operational, regulatory, model, data, and supply-chain risks arising from vendors that provide AI products, services, models, platforms, datasets, infrastructure, or supporting capabilities.

AI-VRM extends conventional third-party risk management because AI vendors can introduce additional dependencies involving:

* Models
* Training data
* Inference data
* Model artifacts
* AI APIs
* Prompt processing
* Model updates
* AI-generated outputs
* Autonomous capabilities
* External tools
* Model marketplaces
* Subprocessors
* Foundation-model dependencies
* Cloud infrastructure

The objective is not simply to determine whether a vendor is secure. The objective is to determine whether the **specific AI service, vendor relationship, and intended use** can be governed within the organization's risk appetite and applicable requirements.

---

### 2. AI-VRM Governance Objective

The primary governance objective is to ensure that AI vendors are subject to risk-based oversight throughout their lifecycle.

The lifecycle should cover:

**Vendor Discovery → Intake → Classification → Due Diligence → Risk Assessment → Contracting → Approval → Onboarding → Monitoring → Reassessment → Renewal → Exit**

AI-VRM should therefore operate as a continuous governance process rather than a one-time procurement questionnaire.

---

### 3. AI Vendor Versus Conventional Technology Vendor

An AI vendor may provide capabilities that create risks not normally present in conventional software procurement.

Examples include:

| Conventional Technology Risk | AI-Specific Extension                                     |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Software vulnerability       | Model and software vulnerability                          |
| Data processing              | Prompt, training, inference, and output processing        |
| Service outage               | Model/API/service outage                                  |
| Access control               | AI tool and agent permissions                             |
| Software updates             | Model-version and behavior changes                        |
| Vendor breach                | Potential model/data supply-chain compromise              |
| Software licensing           | Model and dataset licensing                               |
| Performance                  | Model accuracy and behavioral performance                 |
| Documentation                | Model cards, system documentation, data-use documentation |
| Subprocessors                | Model, cloud, data, and AI-service dependencies           |

AI-VRM should therefore supplement existing third-party risk processes rather than simply rename them.

---

### 4. Vendor Risk Is Use-Case Dependent

The risk associated with an AI vendor cannot be determined solely from the vendor's reputation or product category.

The assessment should consider:

* Intended use
* Business process
* Data processed
* Users
* Model capability
* Autonomy
* Decision impact
* Regulatory environment
* Security architecture
* Integration
* Criticality
* Geographic scope
* Dependency concentration

The same vendor may represent different risk levels for different organizational use cases.

---

### 5. AI Vendor Risk Assessment Object

The primary assessment object should be clearly defined.

Depending on the service, the object may be:

* Vendor
* AI product
* AI model
* API
* SaaS application
* Dataset
* Cloud AI service
* Agent
* Model marketplace artifact
* Software component

A vendor-level assessment should not automatically be treated as sufficient evidence for every service or model supplied by that vendor.

---

### 6. Vendor-to-Service Separation

The organization should distinguish between:

**Vendor Risk**

and

**Service Risk**

A vendor may have strong enterprise security controls while a specific AI product introduces additional risks involving:

* Model behavior
* Data retention
* Training use
* Prompt processing
* Tool access
* Model updates
* Third-party dependencies

AI-VRM should therefore support both vendor-level and service-level assessment.

### 12. Cybersecurity Risk

AI vendors may create cybersecurity exposure through:

* APIs
* Authentication
* Network connections
* Model endpoints
* Administrative interfaces
* Software dependencies
* Containers
* Plugins
* Agent tools
* Cloud infrastructure
* Support access

Security assessment should therefore consider the complete integration surface.

---

### 13. Privacy Risk

AI vendor assessment should determine what personal information is processed and how.

Relevant questions include:

* What data is collected?
* Why is it processed?
* Where is it processed?
* How long is it retained?
* Is it used for model training?
* Is it shared with subprocessors?
* Can the organization control deletion?
* Can the organization restrict processing?

Privacy requirements depend on the applicable jurisdiction and processing context.

---

### 14. Data Governance Risk

Third-party AI services may process:

* Prompts
* Uploaded documents
* Customer information
* Business records
* Training datasets
* Fine-tuning datasets
* Embeddings
* Metadata
* Generated outputs

The organization should determine whether supplier data handling is consistent with approved data-governance requirements.

---

### 15. Model Risk

Model risk can arise from:

* Inaccurate outputs
* Hallucination
* Bias
* Model drift
* Unexpected behavior
* Model degradation
* Model-version changes
* Poor robustness
* Inadequate evaluation
* Unsafe outputs

Model risk should be assessed according to the actual use case.

---

### 16. AI Safety Risk

Where an AI service can materially affect people, systems, or operations, the organization should assess safety-related risks.

Examples include:

* Harmful outputs
* Unsafe recommendations
* Autonomous actions
* Incorrect instructions
* Failure to respect constraints
* Unexpected escalation of model behavior

Safety controls should be proportionate to the application's potential impact.

---

### 17. Operational Risk

Operational AI vendor risk may involve:

* Service interruption
* Capacity limitations
* API limits
* Model retirement
* Service degradation
* Provider support failure
* Configuration changes
* Dependency failures

Operational assessment should consider business-process consequences.

---

### 18. Resilience Risk

Critical AI services may create resilience risk if the organization cannot rapidly operate without the vendor.

Assessment should consider:

* Recovery capability
* Alternative providers
* Model portability
* Data portability
* Manual fallback
* Migration time
* Provider concentration

---

### 19. Regulatory Risk

AI vendor relationships may create regulatory exposure depending on:

* Jurisdiction
* AI system role
* Sector
* Data processing
* Affected persons
* AI use case
* Vendor location
* Customer location
* Applicable contractual obligations

Regulatory analysis should distinguish enacted requirements from:

* Guidance
* Standards
* Frameworks
* Consultations
* Proposed legislation
* Industry practices

* Service levels

### 21. Intellectual-Property Risk

AI vendors may create intellectual-property risks involving:

* Training data
* Model weights
* Fine-tuning data
* Generated content
* Generated code
* Third-party datasets
* Software dependencies
* Licenses

The organization should determine which rights and restrictions apply before relying on the vendor's representations.

---

### 22. Supply-Chain Risk

An AI vendor may depend on multiple additional parties.

Potential dependencies include:

**Vendor → Foundation Model → Cloud → Software → Dataset → Subprocessor**

A vendor assessment should therefore consider material fourth-party dependencies where they can affect organizational risk.

---

### 23. Concentration Risk

Concentration risk exists when multiple critical AI capabilities depend on:

* One vendor
* One foundation model
* One cloud provider
* One geographic region
* One dataset
* One model marketplace
* One critical software component

The organization should assess concentration at both the direct-vendor and underlying-dependency levels.

---

### 24. Geopolitical Risk

Material AI vendors may create geographic or geopolitical exposure through:

* Headquarters
* Ownership
* Development locations
* Cloud regions
* Data-processing locations
* Subprocessors
* Regulatory jurisdiction

Geopolitical analysis should be factual and connected to identifiable business, legal, security, or continuity risks.

---

### 25. Exit and Portability Risk

A vendor may become difficult to replace because of:

* Proprietary APIs
* Proprietary model formats
* Vendor-specific integrations
* Non-portable fine-tuning
* Data-format dependency
* High migration cost
* Lack of alternative providers

Exit feasibility should therefore be assessed during onboarding rather than only when termination becomes necessary.

* **Tier 2 — High-Risk AI Vendor**

* **Tier 4 — Low-Risk AI Vendor**

### 29. Critical AI Vendor

A vendor may be considered critical where disruption, compromise, or material failure could significantly affect:

* Critical business processes
* Customers
* Sensitive information
* Regulatory obligations
* Safety
* Enterprise operations

Critical classification should be based on documented criteria.

---

### 30. High-Risk AI Vendor

A high-risk internal classification may apply where the vendor presents substantial exposure through:

* Sensitive data
* Significant AI decision support
* High business dependency
* Material regulatory exposure
* Complex supply chain
* High autonomy
* Difficult exit

This classification should not be confused with the **EU AI Act's legal classification of an AI system as high-risk**.

### 31. AI Vendor Risk Scoring

* Impact

* AI capability
* Autonomy
* Regulatory exposure
* Dependency

* Exit difficulty

---

### 32. Vendor Risk Scoring Limitations

A numerical score should not replace professional judgment.

Important qualitative factors may not be accurately represented by a single score, particularly:

* Emerging AI threats
* Unknown dependencies
* Regulatory uncertainty
* Novel model behavior
* Limited supplier transparency

Material uncertainty should remain visible even when a numerical score appears acceptable.

* Regulatory interpretation
* Risk aggregation

Responsible for independent assurance over AI-VRM governance and control effectiveness.

### 82. AI Vendor Continuous Compliance

AI-VRM should operate as a continuous process:

**Identify → Assess → Control → Contract → Approve → Monitor → Detect → Respond → Reassess → Renew or Exit**

The objective is to maintain an accurate understanding of vendor risk throughout the relationship.

---

### 83. AI Vendor Risk Governance Principle

The central AI-VRM governance principle is:

> **An AI vendor should not be assessed only as a company; it should be assessed as a specific AI dependency operating within a specific organizational use case, data environment, regulatory context, and supply chain.**

This principle provides the foundation for subsequent controls covering AI vendor assessment, LLM providers, AI SaaS, cloud AI, foundation models, open-source models, marketplaces, datasets, APIs, contracts, assurance, concentration, and exit.

# Part 2 — Vendor Due Diligence, Assessment, Assurance, and Contractual Controls

### 84 — AI-VRM-084 — Vendor Due-Diligence Scope

AI vendor due diligence should be scoped to the actual services, models, datasets, APIs, infrastructure, administrative interfaces, and processing activities that the organization will use.

The assessment should identify:

* the specific AI service or model;
* intended organizational use cases;
* categories of data processed;
* geographic processing locations;
* user populations;
* integrations and privileged connections;
* model-development and training dependencies;
* subprocessors and fourth parties;
* applicable regulatory requirements;
* security and privacy obligations;
* business-criticality;
* expected duration of the relationship; and
* planned exit requirements.

A generic corporate security assessment should not automatically be treated as sufficient AI-specific due diligence.

---

### 85 — AI-VRM-085 — Vendor Corporate Assessment

The organization should assess the vendor's overall corporate security and governance posture as one component of AI vendor risk.

Relevant evidence may include:

* information-security policies;
* organizational security governance;
* security certifications;
* independent assurance reports;
* vulnerability-management processes;
* incident-response capabilities;
* business-continuity arrangements;
* identity and access management;
* personnel-security controls;
* privacy governance;
* data-protection practices;
* secure-development processes; and
* security governance responsibilities.

Corporate assurance provides useful context but does not replace assessment of the specific AI service.

---

### 86 — AI-VRM-086 — AI-Service-Specific Assessment

Each material AI service should be assessed separately from the vendor's general corporate environment.

The assessment should consider whether the particular service:

* uses generative AI;
* processes organizational data;
* retains prompts or outputs;
* uses customer data for model training;
* supports fine-tuning;
* provides retrieval capabilities;
* permits external tool invocation;
* supports autonomous agents;
* exposes APIs;
* connects to enterprise systems;
* uses customer-specific models;
* relies on third-party foundation models; or
* introduces additional supply-chain dependencies.

A vendor can have strong enterprise security controls while a particular AI service presents material use-case-specific risks.

---

### 87 — AI-VRM-087 — Vendor AI Architecture Review

For material AI dependencies, the organization should obtain sufficient architectural information to understand how the vendor's AI service operates.

Architecture review should consider:

* application components;
* model components;
* data flows;
* storage locations;
* inference infrastructure;
* training infrastructure where relevant;
* API gateways;
* authentication mechanisms;
* authorization boundaries;
* logging systems;
* monitoring systems;
* external integrations;
* subprocessors;
* model-serving infrastructure; and
* administrative access paths.

The objective is to establish a defensible understanding of the dependency rather than requiring disclosure of proprietary intellectual property that is unnecessary for the risk decision.

---

### 88 — AI-VRM-088 — AI Data-Flow Assessment

The organization should map organizational data entering, passing through, or generated by the vendor's AI service.

The data-flow assessment should identify:

1. data source;
2. transmission path;
3. processing location;
4. storage location;
5. model-processing function;
6. retention period;
7. downstream recipients;
8. subprocessors;
9. logging destinations;
10. backup locations; and
11. deletion or return mechanism.

Particular attention should be given to prompts, uploaded documents, embeddings, retrieved information, model outputs, telemetry, and administrative logs.

---

### 89 — AI-VRM-089 — Vendor Data Classification Alignment

The organization should determine which data classifications may be processed by each vendor.

For example:

| Data classification | Example vendor treatment                                     |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Public              | May be broadly permitted                                     |
| Internal            | Requires baseline contractual and security controls          |
| Confidential        | Requires stronger access, retention, and processing controls |
| Restricted          | Requires explicit authorization and enhanced assurance       |
| Highly sensitive    | May require dedicated architecture or prohibition            |

The organization's data-classification policy should determine the permitted use rather than the vendor's marketing classification.

---

### 90 — AI-VRM-090 — Prompt and Input Data Governance

Vendor due diligence should determine how prompts and other AI inputs are handled.

The assessment should establish whether inputs are:

* transient;
* stored;
* logged;
* reviewed by personnel;
* used for service improvement;
* used for model training;
* shared with subprocessors;
* transferred internationally;
* retained in backups; or
* available through administrative interfaces.

The organization should not assume that an AI prompt is equivalent to ordinary application input. Prompts may contain confidential business information, personal data, source code, credentials, or proprietary information.

---

### 91 — AI-VRM-091 — AI Output Governance

The organization should determine how vendor-generated outputs are handled and whether contractual or technical restrictions apply.

Assessment should consider:

* output ownership provisions;
* licensing restrictions;
* confidentiality;
* retention;
* downstream processing;
* automated decision use;
* customer responsibilities;
* intellectual-property provisions;
* content restrictions; and
* applicable usage policies.

AI-generated output should not automatically be treated as reliable, original, legally unrestricted, or suitable for unrestricted downstream use.

---

### 92 — AI-VRM-092 — Model-Training Data Governance

Where a vendor operates or trains models, due diligence should establish whether organizational data can contribute to:

* general model training;
* fine-tuning;
* reinforcement processes;
* evaluation datasets;
* abuse-detection systems;
* service improvement;
* human review processes; or
* other machine-learning activities.

The organization should document whether such processing is permitted, prohibited, optional, or subject to explicit customer configuration.

---

### 93 — AI-VRM-093 — Vendor Data-Training Opt-Out Verification

Where the organization requires that its data not be used for model training, the control should be validated rather than relying solely on verbal vendor assurances.

Evidence may include:

* contractual language;
* service configuration;
* account-level settings;
* technical documentation;
* vendor policy;
* independent assurance;
* configuration screenshots;
* test results; and
* written vendor confirmation.

The evidence should correspond to the actual service and subscription tier being used.

---

### 94 — AI-VRM-094 — Data Retention Assessment

Vendor due diligence should establish retention periods for:

* prompts;
* uploaded files;
* outputs;
* embeddings;
* telemetry;
* security logs;
* support tickets;
* account metadata;
* backups; and
* deleted content.

Where different retention periods apply to different data types, the organization should document each applicable period.

---

### 95 — AI-VRM-095 — Data Deletion Verification

The organization should determine how data deletion operates when:

* a user deletes information;
* an administrator deletes information;
* an account is terminated;
* a contract expires;
* a data-subject request is received;
* a security incident occurs; or
* the organization exercises an exit right.

Deletion mechanisms should account for primary storage, replicas, caches, backups, logs, and downstream processors where applicable.

---

### 96 — AI-VRM-096 — Subprocessor Assessment

The vendor should disclose material subprocessors involved in the AI service.

Assessment should consider:

* subprocessor identity;
* processing location;
* function;
* data accessed;
* security controls;
* AI-model dependency;
* geographic exposure;
* change notification;
* contractual flow-down;
* audit rights; and
* termination or objection mechanisms where applicable.

Subprocessor risk should be incorporated into the organization's overall vendor risk assessment.

---

### 97 — AI-VRM-097 — Fourth-Party Dependency Assessment

Where material, the organization should identify dependencies beyond the vendor's immediate subprocessors.

Examples include:

* foundation-model providers;
* cloud infrastructure providers;
* model-hosting platforms;
* external dataset providers;
* identity providers;
* security providers;
* AI moderation providers;
* vector databases;
* observability platforms; and
* external API providers.

The organization should determine whether material fourth-party dependencies could affect confidentiality, integrity, availability, compliance, or exit.

---

### 98 — AI-VRM-098 — Geographic Processing Assessment

The organization should identify relevant geographic locations associated with the vendor's AI service.

The assessment may include:

* data centers;
* model inference locations;
* support operations;
* administrative access locations;
* subprocessors;
* backups;
* disaster-recovery facilities; and
* data-transfer destinations.

Geographic processing should be evaluated against applicable organizational, contractual, privacy, regulatory, and sovereignty requirements.

---

### 99 — AI-VRM-099 — Cross-Border Data Transfer Assessment

Where personal or otherwise regulated data crosses jurisdictions, the organization should determine:

* applicable transfer requirements;
* transfer mechanisms;
* contractual protections;
* supplementary safeguards;
* processor/subprocessor arrangements;
* government-access considerations where relevant; and
* organizational approval requirements.

This assessment should be performed with appropriate legal and privacy expertise where required.

---

### 100 — AI-VRM-100 — Vendor Identity and Ownership Verification

The organization should verify the identity and legal status of material AI vendors.

Verification may include:

* legal entity;
* ownership;
* corporate structure;
* contracting entity;
* service provider;
* relevant subsidiaries;
* acquisition relationships;
* parent organization; and
* material changes in ownership.

This is particularly relevant where the apparent AI provider is a brand or platform operated by another legal entity.

---

### 101 — AI-VRM-101 — Vendor Financial Viability Assessment

Critical AI vendors should be assessed for financial and operational viability.

Relevant indicators may include:

* financial stability;
* funding dependency;
* profitability or operating sustainability;
* significant restructuring;
* acquisition risk;
* service discontinuation risk;
* dependency on a single funding source; and
* ability to maintain security and operational commitments.

Financial viability is a business-continuity consideration and should not be treated as an indicator of technical security by itself.

---

### 102 — AI-VRM-102 — Vendor Business-Continuity Assessment

The organization should evaluate whether the vendor can maintain critical AI services during disruptive events.

Assessment areas may include:

* business-continuity plans;
* disaster recovery;
* backup architecture;
* regional redundancy;
* recovery objectives;
* incident-response capability;
* infrastructure dependencies;
* workforce continuity;
* dependency concentration; and
* recovery testing.

Critical AI services should have resilience requirements proportionate to their business impact.

---

### 103 — AI-VRM-103 — Vendor Disaster-Recovery Evidence

For critical dependencies, the organization should seek evidence that disaster-recovery arrangements are implemented and tested.

Possible evidence includes:

* recovery-test summaries;
* independent assurance reports;
* recovery-time objectives;
* recovery-point objectives;
* documented failover architecture;
* test frequency;
* test results; and
* remediation records.

A vendor's statement that disaster recovery exists should not automatically be treated as evidence that recovery capability is effective.

---

### 104 — AI-VRM-104 — AI Service Availability Assessment

The organization should assess availability characteristics relevant to the intended use case.

Assessment may include:

* historical availability;
* service-level commitments;
* regional dependencies;
* planned maintenance;
* rate limits;
* capacity limitations;
* quota restrictions;
* API throttling;
* dependency outages; and
* degradation behavior.

Availability requirements should reflect business criticality rather than applying identical thresholds to every AI service.

---

### 105 — AI-VRM-105 — AI Service Degradation Assessment

The organization should determine how the vendor behaves when the AI service experiences partial failure or degraded performance.

Potential degradation scenarios include:

* increased latency;
* reduced model availability;
* reduced context limits;
* API failures;
* incomplete outputs;
* safety-filter failures;
* unavailable tools;
* unavailable retrieval services; and
* regional service disruption.

Critical applications should define acceptable degraded-service behavior and fallback mechanisms.

---

### 106 — AI-VRM-106 — Vendor Identity and Access Management Assessment

The vendor's identity and access controls should be assessed for administrative and customer-facing interfaces.

Relevant controls include:

* multifactor authentication;
* role-based access control;
* privileged-access management;
* administrative segregation;
* service accounts;
* API credentials;
* session management;
* access reviews;
* logging; and
* privileged activity monitoring.

Where the organization manages identities directly, federated identity and centralized lifecycle management should be evaluated.

---

### 107 — AI-VRM-107 — Administrative Access Assessment

Vendor administrative access to organizational data or AI environments should be assessed separately from ordinary customer access.

The assessment should establish:

* who can access customer environments;
* under what circumstances;
* approval requirements;
* authentication controls;
* privileged-access controls;
* session monitoring;
* access logging;
* emergency access procedures; and
* access revocation.

Where feasible, privileged access should follow least-privilege and justifiable-access principles.

---

### 108 — AI-VRM-108 — API Security Assessment

For AI services consumed through APIs, due diligence should examine:

* authentication;

* credential management;
* TLS protection;
* rate limiting;
* request validation;
* logging;
* abuse prevention;
* quota controls;
* key rotation;
* service-account governance; and
* API version management.

API security should be evaluated as part of the AI dependency rather than treated solely as an application-development concern.

---

### 109 — AI-VRM-109 — Secrets and Credential Handling

The organization should determine how the vendor stores and protects:

* API keys;
* access tokens;
* service credentials;
* encryption keys;
* integration secrets;
* signing credentials; and
* administrative credentials.

Vendor documentation should establish whether secrets are encrypted, access-controlled, rotated, logged, and protected against unauthorized disclosure.

### 110 — AI-VRM-110 — Encryption Assessment

Vendor assessment should determine encryption controls for data:

* in transit;
* at rest;
* in backups;
* in databases;
* in object storage;
* in logs; and
* where technically applicable to model-processing environments.

Where customer-managed keys are required, the assessment should determine whether the service supports the required key-management architecture.

---

### 111 — AI-VRM-111 — Key-Management Assessment

Where encryption is material to risk, the organization should evaluate:

* key ownership;
* key generation;
* key storage;
* key rotation;
* access control;
* separation of duties;
* key revocation;
* hardware security modules where relevant; and
* customer-managed key capabilities.

The organization should verify whether the vendor's key-management model meets the requirements of the intended use case.

---

### 112 — AI-VRM-112 — Logging and Auditability Assessment

The organization should determine what security and operational events generated by the AI service are logged.

Relevant events may include:

* authentication;

* configuration changes;
* API calls;
* model changes;
* data access;
* security events;
* policy changes;
* integration activity; and
* incident events.

Logs should be sufficient to support security monitoring, investigations, compliance evidence, and incident response where required.

### 113 — AI-VRM-113 — Customer Audit-Log Access

For material services, the organization should determine whether it can obtain relevant audit information.

Assessment should establish:

* available log types;
* retention period;
* export capability;
* API availability;
* timestamp integrity;
* event granularity;
* administrative visibility; and
* limitations on customer access.

Lack of adequate auditability may increase operational and forensic risk.

### 115 — AI-VRM-115 — Secure Software Development Assessment

### 116 — AI-VRM-116 — AI Model Security Assessment

For vendors providing models, the assessment should consider model-specific security risks.

* rollback;

Material model changes may alter the organization's risk profile even when the vendor considers them ordinary product improvements.

### 118 — AI-VRM-118 — Vendor Change Notification

Contracts or service arrangements for material AI dependencies should establish appropriate mechanisms for notification of significant changes.

Potential notification events include:

* material geographic changes;
* security incidents;
* service discontinuation;
* ownership changes;
* material changes in terms;
* significant architectural changes; and
* changes affecting compliance obligations.

Notification requirements should be proportionate to risk and enforceable within the contractual relationship.

### 119 — AI-VRM-119 — Vendor Security Incident Assessment

The organization should determine whether the vendor has an established process for detecting, investigating, containing, and reporting security incidents.

Assessment should consider:

* incident classification;
* escalation procedures;
* customer notification;
* forensic capability;
* evidence preservation;
* containment;
* remediation;
* root-cause analysis; and
* post-incident corrective actions.

Vendor incident processes should complement, not replace, the organization's own incident-management obligations.

---

### 120 — AI-VRM-120 — AI-Specific Incident Scenarios

Vendor due diligence should consider AI-specific incident scenarios where relevant.

Examples include:

* training-data exposure;
* model compromise;
* malicious model replacement;
* prompt-data leakage;
* compromised retrieval sources;
* unsafe autonomous actions;
* model-serving compromise;
* malicious package dependencies;
* unauthorized fine-tuning;
* compromised AI APIs; and
* large-scale abuse of customer credentials.

The assessment should determine whether the vendor can detect and communicate these scenarios effectively.

---

### 121 — AI-VRM-121 — Vendor Incident Notification Requirements

For material vendors, contractual requirements should define incident-notification expectations.

The agreement should establish, where appropriate:

* notification channel;
* responsible contacts;
* incident categories;
* notification timeframe;
* minimum information;
* ongoing updates;
* evidence preservation;
* cooperation requirements; and
* closure reporting.

Contractual notification periods should be aligned with the organization's regulatory and operational obligations.

---

### 122 — AI-VRM-122 — Regulatory Notification Responsibility

The organization should explicitly distinguish vendor notification obligations from the organization's own regulatory reporting responsibilities.

A vendor's notification to the organization does not automatically mean that:

* a regulatory notification has been completed;
* a statutory reporting deadline has been satisfied;
* affected individuals have been notified;
* law-enforcement notification has occurred; or
* another legal obligation has been fulfilled.

Responsibility should be assigned within the organization's incident-response and compliance processes.

---

### 130 — AI-VRM-130 — Vendor Questionnaire Governance

### 131 — AI-VRM-131 — Evidence-Based Questionnaire Validation

Material questionnaire responses should be validated against objective evidence.

Examples include:

**Vendor statement:** “Customer data is not used for training.”

**Evidence:** contractual restriction, technical configuration, service documentation, or independent assurance.

**Vendor statement:** “All data is encrypted.”

**Evidence:** documented encryption architecture, cryptographic controls, and relevant assurance.

**Vendor statement:** “We comply with all applicable regulations.”

**Evidence:** specific legal scope, control mapping, assessment, certification, or contractual commitments.

The organization should document unresolved evidence gaps.

---

### 132 — AI-VRM-132 — Vendor Security Claims Validation

Marketing claims such as:

* “secure by design”;
* “enterprise-grade security”;
* “privacy-first”;
* “fully compliant”;
* “zero risk”; or
* “military-grade security”

should not be accepted as evidence by themselves.

Claims should be translated into testable requirements and supported by objective evidence.

---

### 133 — AI-VRM-133 — AI Regulatory Status Validation

The organization should verify regulatory claims made by AI vendors.

* legally binding requirements;
* regulatory guidance;
* supervisory expectations;
* recognized standards;
* voluntary frameworks;
* certifications;
* contractual commitments;
* vendor policies; and
* marketing claims.

Statements such as “AI Act compliant” should therefore be evaluated against the specific service, role, applicable requirements, implementation status, and relevant date.

### 142 — AI-VRM-142 — Right-to-Audit Practicality

A contractual right to audit is not effective merely because the contract contains the phrase “right to audit.”

The organization should determine:

* who may perform the audit;
* what evidence can be accessed;
* how much notice is required;
* applicable confidentiality restrictions;
* frequency;
* cost allocation;
* remediation rights;
* access limitations; and
* treatment of material findings.

For highly standardized cloud AI services, independent assurance may sometimes be more practical than unrestricted customer audits.

### 143 — AI-VRM-143 — Regulatory Cooperation Clause

The clause should define:

* information-sharing responsibilities;
* evidence availability;
* investigation cooperation;
* incident support;
* audit support;
* regulatory inquiry support; and
* reasonable response mechanisms.

The organization remains responsible for determining its own legal obligations.

### 144 — AI-VRM-144 — Security Incident Cooperation Clause

Contracts should define vendor cooperation during security incidents.

Requirements may include:

* technical assistance;
* log preservation;
* forensic support;
* root-cause information;
* containment assistance;
* remediation;
* evidence preservation; and
* post-incident reporting.

Incident cooperation should be integrated with the organization's incident-response procedures.

### 145 — AI-VRM-145 — Vendor Vulnerability Notification Clause

Where relevant, vendors should be contractually required to communicate material vulnerabilities affecting the services used by the organization.

Notification requirements should consider:

* severity;
* affected services;
* affected versions;
* exploitation status;
* mitigation;
* remediation;
* compensating controls; and
* customer actions.

### 146 — AI-VRM-146 — Model Vulnerability Notification

Where the organization depends on a vendor model, material model vulnerabilities should be subject to appropriate notification requirements.

Examples may include vulnerabilities involving:

* model extraction;
* data leakage;
* malicious model behavior;
* compromised model artifacts;
* unsafe tool invocation;
* prompt-injection exposure;
* insecure model-serving infrastructure; and
* supply-chain compromise.

### 147 — AI-VRM-147 — Material Model Change Clause

For critical AI dependencies, the organization should define what constitutes a material model change.

Possible criteria include changes affecting:

* model family;
* architecture;
* training methodology;
* data processing;
* safety behavior;
* output characteristics;
* capabilities;
* tool access;
* context handling;
* geographic processing; or
* security architecture.

The organization should determine whether notification, reassessment, approval, or testing is required.

### 148 — AI-VRM-148 — Service-Level Agreement Assessment

AI vendor SLAs should be evaluated against the organization's actual business requirements.

Relevant dimensions may include:

* availability;
* response time;
* support response;
* incident communication;
* restoration;
* API performance;
* service credits;
* maintenance windows; and
* service discontinuation.

Availability commitments should not be treated as a complete resilience strategy.

### 149 — AI-VRM-149 — AI Service-Level Metrics

Where appropriate, AI-specific service metrics may include:

* inference availability;

* latency;
* throughput;

Metrics should be measurable and tied to contractual or operational requirements where applicable.

### 150 — AI-VRM-150 — Vendor Risk Treatment Decision

Following due diligence, the organization should formally determine how identified vendor risks will be treated.

Possible treatments include:

* accept;
* mitigate;
* transfer;
* avoid;
* restrict;
* conditionally approve; or
* reject.

Risk treatment should be documented with accountable ownership and supporting evidence.

---

### 151 — AI-VRM-151 — Residual Vendor Risk

Residual risk should be documented after considering:

* vendor controls;
* organizational controls;
* contractual controls;
* technical controls;
* compensating controls;
* business impact;
* regulatory requirements; and
* identified evidence gaps.

Residual risk should be compared with the organization's approved risk appetite and tolerance.

---

### 152 — AI-VRM-152 — Mandatory Requirement Override

Vendor risk acceptance should not be used to override mandatory legal, regulatory, contractual, or internal requirements that prohibit the intended processing or activity.

Where a mandatory requirement cannot be satisfied, the organization should escalate the matter through the appropriate legal, compliance, privacy, security, or governance process.

---

### 153 — AI-VRM-153 — Conditional Vendor Approval

A vendor may receive conditional approval where identified risks can be controlled through explicit conditions.

Conditions may include:

* restricted data types;
* restricted users;
* limited functionality;
* additional monitoring;
* compensating controls;
* contractual amendments;
* remediation deadlines;
* architectural isolation; or
* temporary approval periods.

Conditions should be measurable and assigned to accountable owners.

---

### 154 — AI-VRM-154 — Vendor Remediation Tracking

Material vendor deficiencies should be tracked through a formal remediation process.

Each issue should have, where appropriate:

* issue description;
* risk rating;
* affected service;
* responsible owner;
* remediation action;
* target date;
* evidence requirement;
* status;
* escalation criteria; and
* closure approval.

Vendor remediation should be monitored until objective evidence demonstrates completion.

---

### 155 — AI-VRM-155 — Vendor Exception Management

Exceptions to AI vendor requirements should be formally documented.

An exception record should identify:

* requirement;
* requested deviation;
* business justification;
* risk;
* compensating controls;
* approval authority;
* expiration date;
* review date; and
* remediation plan where applicable.

Exceptions should not become permanent substitutes for required controls.

---

### 156 — AI-VRM-156 — Vendor Risk Acceptance Authority

The organization should establish who has authority to accept residual AI vendor risk.

Authority should be proportional to:

* vendor criticality;
* data sensitivity;
* business impact;
* regulatory exposure;
* AI risk;
* residual risk; and
* duration of the exception or acceptance.

Individuals should not approve risks outside their delegated authority.

---

### 157 — AI-VRM-157 — Vendor Onboarding Evidence Package

Before production approval, the organization should maintain an evidence package appropriate to vendor criticality.

It may contain:

* vendor assessment;
* security questionnaire;
* privacy assessment;
* AI risk assessment;
* architecture information;
* data-flow assessment;
* assurance reports;
* contractual requirements;
* remediation records;
* risk acceptance;
* approval decision; and
* planned monitoring requirements.

The evidence package provides the basis for later reassessment and auditability.

---

### 158 — AI-VRM-158 — Vendor Approval Traceability

The organization should be able to reconstruct why an AI vendor was approved.

The record should show:

1. intended use case;
2. identified risks;
3. evidence reviewed;
4. control gaps;
5. residual risk;
6. decision authority;
7. approval conditions;
8. approval date; and
9. reassessment requirements.

This creates an auditable decision trail rather than relying on undocumented procurement decisions.

---

### 159 — AI-VRM-159 — Vendor Assessment Recency

Vendor assurance evidence should have an appropriate validity period.

The organization should consider:

* evidence age;
* vendor criticality;
* rate of service change;
* model-change frequency;
* regulatory developments;
* security threat environment; and
* materiality of the dependency.

Older evidence may remain useful but should not automatically be treated as current assurance.

---

### 160 — AI-VRM-160 — Vendor Reassessment Trigger

The organization should define events that trigger vendor reassessment.

Examples include:

* material model change;
* material service change;
* security incident;
* privacy incident;
* new subprocessor;
* ownership change;
* regulatory change;
* material control failure;
* significant architecture change;
* increased data sensitivity;
* increased business criticality; or
* planned expansion of the use case.

These are governance triggers derived from risk-management practice; they are not automatically statutory requirements unless a specific law, regulation, or contract makes them mandatory.

---

### 161 — AI-VRM-161 — Continuous Vendor Monitoring

Material AI vendors should be monitored according to their risk tier.

Monitoring may cover:

* security incidents;
* vulnerabilities;
* service availability;
* model changes;
* subprocessor changes;
* regulatory developments;
* contractual compliance;
* assurance evidence;
* financial viability;
* concentration exposure; and
* material changes to the service.

Monitoring frequency should be proportionate to risk.

---

### 162 — AI-VRM-162 — Vendor Risk Reassessment After Incident

A material vendor incident should trigger evaluation of whether the organization's existing vendor risk assessment remains valid.

The review should consider:

* root cause;
* affected services;
* organizational exposure;
* control effectiveness;
* residual risk;
* contractual performance;
* required remediation; and
* continued business necessity.

The incident itself does not establish that the vendor relationship must terminate; the decision should follow documented risk analysis.

---

### 163 — AI-VRM-163 — Vendor Concentration Assessment

Organizations should assess concentration risk where multiple critical AI services depend on:

* the same cloud provider;
* the same foundation-model provider;
* the same AI API;
* the same model marketplace;
* the same geographic region;
* the same infrastructure provider; or
* the same critical subprocessor.

Concentration may create correlated failure across apparently separate vendors.

---

### 164 — AI-VRM-164 — Vendor Exit Feasibility Assessment

Critical AI vendor assessments should consider whether the organization can realistically exit the relationship.

Assessment should examine:

* data portability;
* model portability;
* application dependencies;
* API compatibility;
* replacement providers;
* migration cost;
* migration time;
* retraining requirements;
* contractual restrictions;
* intellectual-property constraints; and
* operational dependencies.

Exit feasibility should be evaluated before dependency becomes difficult to reverse.

---

### 165 — AI-VRM-165 — Vendor Lock-In Assessment

The organization should identify technical and contractual mechanisms that could create excessive vendor lock-in.

Examples include:

* proprietary APIs;
* proprietary model formats;
* proprietary embeddings;
* proprietary vector stores;
* proprietary orchestration;
* non-portable workflows;
* unique model capabilities;
* contractual restrictions; and
* accumulated proprietary configuration.

Lock-in should be considered alongside business value rather than treated automatically as unacceptable.

---

### 166 — AI-VRM-166 — Vendor Exit Controls

Where exit risk is material, contracts and architecture should support:

* data export;
* configuration export;
* credential revocation;
* service termination;
* data deletion;
* migration;
* replacement;
* transition support; and
* verification of termination.

Exit controls should be tested where the AI dependency is sufficiently critical.

The AI vendor risk program should maintain measurable indicators.

* percentage of AI vendors assessed;

Metrics should support governance decisions rather than merely measure administrative activity.

### 168 — AI-VRM-168 — Vendor Key Risk Indicators

AI vendor KRIs should focus on conditions that may materially increase organizational exposure.

Examples include:

* material service outages;
* rapid dependency concentration;
* unresolved critical vulnerabilities;
* significant regulatory changes;
* loss of assurance;

Thresholds should be defined according to organizational risk appetite.

### 169 — AI-VRM-169 — Vendor Assessment Quality Review

The second line or equivalent oversight function should periodically review the quality of vendor assessments.

* cover material AI risks;

* identify fourth-party dependencies;
* address contractual requirements;
* document residual risk; and
* result in appropriate approvals.

This helps prevent AI vendor management from becoming a procurement-only administrative exercise.

### 171 — AI-VRM-171 — Vendor Governance Review

The AI vendor governance program should periodically be reviewed for effectiveness.

### 172 — AI-VRM-172 — Vendor Risk Decision Principle

The decision to use an AI vendor should be based on the relationship between:

* business need;
* use-case risk;
* data sensitivity;
* AI capability;
* vendor assurance;
* contractual protection;
* technical controls;
* regulatory requirements;
* residual risk; and
* exit feasibility.

Vendor size, reputation, brand recognition, or market position should not by themselves determine the risk decision.

---

### 173 — AI-VRM-173 — Vendor Evidence Hierarchy

Evidence should generally be weighted according to its specificity, independence, recency, and verifiability.

A practical hierarchy may distinguish:

1. independently verified evidence;
2. contractual commitments;
3. service-specific technical documentation;
4. configuration evidence;
5. formal vendor policies;
6. vendor questionnaires;
7. vendor statements;
8. marketing claims.

This hierarchy is a governance methodology, not a legal hierarchy.

---

### 174 — AI-VRM-174 — Evidence Scope Matching

Evidence should match the actual service being procured.

For example, a certification covering a vendor's corporate information-security management system should not automatically be interpreted as evidence covering:

* every AI model;
* every cloud region;
* every subprocessor;
* every API;
* every product;
* every customer configuration; or
* every regulatory obligation.

Scope matching should be explicitly documented.

---

### 175 — AI-VRM-175 — Vendor Assurance Limitation Register

Material limitations in vendor assurance should be recorded.

Examples include:

* excluded services;
* excluded locations;
* unavailable testing;
* outdated evidence;
* unverified controls;
* limited audit rights;
* reliance on vendor self-attestation; and
* undisclosed fourth-party dependencies.

The limitations should feed into residual-risk evaluation.

---

### 176 — AI-VRM-176 — Vendor Security Control Mapping

The organization should map material vendor controls to its internal requirements and applicable external obligations.

The mapping may include:

* security requirements;
* privacy requirements;
* AI governance requirements;
* resilience requirements;
* contractual requirements;
* regulatory requirements; and
* internal policies.

The mapping should identify control ownership between the vendor and the organization.

---

### 177 — AI-VRM-177 — Shared Responsibility Assessment

AI vendor risk should explicitly document which controls are owned by:

* the vendor;
* the organization;
* both parties;
* a subprocessor; or
* another infrastructure provider.

Shared responsibility should be documented at the service level because responsibility can vary between AI products and deployment models.

---

### 178 — AI-VRM-178 — Vendor Control Dependency

Where an organizational control depends on a vendor capability, that dependency should be documented.

Examples include:

* vendor logging supporting organizational monitoring;
* vendor deletion supporting privacy processes;
* vendor availability supporting business continuity;
* vendor identity federation supporting access governance;
* vendor incident notification supporting incident response; and
* vendor model documentation supporting AI risk assessment.

A control that depends on an external capability should not be considered fully effective until that dependency is understood and sufficiently assured.

---

### 179 — AI-VRM-179 — Vendor Control Failure Escalation

Material vendor control failures should have defined escalation paths.

Escalation may involve:

* business owner;
* security;
* privacy;
* legal;
* procurement;
* enterprise risk;
* compliance;
* AI governance committee; or
* executive management.

Escalation thresholds should be defined in advance where practical.

---

### 180 — AI-VRM-180 — Vendor Governance Operating Principle

AI vendor governance should operate as a lifecycle control rather than a one-time procurement assessment.

The lifecycle should connect:

**Vendor Identification → Risk Classification → Due Diligence → Evidence Validation → Contracting → Approval → Monitoring → Reassessment → Remediation → Renewal or Exit**

The objective is to ensure that AI vendor risk remains visible and governed throughout the dependency's operational life.
