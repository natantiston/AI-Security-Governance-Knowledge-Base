# 16.05 — Cloud AI Provider Risk

## Part 1 — Foundations, Cloud AI Dependency, Shared Responsibility, Architecture, Data Boundaries and Governance

### 1. Cloud AI Provider Risk

Cloud AI provider risk is the risk arising when an organization depends on an external cloud provider to deliver AI capabilities, infrastructure, platforms, models, data-processing services, or supporting security functions.

The risk extends beyond ordinary cloud infrastructure because AI workloads can introduce additional dependencies involving:

* foundation models;
* model inference;
* training infrastructure;
* AI APIs;
* managed machine-learning platforms;
* vector databases;
* AI data pipelines;
* model-serving infrastructure;
* AI development environments;
* AI agents;
* AI-specific security services.

Cloud AI provider risk should therefore be evaluated across the complete service and dependency chain.

**Control ID: `AI-CLOUD-001 — Cloud AI Provider Risk`**

---

### 2. Objective of Cloud AI Provider Governance

The objective is to ensure that the organization understands and manages the risks created by using cloud providers for AI workloads.

Governance should establish visibility into:

* what AI services are being consumed;
* where AI workloads operate;
* what information is processed;
* which models are used;
* which cloud services support the AI workload;
* who controls each security boundary;
* what dependencies exist;
* how incidents are handled;
* how service continuity is maintained;
* how the organization can exit or migrate.

**Control ID: `AI-CLOUD-002 — Cloud AI Governance Objective`**

---

### 3. Cloud AI Provider as a Third-Party Dependency

A cloud AI provider is an external dependency even when the organization manages the application, model configuration, or data itself.

The dependency may include:

* compute;
* storage;
* networking;
* managed AI services;
* model hosting;
* APIs;
* identity services;
* security services;
* monitoring;
* backup;
* disaster recovery.

The organization should identify which capabilities depend directly on the provider and which depend on the provider's underlying infrastructure ecosystem.

**Control ID: `AI-CLOUD-003 — Cloud AI Third-Party Dependency`**

---

### 4. Cloud AI Versus Conventional Cloud Services

AI workloads introduce characteristics that may not exist in conventional cloud applications.

These can include:

* probabilistic model behavior;
* model version changes;
* training-data dependencies;
* inference APIs;
* embeddings;
* vector search;
* prompt processing;
* model-specific security controls;
* AI agent execution;
* model-provider dependencies.

Traditional cloud controls remain relevant, but additional AI-specific assessment may be necessary.

**Control ID: `AI-CLOUD-004 — Cloud AI Distinction`**

### 50. Cloud AI Governance Principle

Cloud AI governance should begin with clear understanding of the **provider, service model, AI workload, model dependency, data boundary, shared-responsibility boundary, identity architecture, infrastructure dependency, and business criticality**.

The organization should not assume that securing the application alone secures the complete AI workload. Effective governance requires understanding the complete cloud AI dependency chain and assigning responsibility for every material control.

**Control ID: `AI-CLOUD-050 — Cloud AI Governance Principle`**

### Core Principles

1. **Provider Principle** — Identify every material provider supporting the AI workload.
2. **Service-Model Principle** — Understand the cloud service model and resulting responsibility boundary.
3. **Shared-Responsibility Principle** — Clearly distinguish provider controls from customer controls.
4. **Workload Principle** — Govern the complete AI workload rather than only the application.
5. **Dependency Principle** — Identify underlying model, cloud, infrastructure, data, and identity dependencies.
6. **Architecture Principle** — Maintain an accurate architecture and trust-boundary view.
7. **Data-Boundary Principle** — Know what information enters, leaves, and remains within the cloud AI environment.
8. **Minimization Principle** — Process only information necessary for the approved AI use case.
9. **Identity Principle** — Treat human and workload identity as primary security boundaries.
10. **Least-Privilege Principle** — Grant only the permissions required for legitimate AI operations.
11. **API-Security Principle** — Protect model, data, management, and integration APIs.
12. **Network Principle** — Apply appropriate ingress, egress, and segmentation controls.
13. **Encryption Principle** — Protect AI data and artifacts through appropriate encryption and key management.
14. **Monitoring Principle** — Maintain sufficient visibility into material AI cloud activity.
15. **Portability Principle** — Assess practical portability before significant provider dependency develops.
16. **Lock-In Principle** — Identify provider-specific dependencies that may increase migration difficulty.
17. **Criticality Principle** — Apply stronger governance to AI workloads supporting critical business processes.
18. **Evidence Principle** — Validate provider claims using evidence appropriate to the risk.
19. **Accountability Principle** — Assign clear ownership for every material customer-side control.
20. **Governance Principle** — Treat cloud AI as an integrated third-party, cloud, AI, data, cybersecurity, and business dependency.

20. **Governance Principle** — Cloud AI risk should be managed as an integrated cloud, AI, cybersecurity, data, privacy, third-party, and business dependency.

### Core Principles

1. **Continuous-Monitoring Principle** — Cloud AI risk should be monitored throughout the service lifecycle.
2. **Change-Assessment Principle** — Material provider, model, configuration, data, or architectural changes should be assessed.
3. **Behavioral-Assurance Principle** — Technical availability does not prove behavioral stability.
4. **Regression Principle** — Material AI changes should be evaluated against established requirements.
5. **Configuration-Integrity Principle** — Approved cloud AI configurations should be protected against unmanaged drift.
6. **Identity-Monitoring Principle** — Privileged and workload identities require continuous oversight.
7. **Data-Boundary Principle** — Material changes in data flow or processing should be identified.
8. **Subprocessor-Principle** — Material fourth-party dependencies should remain visible.
9. **Incident-Principle** — Provider incidents should be assessed against the organization's own impact and obligations.
10. **Evidence-Principle** — Monitoring should generate reliable evidence for governance decisions.
11. **Regulatory-Applicability Principle** — Regulatory developments require applicability analysis rather than automatic assumptions.
12. **Concentration-Principle** — Provider count alone does not establish effective diversification.
13. **Dependency-Principle** — Cloud AI dependencies should be mapped to understand blast radius.
14. **Resilience-Principle** — Recovery capabilities should be tested rather than merely documented.
15. **Fallback-Principle** — Material AI services should have appropriate fallback or graceful-degradation strategies.
16. **Portability-Principle** — Exit capability depends on practical portability, not merely contractual export rights.
17. **Exit-Readiness Principle** — Critical cloud AI dependencies should have proportionate exit planning.
18. **Assurance-Principle** — Provider assurance should be evaluated for scope, currency, independence, and customer responsibilities.
19. **Automation Principle** — Automated controls should be governed, tested, monitored, and subject to appropriate human oversight.
20. **Continuous-Assurance Principle** — Monitoring, testing, incidents, assurance, and risk decisions should operate as an integrated governance lifecycle.

Cloud AI provider governance should treat the provider relationship as a continuously changing risk dependency rather than a one-time procurement decision.

**Control ID: AI-CLOUD-210**

### Core Principles

1. **Continuous-Monitoring Principle** — Cloud AI risk should be monitored throughout the service lifecycle.
2. **Change-Assessment Principle** — Material provider, model, configuration, data, or architectural changes should be assessed.
3. **Behavioral-Assurance Principle** — Technical availability does not prove behavioral stability.
4. **Regression Principle** — Material AI changes should be evaluated against established requirements.
5. **Configuration-Integrity Principle** — Approved cloud AI configurations should be protected against unmanaged drift.
6. **Identity-Monitoring Principle** — Privileged and workload identities require continuous oversight.
7. **Data-Boundary Principle** — Material changes in data flow or processing should be identified.
8. **Subprocessor-Principle** — Material fourth-party dependencies should remain visible.
9. **Incident-Principle** — Provider incidents should be assessed against the organization's own impact and obligations.
10. **Evidence-Principle** — Monitoring should generate reliable evidence for governance decisions.
11. **Regulatory-Applicability Principle** — Regulatory developments require applicability analysis rather than automatic assumptions.
12. **Concentration-Principle** — Provider count alone does not establish effective diversification.
13. **Dependency-Principle** — Cloud AI dependencies should be mapped to understand blast radius.
14. **Resilience-Principle** — Recovery capabilities should be tested rather than merely documented.
15. **Fallback-Principle** — Material AI services should have appropriate fallback or graceful-degradation strategies.
16. **Portability-Principle** — Exit capability depends on practical portability, not merely contractual export rights.
17. **Exit-Readiness Principle** — Critical cloud AI dependencies should have proportionate exit planning.
18. **Assurance-Principle** — Provider assurance should be evaluated for scope, currency, independence, and customer responsibilities.
19. **Automation Principle** — Automated controls should be governed, tested, monitored, and subject to appropriate human oversight.
20. **Continuous-Assurance Principle** — Monitoring, testing, incidents, assurance, and risk decisions should operate as an integrated governance lifecycle.
