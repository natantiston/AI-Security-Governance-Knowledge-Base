# 10.13 — Membership Inference Attacks

## Part 1 — Foundations and Core Concepts

# 1. Definition of Membership Inference Attacks

A **Membership Inference Attack (MIA)** is an attack in which an adversary attempts to determine whether a particular data record, individual, transaction, document, image, or other data object was included in the dataset used to train or fine-tune an AI model.

The attacker does not necessarily attempt to recover the complete underlying record.

Instead, the fundamental question is:

> **“Was this specific piece of information part of the model's training data?”**

For example, an attacker may possess a candidate customer record and attempt to determine whether that customer was included in a company's training dataset.

```text
Candidate Record
       |
       v
    AI Model
       |
       v
Behavioral Analysis
       |
       v
"Was this record in training?"
```

Membership inference therefore represents an **information disclosure and privacy threat**.

---

# 2. Membership Inference as a Privacy Attack

Membership inference is primarily concerned with determining **dataset membership**.

This can be sensitive even when the underlying record cannot be reconstructed.

For example:

* whether a person was a customer;
* whether a patient appeared in a medical dataset;
* whether an employee participated in a confidential investigation;
* whether a company was part of a restricted transaction dataset;
* whether a particular individual appeared in a legal dataset.

The membership itself may reveal sensitive information.

---

# 3. Why Membership Can Be Sensitive

The fact that a person or organization appears in a dataset can itself constitute confidential information.

Consider:

```text
Dataset:
Cancer Treatment Patients

Attacker:
"Was Person X included?"

Model:
High-confidence inference

Potential Disclosure:
Person X may have received cancer treatment.
```

The model does not need to reveal the medical record.

Confirming membership may already create significant privacy harm.

---

# 4. Membership Inference vs Model Inversion

Membership inference and model inversion are related but distinct.

| Attack               | Primary Objective                          |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Membership Inference | Determine whether a record was in training |
| Model Inversion      | Infer information about training data      |
| Data Extraction      | Recover memorized information              |
| Attribute Inference  | Infer sensitive attributes                 |
| Prompt Injection     | Manipulate model instructions              |
| Model Extraction     | Reconstruct model behavior                 |

The distinction is important because a model can resist direct data reconstruction while still leaking membership information.

---

# 5. Membership Inference vs Data Extraction

A membership inference attack asks:

> “Was this record included?”

Data extraction asks:

> “Can I obtain the record or memorized information?”

For example:

```text
Known Candidate:
John Smith — Customer ID 4821

Membership Inference:
"Was John Smith included?"

Extraction:
"Give me information about John Smith."
```

Successful membership inference does not necessarily require successful extraction.

---

# 6. Membership Inference vs Sensitive Attribute Inference

Membership inference determines whether an entity is present in a dataset.

Attribute inference attempts to determine sensitive characteristics.

For example:

```text
Membership:
"Was Alice included in the medical dataset?"

Attribute:
"What medical condition does Alice have?"
```

These attacks can be combined.

An attacker who first establishes membership may gain additional confidence when attempting to infer sensitive attributes.

---

# 7. Membership Inference and Machine-Learning Models

Membership inference is closely associated with machine-learning models because models may behave differently on data they have seen during training compared with previously unseen data.

Potential signals can include:

* confidence;
* prediction probabilities;
* loss values;
* response consistency;
* classification behavior;
* likelihood estimates;
* generated responses.

The degree of exposure depends heavily on the model architecture, training process, interface, and information returned to the attacker.

---

# 8. Membership Inference in Generative AI

Generative AI introduces additional complexity.

Large language models and other generative models may process enormous datasets containing:

* documents;
* conversations;
* code;
* web content;
* business information;
* personal information.

A model may exhibit different behavior when prompted with information that closely resembles material encountered during training.

This does not mean every recognizable response proves training-data membership.

Membership inference requires a defensible statistical or behavioral inference process rather than simply observing that the model knows something.

---

# 9. Training Data as a Security Asset

Training data should be treated as a security-sensitive asset when it contains confidential or personal information.

```text
Sensitive Dataset
       |
       v
Training Pipeline
       |
       v
Model
       |
       v
Potential Information Leakage
```

Once sensitive information is used to train a model, the model itself becomes part of the information-security boundary.

The dataset cannot be considered protected merely because the original database remains inaccessible.

---

# 10. Overfitting and Membership Inference

**Overfitting** can increase membership-inference risk.

An overfit model may behave unusually well on training examples while behaving differently on unseen examples.

Conceptually:

```text
Training Record
      |
      v
Strong Model Familiarity
      |
      v
Distinctive Behavior
      |
      v
Membership Signal
```

This difference can provide an attacker with information about whether a candidate record was included in training.

---

# 11. Memorization and Membership Signals

Memorization can also contribute to membership inference.

A model may retain statistical or behavioral characteristics associated with training examples.

However:

> **Memorization and membership inference are not identical.**

A model can leak membership information without reproducing the original record.

Likewise, a model can memorize particular content without providing a reliable membership signal.

---

# 12. Confidence Scores as an Attack Surface

Detailed confidence or probability information can increase membership-inference exposure.

For example:

```text
Prediction:
Class A = 99.8%
Class B = 0.2%
```

If training examples systematically receive different confidence distributions than unseen examples, attackers may use those differences as membership signals.

Therefore, detailed confidence information should not automatically be exposed to every user.

---

# 13. Probability and Likelihood Outputs

Probability distributions, token likelihoods, loss information, and related statistical outputs can provide additional information about model behavior.

The more detailed the interface, the greater the potential analytical surface available to an attacker.

Organizations should therefore evaluate whether such outputs are necessary for the intended business purpose.

---

# 14. Repeated Query Analysis

Membership inference can involve repeated interaction with a model.

An attacker may vary inputs and compare responses to identify statistical differences.

Conceptually:

```text
Query 1
   |
Query 2
   |
Query 3
   |
Query 4
   |
   v
Behavioral Comparison
   |
   v
Membership Hypothesis
```

This means that individual queries may appear harmless while the aggregate interaction becomes suspicious.

---

# 15. Black-Box Membership Inference

A **black-box attack** occurs when the attacker interacts with the model through an exposed interface without having access to internal model parameters.

The attacker may observe:

* predictions;
* responses;
* confidence values;
* probabilities;
* API behavior;
* response timing;
* other permitted outputs.

Black-box exposure is particularly relevant to public and enterprise AI APIs.

---

# 16. White-Box Membership Inference

A **white-box attack** assumes the attacker has deeper access to the model.

Potentially available information could include:

* model parameters;
* gradients;
* internal representations;
* architecture;
* training configuration;
* model artifacts.

Such access can provide substantially more information for inference.

Consequently, protection of model artifacts remains an important security control.

---

# 17. Membership Inference and Model APIs

An AI model exposed through an API becomes an information-inference interface.

The security boundary includes:

```text
User
 |
 v
Authentication
 |
 v
API
 |
 v
Model Gateway
 |
 v
Model
 |
 v
Response
```

Security teams should evaluate not only whether unauthorized users can access the model, but also how much information authorized users can infer through repeated interaction.

---

# 18. Membership Inference and RAG

Retrieval-Augmented Generation can create additional membership-inference considerations.

A RAG system may expose whether particular information exists within its knowledge environment.

However, **retrieval presence is not automatically equivalent to model-training membership**.

The distinction should be maintained between:

* information stored in the retrieval system;
* information retrieved at runtime;
* information used during model training;
* information used during fine-tuning.

Each has different security implications.

---

# 19. Membership Inference and Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning can introduce additional membership risks when sensitive records are included in a specialized training dataset.

For example:

```text
Base Model
    +
Sensitive Fine-Tuning Dataset
    |
    v
Specialized Model
    |
    v
Potential Membership Leakage
```

Fine-tuning data should therefore be governed and tested independently from the original foundation model.

---

# 20. Membership Inference and Federated Learning

Federated learning can reduce the need to centrally collect raw training data, but it does not automatically eliminate membership-inference risk.

Information may potentially be exposed through:

* model updates;
* gradients;
* aggregation behavior;
* participant responses;
* model outputs.

Privacy and security controls must therefore consider the complete federated-learning lifecycle.

---

# 21. Membership Inference and Differential Privacy

Differential privacy can reduce membership-inference risk by limiting the influence of an individual record on the resulting model.

Conceptually:

```text
Individual Record
       |
       v
Privacy Mechanism
       |
       v
Training Process
       |
       v
Model
       |
       v
Reduced Membership Signal
```

Differential privacy is not a universal solution, but it provides a formal privacy mechanism that can reduce sensitivity to individual records.

---

# 22. Data Minimization

Data minimization is one of the strongest ways to reduce membership-inference exposure.

If sensitive information does not need to be included in training, it cannot create membership leakage from that training process.

Organizations should therefore ask:

1. Is this data necessary?
2. Is the entire record necessary?
3. Can sensitive fields be removed?
4. Can aggregation be used?
5. Can synthetic data be used?
6. Can privacy-preserving training be applied?

---

# 23. Data Classification

Training datasets should be classified according to sensitivity.

Example:

| Classification         | Membership Risk |
| ---------------------- | --------------- |
| Public                 | Low             |
| Internal               | Moderate        |
| Confidential           | High            |
| Personal               | High            |
| Regulated / Restricted | Very High       |

The more sensitive the dataset, the stronger the requirements should be for model access, privacy protection, testing, monitoring, and assurance.

---

# 24. Membership Inference and Access Control

Access control reduces the population capable of attempting membership inference.

Controls should include:

* authentication;
* authorization;
* application identity;
* role-based access;
* attribute-based access;
* privileged access management;
* access reviews.

However:

> **Authorization to use a model does not automatically authorize unlimited inference about its training data.**

---

# 25. Membership Inference and Excessive Information Exposure

Even legitimate model users may receive more information than required.

Examples include:

* confidence scores;
* probabilities;
* detailed explanations;
* diagnostic information;
* model metadata;
* unrestricted API access.

Reducing unnecessary information exposure can reduce the statistical signals available for membership inference.

---

# 26. Membership Inference and Query Economics

Attackers may use large numbers of queries to improve their inference confidence.

The attack therefore has an economic dimension:

```text
More Queries
     |
     v
More Observations
     |
     v
Better Statistical Signal
     |
     v
Higher Inference Confidence
```

Rate limits, quotas, and behavioral monitoring can increase the cost of systematic attacks.

---

# 27. Membership Inference and Tenant Isolation

Multi-tenant systems must prevent one customer from using model behavior to infer information about another customer's data.

Potential isolation boundaries include:

* datasets;
* models;
* fine-tuning jobs;
* embeddings;
* memory;
* caches;
* sessions;
* APIs.

Cross-tenant membership inference should be treated as a significant confidentiality failure.

---

# 28. Membership Inference and Privacy

Membership inference can transform seemingly harmless model behavior into a privacy disclosure.

For example:

```text
Model Knowledge
      +
Membership Signal
      |
      v
Sensitive Fact About Individual
```

The privacy impact depends not only on the model but also on what the dataset represents.

A membership result may reveal highly sensitive information even when the model does not reveal the underlying record.

---

# 29. Membership Inference and Confidentiality

The primary security objective is confidentiality.

The organization should prevent unauthorized parties from determining sensitive facts about the composition of protected datasets.

Confidentiality therefore extends beyond:

> “Can someone download the database?”

It must also address:

> “Can someone infer information about who or what is contained in the database through the AI system?”

---

# 30. Membership Inference and Integrity

Membership inference is primarily a confidentiality threat, but it can also support integrity attacks.

An attacker who learns the composition of a training dataset may use that knowledge to:

* identify weaknesses;
* manipulate future training data;
* target specific records;
* design adversarial examples;
* exploit known dataset characteristics.

Thus, information disclosure can become an enabler for subsequent attacks.

---

# 31. Membership Inference and Availability

Availability is generally a secondary concern.

However, systematic membership probing can consume:

* API capacity;
* model compute;
* inference resources;
* security-analysis resources.

Large-scale automated probing can therefore become an abuse or resource-exhaustion issue.

---

# 32. Membership Inference as Reconnaissance

Membership inference can function as reconnaissance.

An attacker may use it to discover:

* whether an organization has particular customers;
* whether an individual appears in a dataset;
* whether a particular transaction occurred;
* whether a particular document was processed;
* whether a sensitive population is represented.

The resulting knowledge can support more targeted attacks.

---

# 33. Membership Inference Attack Chaining

Membership inference can be combined with other AI attacks.

Example:

```text
Model Access
     |
     v
Membership Inference
     |
     v
Dataset Discovery
     |
     v
Sensitive Attribute Inference
     |
     v
Targeted Information Disclosure
```

It can also be combined with:

* model inversion;
* data extraction;
* prompt injection;
* indirect prompt injection;
* model extraction;
* social engineering;
* account compromise.

Therefore, membership inference should not be assessed in isolation.

---

# 34. Membership Inference Attack Surface

The attack surface may include:

* public model APIs;
* enterprise AI applications;
* ML prediction services;
* generative AI interfaces;
* fine-tuned models;
* model endpoints;
* confidence APIs;
* probability APIs;
* embeddings;
* model artifacts;
* federated-learning infrastructure;
* evaluation interfaces.

Every additional interface can potentially provide information useful to an attacker.

# 36. Membership Inference Risk Factors

Risk can increase with:

* sensitive training data;
* small datasets;
* rare records;
* overfitting;
* memorization;
* repeated model access;
* detailed confidence outputs;
* unrestricted APIs;
* insufficient query controls;
* weak tenant isolation;
* vulnerable fine-tuning;
* absence of privacy-preserving training;
* model artifact exposure.

The combination of these conditions can materially increase membership-inference risk.

---

# 37. Rare and Unique Records

Rare records can create elevated inference risk.

If a record is highly distinctive, model behavior may provide stronger signals about its presence.

For example:

```text
Common Record
     |
Weak Signal

Unique / Rare Record
     |
Potentially Stronger Signal
```

Dataset uniqueness should therefore be considered during privacy and security assessments.

---

# 38. Membership Inference and Small Datasets

Small datasets can create additional risk because individual records may have greater influence on model behavior.

This is particularly relevant to:

* specialized models;
* internal datasets;
* small medical datasets;
* proprietary research datasets;
* niche financial datasets.

The risk should be evaluated before training models on small sensitive populations.

---

# 39. Membership Inference and High-Impact AI

Membership inference becomes particularly important when AI models process information related to high-impact domains.

Examples may include:

* healthcare;
* financial services;
* employment;
* insurance;
* education;
* legal services;
* government services.

A membership disclosure in such environments may have significant privacy, legal, financial, or reputational consequences.

---

# 40. Enterprise Model Inversion and Membership Inference Relationship

Membership inference should be evaluated alongside model inversion.

```text
                 AI Model
                    |
          +---------+---------+
          |                   |
          v                   v
 Membership Inference    Model Inversion
 "Was it included?"      "What can be inferred?"
          |                   |
          +---------+---------+
                    |
                    v
             Privacy Exposure
```

The two attacks answer different questions but may share underlying weaknesses such as memorization, overfitting, excessive model exposure, and insufficient privacy controls.

---

The membership-inference security boundary should extend across:

# 42. Zero Trust for Model Information

Zero Trust principles should apply to information obtained from AI models.

The organization should not assume:

> “The user is authorized to use the model, therefore any information inferable from the model is authorized.”

Instead, access decisions should consider:

* identity;
* purpose;
* model sensitivity;
* requested capability;
* output type;
* data scope;
* behavioral risk.

---

# 43. Enterprise Membership Inference Risk Model

A practical enterprise risk model can consider:

```text
Membership Inference Risk
=

×

×

```

Additional factors may include:

* number of affected individuals;
* regulatory obligations;
* attacker capability;
* model memorization;
* detectability;
* tenant architecture.

This enables organizations to prioritize the models presenting the greatest privacy and confidentiality exposure.

---

# 44. Membership Inference Security Objectives

Enterprise security objectives should include:

1. Minimize sensitive data used for training.
2. Restrict access to sensitive models.
3. Minimize unnecessary model information exposure.
4. Reduce membership signals.
5. Detect systematic probing.
6. Limit query-based attacks.
7. Protect model artifacts.
8. Test membership-inference resistance.
9. Isolate tenants and datasets.
10. Monitor model behavior.
11. Contain confirmed information leakage.
12. Maintain evidence of control effectiveness.

---

# 45. Defense-in-Depth Principle

Membership inference should never be addressed through a single control.

A layered architecture should combine:

```text
Data Minimization
       +
Privacy-Preserving Training
       +
Model Access Control
       +
Output Minimization
       +
Query Controls
       +
Tenant Isolation
       +
Monitoring
       +
Membership Testing
       +
Incident Response
```

If one layer fails, independent controls should reduce the likelihood and impact of information disclosure.

---

# 46. Fundamental Security Principle

> **Treat membership information as potentially sensitive information and treat the AI model as a potential inference channel through which that information can be disclosed.**

The organization should therefore assume that an attacker may attempt to determine whether sensitive records were used to train or fine-tune an AI model.

The security boundary must protect not only the original dataset, but also the **model, model interface, query process, outputs, and surrounding AI infrastructure** that could reveal information about dataset membership.


## Part 2 — Technical Security and Control Mechanisms

# 47. Membership Inference Defense Architecture

Membership-inference protection should use multiple independent controls across the AI lifecycle.

```text
                    AI Security Boundary
                           |
        +------------------+------------------+
        |                  |                  |
        v                  v                  v
   Data Controls      Model Controls     Interface Controls
        |                  |                  |
   Minimization        Access Control      Query Limits
   Privacy             Artifact Security   Rate Limits
   Isolation            Model Hardening    Output Control
        |                  |                  |
        +------------------+------------------+
                           |
                           v
                    Monitoring / SOC
                           |
                           v
                    Testing / Assurance
```

The objective is to reduce the ability of an attacker to obtain reliable membership signals from model behavior.

---

# 48. Training Data Minimization

Only data necessary for the approved AI purpose should be included in training or fine-tuning.

Controls should evaluate:

* unnecessary records;
* unnecessary attributes;
* duplicate records;
* historical records no longer required;
* sensitive identifiers;
* highly unique information.

Reducing the amount of sensitive information entering the training process directly reduces potential membership exposure.

---

# 49. Sensitive Attribute Reduction

Sensitive attributes should be removed, generalized, aggregated, or transformed where they are not required.

Examples include:

* exact dates → broader time periods;
* exact locations → geographic regions;
* individual identifiers → pseudonymous identifiers;
* precise financial values → ranges.

The objective is to reduce the privacy significance of individual training records.

---

# 50. Data Classification

Training data should be classified before entering the AI pipeline.

Example:

```text id="u9s4ye"
Public
  |
Internal
  |
Confidential
  |
Personal
  |
Restricted
```

Classification should determine the required level of:

* privacy protection;
* access control;
* training isolation;
* model testing;
* monitoring;
* assurance.

---

# 51. Training Data Authorization

Only explicitly authorized datasets should be permitted to enter model-training pipelines.

Authorization should verify:

* dataset identity;
* owner;
* approved purpose;
* classification;
* approved model;
* retention requirements;
* applicable privacy requirements.

A dataset being technically accessible does not mean it is authorized for AI training.

---

# 52. Training Dataset Isolation

Sensitive datasets should be isolated from general-purpose development environments.

```text id="x9qjfk"
Restricted Dataset
       |
       v
Controlled Training Environment
       |
       v
Approved Model
       |
       v
Production Registry
```

This reduces unauthorized copying, accidental reuse, and uncontrolled model creation from sensitive data.

---

# 53. Dataset Integrity Controls

Training datasets should be protected against unauthorized modification.

Controls may include:

* cryptographic hashes;
* signed datasets;
* version control;
* access control;
* immutable storage;
* modification logging.

Dataset integrity is important because unauthorized changes can alter the resulting model and its privacy characteristics.

---

# 54. Dataset Version Control

Every production model should be traceable to specific dataset versions.

Example:

```text id="1j6b6h"
Model v4.2
   |
   +-- Dataset A v7
   +-- Dataset B v3
   +-- Fine-Tuning Set v5
   |
   v
Security Test Results
```

This allows organizations to identify which data contributed to a model when investigating membership-inference exposure.

---

# 55. Model Artifact Protection

Model weights, checkpoints, adapters, and related artifacts should be protected through:

* encryption;
* repository access control;
* privileged-access management;
* integrity verification;
* secure storage;
* controlled distribution.

White-box access can provide attackers with significantly more information for membership analysis.

---

# 56. Model Access Control

Access to models should require strong authentication and authorization.

Controls should include:

* user authentication;
* application identity;
* role-based access;
* attribute-based access;
* service identity;
* privileged access controls;
* periodic access review.

Sensitive models should not be exposed through unrestricted endpoints.

---

# 57. Fine-Grained Model Authorization

Authorization should consider the specific model capability being requested.

```text id="l3nqsp"
Identity
   |
   v
Application
   |
   v
Model
   |
   +--> Standard Prediction
   |
   +--> Detailed Probability
   |
   +--> Diagnostic Output
   |
   +--> Embedding Access
```

A user authorized for ordinary prediction should not automatically receive detailed information that could strengthen membership inference.

---

# 58. Output Minimization

AI systems should expose only the information necessary for the business function.

Potentially unnecessary outputs include:

* confidence scores;
* probability distributions;
* likelihood values;
* internal diagnostics;
* detailed model explanations;
* intermediate representations.

Reducing output detail can reduce the statistical information available to an attacker.

---

# 59. Confidence Score Protection

Confidence information should be treated as a potentially sensitive model interface.

Where confidence values are not operationally required, organizations should consider:

* removing them;
* coarsening them;
* rounding them;
* limiting access;
* applying thresholds.

The objective is to reduce distinguishability between training and non-training records.

---

# 60. Query Rate Limiting

Rate limiting should restrict excessive model interaction.

For example:

```text id="d4j6wv"
Normal Usage
     |
     v
Rate Threshold
     |
     +----> Continue
     |
     +----> Throttle
     |
     +----> Block
```

Rate limiting increases the cost of systematic probing.

---

# 61. Query Quotas

Per-user, per-application, and per-tenant quotas can limit the total number of model interactions.

Quota policies may consider:

* model sensitivity;
* user role;
* application;
* time period;
* cumulative requests.

High-risk models should generally have stricter quotas than public or low-sensitivity models.

---

# 62. Query Pattern Detection

Security controls should analyze the relationship between queries rather than evaluating every query independently.

Potential indicators include:

* repeated candidate records;
* systematic input variation;
* repeated classification attempts;
* unusual query sequences;
* automated probing;
* high-volume statistical testing.

```text id="i5t7eq"
Query A
Query A'
Query A''
Query A'''
   |
   v
Pattern Correlation
   |
   v
Potential Membership Testing
```

---

# 63. Behavioral Anomaly Detection

Behavioral analytics can identify activity inconsistent with normal model usage.

Signals may include:

* sudden query-volume increases;
* unusual access times;
* new application identities;
* automated interaction;
* repeated requests against rare cases;
* simultaneous access to multiple sensitive models.

Behavioral detection should supplement deterministic controls.

---

# 64. Output Filtering

Model responses should be inspected before being returned to the requester.

Filtering can evaluate:

* sensitive information;
* restricted attributes;
* excessive model detail;
* unexpected identifiers;
* prohibited output types.

The filtering layer should remain independent of model-generated instructions.

---

# 65. Sensitive Information Detection

AI output should be evaluated for potentially sensitive information that could reveal dataset membership.

Detection may include:

* identifiers;
* personal information;
* confidential classifications;
* medical or financial attributes;
* restricted business information.

Detection should support blocking, redaction, or escalation according to risk.

---

# 66. Semantic Output Inspection

Simple keyword filtering is insufficient for membership-inference protection.

Semantic inspection should consider whether the response effectively communicates:

> “This individual was part of the protected dataset.”

Even when no direct identifier appears, contextual information may create a meaningful membership signal.

---

# 67. Query Isolation

High-risk queries should be isolated from unrelated model interactions.

Controls may include:

* dedicated sessions;
* isolated execution contexts;
* restricted memory;
* separate caches;
* controlled conversation history.

This reduces the possibility that information from one interaction influences another.

---

# 68. Tenant Isolation

Multi-tenant AI systems should enforce strict separation between customer environments.

Isolation should cover:

* training data;
* fine-tuning data;
* models;
* model adapters;
* embeddings;
* caches;
* memory;
* sessions;
* logs.

A model interaction by Tenant A should not provide information about whether Tenant B's data was used or accessed.

---

# 69. Session Isolation

User sessions should be isolated to prevent cross-session information leakage.

Controls should address:

* conversation memory;
* temporary context;
* cached outputs;
* session identifiers;
* inference state.

Session isolation is especially important where models maintain conversational or application-level state.

---

# 70. Memory Security

Persistent AI memory can create additional membership-inference exposure.

Controls should govern:

* what information is stored;
* who can access memory;
* retention;
* deletion;
* tenant separation;
* retrieval authorization.

Memory should not silently become an additional repository of sensitive training or interaction data.

---

# 71. Embedding Access Controls

Embeddings can expose information about underlying data and should not automatically be considered harmless representations.

Controls should include:

* access authorization;
* tenant isolation;
* encryption;
* repository security;
* controlled retrieval;
* monitoring.

Where embeddings are derived from sensitive datasets, they should receive an appropriate security classification.

---

# 73. Differential Privacy

Differential privacy can reduce the contribution of individual records to model behavior.

Its objective is to make model outputs less dependent on whether a particular individual record was included in training.

Implementation may involve:

* noise mechanisms;
* privacy budgets;
* clipping;
* controlled training algorithms.

The privacy guarantees should be documented and measured rather than described only qualitatively.

# 75. Synthetic Data

* sensitive attribute preservation;
* generation methodology.

Poorly generated synthetic data can retain privacy risks from its source data.

# 90. Core Engineering Principle

> **Do not rely on model confidentiality, model instructions, or model behavior alone to prevent membership inference.**

A model trained on sensitive information should be treated as a potential information-inference surface.

Effective protection requires independent controls across:

* data minimization;
* privacy-preserving training;
* dataset security;
* model artifact protection;
* fine-grained access control;
* query restrictions;
* output minimization;
* tenant isolation;
* behavioral monitoring;
* membership-inference testing;
* DLP;
* incident response.

The security boundary must therefore exist **around the complete AI data-to-model-to-query-to-output lifecycle**, rather than only around the underlying training database.

* fine-grained authorization;
* query controls;
* output minimization;

* independent assurance.
