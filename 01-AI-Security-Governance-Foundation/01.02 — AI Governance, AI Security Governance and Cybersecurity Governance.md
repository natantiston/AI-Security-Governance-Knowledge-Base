# 01.02 — AI Governance, AI Security Governance and Cybersecurity Governance

## Part 1 — Foundations and Conceptual Distinctions

### 1. Introduction

AI Governance, AI Security Governance, and Cybersecurity Governance are closely related disciplines, but they are not interchangeable.

Organizations sometimes use these terms as though they describe the same activity. This creates confusion about accountability, risk ownership, security responsibilities, compliance requirements, and the scope of governance activities.

A mature organization should understand the relationship between the three:

**AI Governance → governs the overall use and management of AI**

**AI Security Governance → governs security risks associated with AI**

**Cybersecurity Governance → governs the organization's overall cybersecurity capability**

These disciplines overlap significantly, but each has a different primary purpose.

Understanding this distinction is particularly important for Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) professionals because AI increasingly intersects with enterprise risk management, cybersecurity, privacy, compliance, technology governance, and internal audit.

---

## 2. What Is AI Governance?

AI Governance is the broader organizational framework for directing, managing, and overseeing the use and development of Artificial Intelligence.

It establishes:

* Organizational principles
* Accountability
* Decision rights
* Policies
* Risk management
* Compliance requirements
* Oversight
* AI lifecycle requirements
* Responsible-use expectations
* Monitoring
* Assurance

A practical definition is:

> **AI Governance is the system of policies, roles, responsibilities, processes, controls, and oversight mechanisms used to direct and manage an organization's development, acquisition, deployment, use, monitoring, and retirement of AI systems.**

AI Governance therefore addresses questions such as:

* Why should the organization use AI?
* Where may AI be used?
* Who is accountable for AI?
* What AI use cases are acceptable?
* What risks are acceptable?
* What regulatory requirements apply?
* How should AI systems be classified?
* What human oversight is required?
* How should AI systems be monitored?
* When should an AI system be modified or retired?

AI Governance is therefore broader than security.

---

## 3. What Is AI Security Governance?

AI Security Governance is the security-focused component of AI Governance.

It establishes how security risks associated with AI systems are identified, assessed, controlled, monitored, and reported.

A practical definition is:

> **AI Security Governance is the system of governance structures, policies, processes, risk-management practices, controls, and oversight mechanisms used to ensure that AI systems and their supporting ecosystem are securely designed, developed, acquired, deployed, operated, changed, monitored, and retired.**

AI Security Governance focuses on issues such as:

* AI security risk
* Model security
* Data security
* AI application security
* AI access control
* Prompt security
* AI agent security
* AI supply-chain security
* Security testing
* AI monitoring
* AI incident response
* Security assurance

AI Security Governance therefore sits at the intersection of:

**AI Governance + Cybersecurity Governance + AI-specific security risks**

---

## 4. What Is Cybersecurity Governance?

Cybersecurity Governance is the broader governance discipline for managing cybersecurity across the enterprise.

It establishes:

* Cybersecurity strategy
* Cybersecurity policies
* Roles and responsibilities
* Risk management
* Security objectives
* Security controls
* Security operations oversight
* Compliance
* Metrics
* Assurance
* Management reporting

A practical definition is:

> **Cybersecurity Governance is the system through which an organization directs, oversees, and manages cybersecurity risks, capabilities, responsibilities, controls, and performance.**

Cybersecurity Governance covers the organization's broader technology and information environment.

This can include:

* Networks
* Servers
* Endpoints
* Applications
* Cloud
* Identity
* Databases
* Operational technology
* Mobile environments
* Infrastructure
* Information assets
* AI systems

AI therefore becomes one component of the broader cybersecurity environment.

## 6. A Simple Analogy

Consider an organization operating a hospital.

### AI Governance asks:

> "Should we use AI to support medical decision-making, and under what conditions?"

### AI Security Governance asks:

> "How do we protect the AI system, its data, model, interfaces, and users from security threats?"

### Cybersecurity Governance asks:

> "How do we govern the security of the hospital's entire digital environment?"

The three questions are different, but they must be coordinated.

## 7. Primary Objective of AI Governance

The primary objective of AI Governance is to ensure that AI is used in a manner consistent with:

* Organizational strategy
* Risk appetite
* Legal obligations
* Regulatory requirements
* Ethical principles
* Business objectives
* Stakeholder expectations

AI Governance is concerned with the **overall management and oversight of AI**.

Security is one component of that broader governance framework.

## 8. Primary Objective of AI Security Governance

The primary objective of AI Security Governance is to ensure that security risks arising from AI are appropriately governed.

This includes risks affecting:

* Confidentiality
* Integrity
* Availability
* Authenticity
* Accountability
* Privacy
* Resilience
* AI system integrity
* Model integrity
* Data integrity

AI Security Governance asks:

> **"How do we ensure that AI-related security risks are identified, owned, treated, controlled, monitored, and assured?"**

---

## 9. Primary Objective of Cybersecurity Governance

Cybersecurity Governance seeks to ensure that the organization's overall cybersecurity capability supports business objectives while managing cybersecurity risk.

It asks:

> **"How does the organization establish, direct, oversee, measure, and improve cybersecurity across the enterprise?"**

This includes governance over traditional and emerging technologies.

AI is therefore increasingly becoming part of enterprise cybersecurity governance.

## 11. AI Governance Is Broader Than AI Security Governance

A common mistake is to treat AI Security Governance as synonymous with AI Governance.

AI Governance may address:

* Fairness
* Transparency
* Explainability
* Accountability
* Human oversight
* Responsible use
* Business purpose
* Regulatory compliance
* Social impact
* AI strategy

AI Security Governance focuses specifically on security-related aspects.

For example:

An AI system may be secure from unauthorized access but still have governance problems involving:

* Inappropriate business use
* Lack of transparency
* Poor human oversight
* Inadequate documentation
* Unclear accountability
* Regulatory non-compliance

Therefore:

**Secure AI does not automatically mean well-governed AI.**

## 12. AI Security Governance Is Broader Than Technical AI Security

Another important distinction is between **AI Security Governance** and **AI Security Engineering**.

AI Security Engineering focuses on implementing technical protections.

Examples include:

* Authentication
* Encryption
* API security
* Network controls
* Input validation
* Output filtering
* Vulnerability management
* Secure coding
* Security testing

AI Security Governance determines:

* Which controls are required
* Why they are required
* Who owns them
* What level of protection is appropriate
* How effectiveness is measured
* How exceptions are handled
* How compliance is demonstrated

Therefore:

**Security Engineering implements.**

**Security Governance directs, oversees, and assures.**

## 22. Why the Distinction Matters for GRC Professionals

For a GRC professional, understanding these boundaries is critical.

A risk may initially appear to belong to one domain but actually require multiple governance functions.

For example:

**AI model exposes sensitive customer information.**

This may involve:

* AI Governance
* AI Security Governance
* Cybersecurity Governance
* Privacy Governance
* Data Governance
* Legal and Compliance
* Enterprise Risk Management

A mature GRC professional should therefore avoid treating governance domains as isolated silos.

Instead, the professional should identify:

1. The primary risk.
2. The affected governance domains.
3. The accountable owner.
4. Applicable requirements.
5. Required controls.
6. Evidence.
7. Assurance responsibilities.

---

## 23. Governance Does Not Mean Centralization

An organization does not necessarily need one department to own every AI governance activity.

Instead, governance can be distributed.

For example:

* Business owns business risk.
* AI Governance owns AI governance requirements.
* Cybersecurity owns security risk and controls.
* Privacy owns privacy requirements.
* Legal interprets legal requirements.
* Compliance coordinates regulatory obligations.
* Internal Audit provides independent assurance.

The governance model should define how these functions work together.

---

## 24. Governance Does Not Mean Bureaucracy

A poorly designed governance program can create excessive approval processes.

A mature program should instead use:

**Risk-Based Governance**

Low-risk AI should have proportionate requirements.

High-risk AI should receive stronger scrutiny.

Critical AI systems should receive the highest level of oversight.

This allows organizations to balance:

**Security + Compliance + Innovation + Business Agility**

---

## 25. Foundational Governance Principle

The most important principle is:

> **AI Governance, AI Security Governance, and Cybersecurity Governance should be treated as complementary capabilities rather than competing governance structures.**

AI Governance determines how AI should be governed overall.

Cybersecurity Governance determines how cybersecurity should be governed across the enterprise.

AI Security Governance ensures that the security implications of AI are appropriately addressed at the intersection of the two.

This conceptual foundation is essential before studying more specialized AI governance concepts such as **Responsible AI, Trustworthy AI, Secure AI, Ethical AI, AI Risk Management, AI Compliance, and AI Assurance**.

### 30. Governance Versus Management

An important GRC distinction is the difference between **governance** and **management**.

### Governance

Governance determines:

* Direction
* Objectives
* Accountability
* Oversight
* Risk appetite
* Decision rights
* Performance expectations

### Management

Management performs:

* Planning
* Implementation
* Operation
* Monitoring
* Corrective action

For example:

**Governance:**
Management establishes that high-risk AI systems require formal security assessment.

**Management:**
The security team performs the assessment.

This distinction is important because governance should not become confused with day-to-day operational execution.

### 31. Governance Versus Technical Security

Examples:

* MFA

* Which controls are required

Therefore:

**Technical Security = implementation**

**Security Governance = direction, oversight, accountability, and assurance**

### 35. AI Security Risk Versus General AI Risk

Not every AI risk is a security risk.

For example:

**AI-generated inaccurate financial forecast**

may primarily represent a:

* Business risk
* Model risk
* Decision-making risk

Whereas:

**Attacker manipulates an AI application to retrieve unauthorized customer data**

is clearly a security risk.

Some risks overlap.

For example:

**AI model produces harmful incorrect decisions because an attacker manipulated its input data.**

This can involve:

* Security risk
* Model risk
* Operational risk
* Business risk
* Compliance risk

GRC professionals must therefore understand risk relationships rather than forcing every risk into a single category.

### 42. The Business Owner Remains Important

Governance should not transfer business accountability to cybersecurity simply because an AI system creates security risks.

The business owner remains accountable for the business purpose and business consequences.

For example, if an AI system is used to automate customer service, the business owner should remain accountable for:

* Business objectives
* Business process impact
* Customer impact
* Business risk
* Service performance

Cybersecurity should provide security expertise and control requirements.

This separation prevents security teams from becoming the default owners of business risk.

### 77. Common Operating Model Failures

Several governance failures occur repeatedly.

#### Failure 1 — Security Owns Everything

Cybersecurity becomes responsible for business, privacy, compliance, and AI decisions.

#### Failure 2 — AI Governance Ignores Security

AI governance focuses on ethics and strategy while overlooking technical security.

#### Failure 3 — Cybersecurity Ignores AI-Specific Risk

Traditional security controls are applied without considering AI-specific threats.

#### Failure 4 — No Business Ownership

The security department becomes the de facto owner of business AI risk.

#### Failure 5 — Duplicate Controls

Each governance function creates separate controls for the same requirement.

#### Failure 6 — Fragmented Reporting

Management receives separate and contradictory AI, security, risk, and compliance reports.

#### Failure 7 — No Lifecycle Integration

Governance occurs during procurement but disappears after deployment.

### 82. Final Perspective

The distinction between AI Governance, AI Security Governance, and Cybersecurity Governance can be reduced to three questions:

> **AI Governance:** Are we governing AI appropriately?

> **AI Security Governance:** Are we governing the security risks created by AI appropriately?

> **Cybersecurity Governance:** Are we governing cybersecurity across the enterprise appropriately?

A mature organization does not answer these questions independently.

Instead, it creates an integrated governance system in which:

**AI Governance establishes the overall AI direction.**

**Cybersecurity Governance establishes the enterprise security direction.**

**AI Security Governance ensures that AI-specific security risks are properly governed at the intersection of the two.**

For a GRC professional, this integrated perspective is essential because AI security is not merely a technical problem.

It is a **governance, risk, control, compliance, assurance, and accountability problem**.

That understanding provides the foundation for the next topic: **Responsible AI, Trustworthy AI, Secure AI, and Ethical AI**, where the focus shifts from governance structures and security responsibilities to the broader principles that determine what constitutes well-governed and trustworthy AI.

### 100. Common Governance Mistakes

Several mistakes should be avoided.

#### Mistake 1 — Treating AI Governance as Cybersecurity

AI Governance includes much more than security.

#### Mistake 2 — Treating AI Security as Traditional Cybersecurity

AI introduces security considerations that may require additional controls and analysis.

#### Mistake 3 — Creating a Separate AI Security Island

AI security should integrate with enterprise cybersecurity.

#### Mistake 4 — Making Cybersecurity the AI Risk Owner

Security may advise and control security risk, but business owners remain accountable for business decisions.

#### Mistake 5 — Creating Duplicate Controls

Common controls should be reused where possible.

#### Mistake 6 — Governance Only During Procurement

AI risk changes throughout the lifecycle.

#### Mistake 7 — Excessive Approval Requirements

Governance should be proportional to risk.

#### Mistake 8 — No Evidence

Policies and claims must be supported by demonstrable evidence.

### 111. Final Perspective

The simplest mental model is:

**AI Governance**

> Governs **AI as an organizational capability**.

**AI Security Governance**

> Governs **security risks created by AI**.

**Cybersecurity Governance**

> Governs **cybersecurity across the enterprise**.

Their relationship can therefore be represented as:

**AI Governance**

↘

**AI Security Governance**

↗

**Cybersecurity Governance**

with both ultimately connected to:

**Enterprise Governance + Enterprise Risk Management + GRC**

For a cybersecurity GRC professional, understanding this relationship is critical.

AI should not be treated as a completely separate governance universe. Instead, AI should be integrated into the organization's existing governance, risk, security, compliance, control, and assurance architecture while introducing specialized capabilities where AI creates unique risks.

The mature end state is therefore:

**Govern AI → Secure AI → Manage AI Risk → Demonstrate Compliance → Assure Effectiveness → Continuously Improve**

This integrated perspective provides the foundation for understanding the next major distinction in AI governance: **Responsible AI, Trustworthy AI, Secure AI, and Ethical AI**.
