# 01.08 — The AI System Lifecycle

## Part 1 — Foundations of the AI System Lifecycle

### 1. Introduction

An AI system does not exist only when a model is running in production. It passes through a series of stages beginning with an idea or business need and continuing through design, development, deployment, operation, monitoring, modification, and eventual retirement.

The **AI system lifecycle** provides a structured way to understand these stages and the governance, security, risk, privacy, safety, and operational requirements that apply throughout them.

A lifecycle perspective is essential because AI risks can emerge at any stage.

A security weakness introduced during development may become a production vulnerability.

Poor-quality training data may create unreliable model behavior.

An inappropriate deployment decision may expose an AI system to unnecessary attack surfaces.

A model that was initially safe may become inappropriate after a significant change in its data, model, tools, or operating environment.

Therefore:

> **AI Security Governance must govern the AI system across its entire lifecycle rather than focusing only on the production environment.**

---

### 2. What Is an AI System Lifecycle?

The **AI system lifecycle** is the sequence of stages through which an AI system progresses from initial conception to retirement.

A simplified lifecycle can be represented as:

**Ideation**

→ **Planning**

→ **Design**

→ **Data Preparation**

→ **Development**

→ **Testing and Validation**

→ **Approval**

→ **Deployment**

→ **Operation**

→ **Monitoring**

→ **Change and Improvement**

→ **Retirement**

The exact number and names of stages may differ between organizations and AI frameworks.

---

### 3. Why Lifecycle Thinking Matters

Without lifecycle thinking, organizations may treat AI security as a one-time activity.

For example:

**Security Assessment**

→ completed before deployment

→ considered finished

This approach is insufficient because AI systems change continuously.

Models may be:

* Retrained
* Fine-tuned
* Replaced
* Connected to new data
* Connected to new tools
* Exposed to new users
* Integrated with new systems
* Given additional permissions

Each change can create new risks.

---

### 4. AI Lifecycle Versus Software Development Lifecycle

The AI system lifecycle overlaps with the traditional **Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC)** but is not identical.

Traditional software development may focus primarily on:

* Requirements
* Design
* Development
* Testing
* Deployment
* Maintenance
* Retirement

AI systems introduce additional considerations involving:

* Data
* Models
* Training
* Evaluation
* Model behavior
* Model drift
* AI-specific testing
* Human oversight
* AI safety
* AI security

Therefore, AI governance should integrate with existing SDLC practices while addressing AI-specific characteristics.

---

### 5. AI Lifecycle Versus Machine Learning Lifecycle

The **machine learning lifecycle** is typically more focused on the development and operation of models.

It may include:

* Data collection
* Data preparation
* Training
* Evaluation
* Deployment
* Monitoring
* Retraining

The broader AI system lifecycle includes the entire system surrounding the model.

For example:

**AI Application**

may include:

* Model
* Data
* APIs
* User interface
* Identity
* Infrastructure
* Tools
* Agents
* Monitoring
* Human processes

Therefore, the AI system lifecycle is broader than the model lifecycle.

---

### 6. The AI System Is More Than the Model

One of the most important concepts in AI Security Governance is that:

> **The model is only one component of an AI system.**

An AI system may consist of:

**Users**

↓

**Application**

↓

**Prompt / Instructions**

↓

**AI Model**

↓

**Data / Retrieval**

↓

**Tools / APIs**

↓

**Enterprise Systems**

↓

**Infrastructure**

Each component introduces different governance and security considerations.

---

### 7. AI Lifecycle Scope

The lifecycle should cover the complete AI capability, including:

* Business purpose
* Users
* Data
* Models
* Applications
* Infrastructure
* Interfaces
* APIs
* Tools
* Agents
* Security controls
* Human oversight
* Operational processes

This prevents organizations from governing only the model while ignoring the surrounding system.

---

### 8. AI Lifecycle Begins Before Development

AI governance should begin before technical development starts.

An organization may first identify a business problem such as:

* Customer service improvement
* Fraud detection
* Process automation
* Document analysis
* Predictive analytics
* Employee productivity

The organization should determine whether AI is actually appropriate for the problem.

---

### 9. AI Ideation Stage

The **ideation stage** is where a potential AI use case is identified.

Questions may include:

* What problem are we trying to solve?
* Why is AI being considered?
* What outcome is expected?
* Who will use the system?
* Who may be affected?
* What data is required?
* What risks could result?

The objective is to establish a clear business rationale before resources are committed.

---

### 10. AI Business Case

An AI initiative should have an appropriate business case.

The business case may consider:

* Expected benefits
* Costs
* Operational impact
* Security implications
* Privacy implications
* Regulatory requirements
* Safety concerns
* Alternative solutions

AI should not be adopted solely because it is technologically available.

---

### 11. AI Use-Case Definition

The organization should clearly define the intended use case.

A use-case definition may identify:

* Business objective
* Intended users
* Intended environment
* Expected inputs
* Expected outputs
* Decision context
* Required level of autonomy
* Prohibited uses

A well-defined use case creates a foundation for later risk assessment.

---

### 12. AI Use-Case Boundaries

AI systems should have clearly defined boundaries.

These may include:

* Permitted users
* Permitted data
* Permitted actions
* Permitted systems
* Geographic boundaries
* Regulatory boundaries
* Business boundaries

For example, an AI assistant approved for summarizing internal documents should not automatically be permitted to execute financial transactions.

---

### 13. AI Risk Classification

Once a use case has been defined, the organization should determine its risk level.

Risk classification may consider:

* Potential harm
* Business criticality
* Data sensitivity
* Degree of autonomy
* Number of affected individuals
* Regulatory significance
* Security exposure

The risk classification influences the level of governance required.

---

### 14. AI Impact Assessment

An AI impact assessment may evaluate potential consequences of the proposed system.

Possible impact categories include:

* Security
* Privacy
* Safety
* Financial
* Operational
* Legal
* Regulatory
* Reputational
* Human impact

The assessment should occur early enough to influence design decisions.

---

### 15. AI Feasibility Assessment

Technical feasibility should be evaluated alongside governance requirements.

Questions may include:

* Is sufficient data available?
* Is the model capable of performing the task?
* Can the system be secured?
* Can outputs be adequately validated?
* Can the system be monitored?
* Can human oversight be implemented?
* Can regulatory obligations be satisfied?

A technically feasible system may still be unsuitable for deployment.

---

### 16. AI Architecture and Design Stage

After the use case is approved for further development, the organization designs the AI system.

Design considerations may include:

* Model architecture
* Application architecture
* Data architecture
* Identity architecture
* Network architecture
* API architecture
* Tool architecture
* Security architecture
* Human oversight

Security and governance requirements should be incorporated during design rather than added after implementation.

---

### 17. Secure-by-Design for AI

**Secure-by-design** means security requirements are incorporated into the AI system from the beginning.

Examples include:

* Least privilege
* Strong authentication
* Authorization
* Data protection
* Secure APIs
* Logging
* Monitoring
* Segmentation
* Secrets management
* Secure model handling

Retrofitting these controls after deployment can be more expensive and less effective.

---

### 18. Privacy-by-Design for AI

Where personal information is involved, privacy should also be incorporated during design.

Considerations may include:

* Data minimization
* Purpose limitation
* Access controls
* Retention
* Anonymization or pseudonymization where appropriate
* Privacy-preserving techniques
* User rights

Privacy should not be treated as a final compliance checkpoint.

---

### 19. Safety-by-Design for AI

For systems where incorrect or unsafe behavior can cause meaningful harm, safety requirements should be incorporated into architecture and design.

This may include:

* Fail-safe behavior
* Human intervention
* Operational limits
* Guardrails
* Testing
* Monitoring
* Emergency shutdown

Safety requirements should correspond to the potential consequences of system failure.

---

### 20. Governance-by-Design

AI governance should also be considered during architecture.

This means designing the system so that governance requirements can actually be implemented.

Examples include:

* Identifiable ownership
* Decision logging
* Audit trails
* Approval workflows
* Access reviews
* Change control
* Monitoring
* Evidence retention

Governance should be technically and operationally enforceable where possible.

---

### 21. AI Data Lifecycle

Data has its own lifecycle within the broader AI system lifecycle.

It may include:

**Collection**

→ **Classification**

→ **Preparation**

→ **Storage**

→ **Processing**

→ **Training / Retrieval**

→ **Use**

→ **Retention**

→ **Deletion**

Each stage introduces potential security, privacy, quality, and governance considerations.

---

### 22. Data Collection

AI systems may obtain data from:

* Internal databases
* Applications
* Sensors
* Documents
* APIs
* Public sources
* Customers
* Employees
* Third parties

The organization should understand where the data originates and whether its use is authorized.

---

### 23. Data Classification

Data used by AI should be appropriately classified.

Examples may include:

* Public
* Internal
* Confidential
* Restricted
* Highly sensitive

Classification helps determine appropriate controls for:

* Storage
* Access
* Processing
* Transmission
* AI use

---

### 24. Data Quality

AI performance depends significantly on data quality.

Poor-quality data may result in:

* Incorrect predictions
* Inconsistent outputs
* Unreliable recommendations
* Unexpected behavior

Data-quality governance is therefore part of AI governance.

---

### 25. Data Provenance

**Data provenance** refers to understanding where data originated, how it was obtained, and how it has been transformed.

Provenance can support:

* Data quality
* Compliance
* Reproducibility
* Investigations
* Security
* Accountability

Organizations should establish appropriate provenance requirements for material AI systems.

---

### 26. Model Development Stage

During model development, teams may:

* Select models
* Prepare datasets
* Train models
* Fine-tune models
* Configure parameters
* Develop prompts
* Develop retrieval mechanisms
* Integrate tools

Development environments should be governed according to their risk.

---

### 27. Development Environment Security

AI development environments can contain valuable:

* Training data
* Source code
* Model weights
* Credentials
* Research
* Intellectual property

They should therefore receive appropriate security protections.

---

### 28. Model Selection

Organizations may choose between:

* Internally developed models
* Open-source models
* Commercial foundation models
* Specialized models
* Third-party AI services

Model selection should consider more than technical performance.

Relevant factors may include:

* Security
* Privacy
* Reliability
* Transparency
* Vendor dependency
* Cost
* Regulatory requirements
* Operational resilience

---

### 29. Model Training

Training involves exposing a model to data so that it can learn patterns or relationships.

Governance should consider:

* Training-data authorization
* Data quality
* Data security
* Data provenance
* Model performance
* Reproducibility
* Training infrastructure

Training activities may also create security risks if sensitive information is improperly incorporated.

---

### 30. Model Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning adapts an existing model using additional data or training.

Governance should establish:

* Approved data sources
* Security controls
* Version control
* Testing
* Validation
* Approval requirements

Fine-tuning can change model behavior and therefore should be treated as a controlled lifecycle activity.

---

### 31. Prompt and Configuration Development

Modern AI systems may rely heavily on:

* System instructions
* Prompts
* Policies
* Retrieval configurations
* Tool definitions

These elements can materially affect system behavior and should be managed through appropriate development and change-control processes.

---

### 32. AI Application Development

The AI application surrounding the model may contain:

* User interfaces
* APIs
* Authentication
* Authorization
* Business logic
* Data connections
* Tool integrations

Application security therefore remains an essential part of AI security.

---

### 33. AI Integration Stage

AI systems may be integrated with:

* Databases
* Enterprise applications
* SaaS platforms
* Cloud services
* APIs
* Identity systems
* Business workflows

Every integration creates a potential trust boundary and should be appropriately assessed.

---

### 34. AI Tool Integration

When AI systems can invoke tools, governance must establish:

* Which tools are available
* Which users can invoke them
* Which agents can invoke them
* What permissions exist
* What actions are allowed
* What actions require approval

Tool access can significantly expand the effective attack surface of an AI system.

---

### 35. AI Agent Development

AI agents can introduce additional lifecycle considerations because they may:

* Plan actions
* Call tools
* Retrieve information
* Execute workflows
* Interact with external systems

Agent capabilities should therefore be evaluated according to their autonomy and potential impact.

---

### 36. Testing Stage

Before deployment, AI systems should undergo appropriate testing.

Testing may cover:

* Functional behavior
* Security
* Privacy
* Safety
* Performance
* Reliability
* Model behavior
* Adversarial behavior

Testing requirements should be proportionate to the system's risk.

---

### 37. AI Security Testing

Security testing may include:

* Vulnerability assessment
* Penetration testing
* Threat modeling
* Prompt-injection testing
* Access-control testing
* Data-leakage testing
* API security testing
* Agent security testing

AI-specific testing should complement, not replace, conventional cybersecurity testing.

---

### 38. AI Red-Team Testing

Red-team testing evaluates how an AI system behaves under adversarial conditions.

Testers may attempt to:

* Bypass safeguards
* Extract sensitive information
* Manipulate model behavior
* Abuse tools
* Circumvent authorization
* Trigger unintended actions

Results should feed into remediation before production deployment where appropriate.

---

### 39. AI Validation

Validation determines whether the AI system is fit for its intended purpose.

Validation may evaluate:

* Accuracy
* Reliability
* Robustness
* Limitations
* Security
* Safety
* Performance

Validation should consider the actual operational context rather than laboratory performance alone.

---

### 40. Pre-Production Governance Gate

Before production deployment, an AI system may pass through a governance gate.

The gate may confirm:

* Business approval
* Risk assessment
* Security assessment
* Privacy assessment
* Testing
* Validation
* Documentation
* Monitoring
* Ownership
* Residual-risk acceptance

The approval process should be proportionate to the AI system's risk and impact.


## Part 2 — AI Deployment, Operation, Monitoring and Continuous Management

### 41. Production Deployment

**Production deployment** is the point at which an AI system becomes available for its intended operational use.

Deployment should occur only after the organization has established that the system is appropriately:

* Approved
* Tested
* Secured
* Documented
* Monitored
* Owned
* Governed

Production deployment converts an AI capability from a development asset into an operational business capability.

---

### 42. Deployment Authorization

Deployment should require authorization from the appropriate authority.

The authorization process should confirm that:

* Required governance activities are complete
* Identified risks have been treated appropriately
* Residual risks have authorized owners
* Required controls are operational
* Monitoring is available
* Incident procedures exist
* Users are appropriately authorized

The required level of authorization should depend on the system's risk classification.

---

### 43. Production Environment

The production environment should be appropriately separated from development and testing environments.

This separation helps protect:

* Production data
* Model assets
* Credentials
* Business processes
* Customer information
* Critical integrations

Changes should not be introduced directly into production without appropriate controls.

---

### 44. Production Access

Access to production AI systems should follow the principle of **least privilege**.

Access should be based on:

* Business need
* Role
* Authorization
* Risk
* Operational requirements

Privileged access should receive additional controls and monitoring.

---

### 45. AI Identity Management

AI systems, applications, services, and agents should have identifiable identities where technically appropriate.

Identity management should support:

* Authentication
* Authorization
* Attribution
* Access reviews
* Monitoring
* Revocation

An AI agent should not operate using an unmanaged or shared identity when individual attribution is required.

---

### 46. AI Service Accounts

AI applications may require service accounts to interact with:

* Databases
* APIs
* Cloud services
* Enterprise applications

Service accounts should have only the permissions required for their approved functions.

Excessive privileges can allow an AI compromise to become a broader enterprise compromise.

---

### 47. AI Secrets Management

AI systems may require:

* API keys
* Tokens
* Certificates
* Credentials
* Encryption keys

These secrets should be managed using appropriate enterprise secrets-management capabilities rather than being embedded directly into prompts, source code, configuration files, or model instructions.

---

### 48. AI Configuration Management

Production AI configurations should be controlled.

Configuration may include:

* Model selection
* System prompts
* Guardrails
* Retrieval settings
* Tool permissions
* API connections
* Access policies
* Safety controls

Configuration changes can materially affect AI behavior and should therefore be traceable.

---

### 49. AI Model Version Management

AI systems should maintain appropriate model-version information.

The organization should be able to determine:

* Which model is currently deployed
* Which version is deployed
* When it was deployed
* Who approved it
* What changed
* What testing was performed

Version management supports traceability and incident investigation.

---

### 50. Model Deployment

Model deployment may involve:

* Model packaging
* Infrastructure provisioning
* API configuration
* Access configuration
* Security controls
* Monitoring
* Performance testing

The deployment process should maintain consistency between the validated model and the production model.

---

### 51. AI Model Registry

Organizations with multiple models may maintain a **model registry**.

A registry can track:

* Model name
* Version
* Owner
* Purpose
* Risk classification
* Status
* Approval
* Validation
* Deployment location
* Retirement status

A model registry can become an important component of AI governance.

---

### 52. AI System Inventory

Organizations should maintain an inventory of material AI systems.

The inventory may contain:

* System name
* Business owner
* System owner
* Model
* Data sources
* Vendor
* Risk classification
* Users
* Deployment environment
* Status

An AI inventory provides visibility into the organization's AI estate.

---

### 53. AI Asset Management

AI governance should extend beyond models to other important assets.

These may include:

* Datasets
* Model weights
* Prompts
* System instructions
* APIs
* Tools
* Agents
* Applications
* Infrastructure

Each important asset should have appropriate ownership and protection.

---

### 54. AI Operational Management

Once deployed, AI systems enter the operational phase.

Operational management may include:

* Availability
* Performance
* Capacity
* Access
* Security
* Incident response
* Change management
* Backup
* Recovery

AI operations should be integrated into existing IT and cybersecurity operating processes where appropriate.

---

### 55. AI Service Management

AI services may require service-management practices covering:

* Service availability
* Service levels
* Incidents
* Problems
* Changes
* Requests
* Capacity

Organizations should define the operational expectations for critical AI services.

---

### 56. AI Service-Level Management

For business-critical AI systems, service levels may address:

* Availability
* Response time
* Throughput
* Recovery time
* Recovery point
* Support response

Service levels should reflect business requirements rather than technology capabilities alone.

---

### 57. AI Availability

AI availability can depend on multiple components:

**Application**

→ **Model**

→ **API**

→ **Cloud**

→ **Data**

→ **Network**

→ **External Services**

A failure in any component may affect the AI capability.

---

### 58. AI Reliability

Reliability involves the ability of an AI system to perform its intended function consistently.

Reliability considerations may include:

* Model performance
* Infrastructure stability
* Dependency availability
* Data availability
* Error handling
* Recovery mechanisms

Reliability should be evaluated in the context of the business process supported by AI.

---

### 59. AI Performance Monitoring

Organizations may monitor:

* Response time
* Throughput
* Error rates
* Resource consumption
* Model performance
* User activity

Performance degradation may indicate technical problems or changes in the operating environment.

---

### 60. AI Model Performance Monitoring

Model monitoring may examine:

* Accuracy
* Precision
* Recall
* False positives
* False negatives
* Confidence
* Output quality

The appropriate metrics depend on the AI use case.

---

### 61. Model Drift

**Model drift** occurs when the relationship between the model and the environment changes over time.

Examples include:

* Changes in user behavior
* Changes in market conditions
* Changes in data distributions
* Changes in business processes

A model that performed well initially may therefore become less effective.

---

### 62. Data Drift

**Data drift** occurs when the statistical characteristics of input data change over time.

Examples may include changes in:

* Data distributions
* User populations
* Transaction patterns
* Geographic composition
* Data sources

Data drift can affect AI performance and should be monitored where relevant.

---

### 63. Concept Drift

**Concept drift** occurs when the underlying relationship between inputs and outcomes changes.

For example, patterns that previously indicated fraudulent activity may become less reliable after attackers change their behavior.

Concept drift can require model reassessment or retraining.

---

### 64. AI Behavioral Monitoring

AI systems should be monitored not only for technical performance but also for unexpected behavior.

Monitoring may identify:

* Unexpected outputs
* Policy violations
* Unsafe responses
* Unusual tool usage
* Excessive data retrieval
* Abnormal user activity

Behavioral monitoring becomes particularly important for generative AI and autonomous systems.

---

### 65. AI Security Monitoring

Security monitoring may cover:

* Authentication
* Authorization
* API activity
* Prompt activity
* Data access
* Tool invocation
* Administrative changes
* Model interactions

Security monitoring should be integrated into the organization's broader security monitoring capability.

---

### 66. AI Logging

AI systems should generate appropriate logs to support:

* Security
* Operations
* Investigations
* Compliance
* Accountability

Logging requirements should reflect the sensitivity and criticality of the AI system.

---

### 67. AI Audit Trails

An audit trail should allow authorized personnel to reconstruct significant events.

Depending on the system, this may include:

* User identity
* Timestamp
* Request
* Model version
* Tool invocation
* Administrative action
* Approval
* Output
* Result

Auditability becomes increasingly important as AI systems gain autonomy.

---

### 68. AI Output Monitoring

AI outputs may need to be monitored for:

* Accuracy
* Security violations
* Sensitive information disclosure
* Unsafe content
* Policy violations
* Unexpected behavior

The degree of monitoring should correspond to the consequences of incorrect or harmful outputs.

---

### 69. Human Review of AI Outputs

Human review may be required where AI outputs influence important decisions.

Reviewers should have:

* Appropriate expertise
* Sufficient context
* Authority to reject the output
* Ability to request additional information
* Ability to escalate concerns

Human review should be meaningful rather than purely procedural.

---

### 70. AI Human-in-the-Loop Operations

A **human-in-the-loop** operating model requires human approval before certain AI actions occur.

Examples include:

* Financial transactions
* Employment decisions
* Security actions
* High-value purchases
* High-impact customer decisions

The organization should define exactly which actions require human intervention.

---

### 71. AI Human-on-the-Loop Operations

A **human-on-the-loop** model allows AI to operate autonomously while humans supervise overall behavior.

This model may be appropriate where:

* The AI operates continuously
* Individual actions do not all require approval
* Monitoring is effective
* Intervention mechanisms exist

The organization should define thresholds that require human intervention.

---

### 72. AI Autonomous Operations

AI systems may eventually perform actions with limited or no direct human intervention.

Autonomous operations require governance over:

* Permissions
* Action limits
* Monitoring
* Escalation
* Human intervention
* Shutdown

The level of autonomy should be appropriate to the potential impact.

---

### 73. AI Agent Permissions

AI agents should operate with narrowly defined permissions.

Permissions should specify:

* Which systems they can access
* Which data they can retrieve
* Which tools they can use
* Which actions they can perform
* Which actions require approval

Agent permissions should not exceed the requirements of the approved use case.

---

### 74. AI Transaction Limits

Where AI can initiate financial or operational transactions, organizations may establish limits.

Limits may apply to:

* Transaction value
* Transaction frequency
* Number of actions
* Approved destinations
* Operating periods

Transaction limits reduce the potential impact of erroneous or malicious AI behavior.

---

### 75. AI Guardrails

Guardrails are mechanisms designed to constrain AI behavior.

They may include:

* Input restrictions
* Output filters
* Tool restrictions
* Policy enforcement
* Data-access restrictions
* Transaction limits
* Human approval

Guardrails should be tested rather than assumed to be effective.

---

### 76. AI Safety Monitoring

Safety monitoring may identify:

* Unsafe outputs
* Harmful recommendations
* Unexpected autonomous actions
* Repeated failures
* Unsafe edge cases

Safety monitoring is especially relevant where AI can affect people, physical systems, or critical business processes.

---

### 77. AI Privacy Monitoring

Privacy monitoring may identify:

* Unauthorized personal-data access
* Excessive data collection
* Sensitive-data exposure
* Improper retention
* Unauthorized sharing

Privacy monitoring should complement privacy-by-design controls.

---

### 78. AI Compliance Monitoring

Compliance monitoring can evaluate whether AI systems continue to meet:

* Internal policies
* Regulatory obligations
* Contractual requirements
* Approved use restrictions

Compliance should not be treated as complete simply because an initial approval was granted.

---

### 79. Continuous Control Monitoring

Where feasible, AI controls can be monitored continuously.

Examples include:

* Privileged access
* Tool permissions
* Model versions
* Configuration changes
* Data access
* Security events

Continuous monitoring can identify control failures earlier than periodic reviews.

---

### 80. AI Incident Detection

AI incidents may be detected through:

* Security monitoring
* User reports
* Automated alerts
* Model monitoring
* Privacy monitoring
* Vendor notifications
* Audit activities

Detection mechanisms should reflect the AI system's risk profile.

---

### 81. AI Incident Response

AI incident response may involve:

1. Detection
2. Triage
3. Containment
4. Investigation
5. Eradication
6. Recovery
7. Notification
8. Lessons learned

AI-specific scenarios should be incorporated into the broader incident-response framework.

---

### 82. AI Containment

Containment measures may include:

* Disabling an agent
* Revoking credentials
* Restricting tools
* Blocking users
* Isolating infrastructure
* Disabling an API
* Switching to a safer model

The appropriate response depends on the incident.

---

### 83. AI Recovery

Recovery should restore the AI service to an acceptable operational state.

Activities may include:

* Restoring configurations
* Reverting model versions
* Removing compromised components
* Revalidating controls
* Reauthorizing access
* Monitoring the restored environment

Recovery should not simply return a compromised system to production without validation.

---

### 84. AI Rollback

A rollback mechanism may allow an organization to return to a previously validated:

* Model
* Configuration
* Prompt
* Application version
* Infrastructure state

Rollback capability can reduce the impact of problematic changes.

---

### 85. AI Change Management

AI systems should be subject to appropriate change management.

Changes may include:

* New model
* New training data
* New prompt
* New tool
* New integration
* New user group
* New permission
* New business purpose

The required approval should depend on the significance of the change.

---

### 86. AI Change Impact Assessment

Before significant changes are implemented, organizations should assess potential impacts on:

* Security
* Privacy
* Safety
* Performance
* Compliance
* Business processes

A change that appears technically minor may have significant governance implications.

---

### 87. AI Model Retraining

Retraining may change model behavior.

Before retraining, organizations should establish:

* Approved training data
* Testing requirements
* Validation requirements
* Version management
* Approval requirements

A retrained model should not automatically be assumed to have the same risk profile as the previous version.

---

### 88. AI Model Replacement

Replacing one model with another can affect:

* Accuracy
* Security
* Privacy
* Cost
* Performance
* Behavior
* Regulatory compliance

Model replacement should therefore be governed as a potentially significant lifecycle change.

---

### 89. AI Vendor Change

External providers may change:

* Models
* APIs
* Infrastructure
* Data practices
* Subprocessors
* Security controls

Organizations should establish mechanisms for identifying and assessing material provider changes.

---

### 90. AI Continuous Improvement

AI systems may be continuously improved through:

* New data
* New models
* Updated prompts
* New tools
* User feedback
* Performance optimization

Continuous improvement should remain within the organization's governance framework.

---

### 91. AI Feedback Loops

AI systems may receive feedback from users or downstream processes.

Feedback may influence:

* Model behavior
* Future training
* Prompts
* Recommendations
* System configuration

Feedback mechanisms should be controlled to prevent malicious or poor-quality feedback from degrading the system.

---

### 92. AI User Feedback

Users may report:

* Incorrect outputs
* Unsafe behavior
* Security issues
* Privacy issues
* Poor performance

Organizations should establish channels for collecting and evaluating this feedback.

---

### 93. AI Problem Management

Repeated AI incidents should be analyzed for underlying causes.

Problem management may identify:

* Audits
* User feedback
* Model failures
* Vendor failures
* Governance exceptions

Lessons should be used to improve future AI systems.

### 185. AI Lifecycle Knowledge Management

Organizations should retain institutional knowledge about AI systems.

Knowledge may include:

* Architecture
* Design decisions
* Known limitations
* Risk decisions
* Security controls
* Operational procedures
* Incident history

Knowledge management becomes especially important when personnel or vendors change.

---

### 186. AI Lifecycle Transition Management

AI systems may transition between:

* Development teams
* Operations teams
* Vendors
* Business units
* Cloud environments

Transitions should preserve:

* Ownership
* Documentation
* Security controls
* Access management
* Monitoring
* Risk information

A change in ownership should not create a governance gap.

---

### 187. AI Lifecycle Handover

Formal handover should occur when responsibility moves between teams.

A handover may include:

* System documentation
* Model information
* Risk assessment
* Security assessment
* Known issues
* Credentials and access processes
* Monitoring procedures
* Incident procedures

Handover should be completed before the receiving team assumes operational responsibility.

---

### 188. AI Lifecycle Retirement Criteria

Retirement criteria should be established before an AI system becomes obsolete.

Criteria may include:

* Business need has ended
* Technology is obsolete
* Risk exceeds acceptable levels
* Vendor support has ended
* Regulatory requirements cannot be satisfied
* Replacement system is available
* Security weaknesses cannot be adequately mitigated

Retirement decisions should be deliberate rather than accidental.

---

### 189. AI Data at Retirement

Retirement should address all data associated with the AI system.

This may include:

* Training data
* Fine-tuning data
* User inputs
* Outputs
* Logs
* Cached information
* Embeddings
* Vector databases

The organization should determine what must be retained and what must be securely deleted.

---

### 190. AI Model Retirement

Retiring a model may require:

* Removing production endpoints
* Revoking model access
* Removing model artifacts
* Updating documentation
* Updating inventories
* Preserving required evidence

Retired models should not remain unnecessarily accessible.

---

### 191. AI Credential Retirement

Credentials associated with retired AI systems should be:

* Revoked
* Rotated where necessary
* Removed from active systems
* Reviewed for reuse elsewhere

Credential cleanup is an important part of secure decommissioning.

---

### 192. AI Integration Retirement

AI systems may have integrations with:

* APIs
* Databases
* Applications
* Identity systems
* Cloud services
* External providers

These integrations should be formally disconnected when the system is retired.

---

### 193. AI Inventory Closure

The AI inventory should be updated when a system is retired.

The record should indicate:

* Retirement date
* Retirement authority
* Final status
* Data disposition
* Model disposition
* Dependency removal
* Relevant retained evidence

This prevents retired systems from appearing operational or active.

---

### 194. AI Retirement Assurance

For material AI systems, retirement should be independently or systematically verified.

Verification may confirm that:

* Access was removed
* Credentials were revoked
* Integrations were disconnected
* Data was handled appropriately
* Systems were decommissioned
* Inventory records were updated

Retirement should therefore have a formal closure mechanism.

→ **How it was tested**

→ **Who approved it**

→ **How it operated**

→ **What changed**

→ **What incidents occurred**

→ **Why it was retired**

Auditability strengthens accountability across the entire lifecycle.

### 199. AI Lifecycle Governance as a Continuous Cycle

The lifecycle should be understood as continuous rather than strictly linear.

A production AI system may repeatedly move through:

**Operation**

→ **Monitoring**

→ **Change**

→ **Testing**

→ **Validation**

→ **Reapproval**

→ **Redeployment**

This cycle may occur multiple times before final retirement.

Each material change creates an opportunity to reassess whether the system remains secure, appropriate, compliant, and aligned with its approved purpose.

### 200. Enterprise AI Lifecycle Control Model

A mature AI lifecycle integrates five dimensions throughout the system's existence:

**Business**

→ Purpose, value, ownership, and outcomes

**Risk**

→ Identification, assessment, treatment, and acceptance

**Security**

→ Protection, resilience, monitoring, and response

**Governance**

→ Policies, decision rights, oversight, and accountability

**Assurance**

→ Testing, validation, audit, evidence, and continuous improvement

The AI system lifecycle therefore provides the operational structure through which an organization can continuously manage an AI capability from **initial concept through design, development, deployment, operation, change, reassessment, and retirement**, while maintaining appropriate business accountability, risk management, security, governance, and assurance throughout its existence.
