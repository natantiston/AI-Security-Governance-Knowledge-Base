# 01.14 — AI Security Governance Principles

## Part 1 — Foundational Principles for AI Security Governance

### 1. Introduction to AI Security Governance Principles

AI Security Governance requires a set of principles that guide how an organization establishes authority, manages risk, protects AI systems, and maintains accountability.

These principles provide the foundation for decisions involving:

* AI strategy
* AI security
* AI risk
* AI data
* AI development
* AI deployment
* AI operations
* AI monitoring
* AI incidents
* AI lifecycle management

Principles provide direction at a higher level than detailed policies, standards, or procedures.

---

### 2. Purpose of AI Security Governance Principles

AI Security Governance principles establish the fundamental expectations that should apply across the organization.

They help ensure that AI is:

* Governed
* Secure
* Risk-aware
* Accountable
* Controlled
* Monitored
* Resilient
* Appropriate for its intended purpose

They also provide a consistent basis for developing more detailed governance requirements.

---

### 3. Principles as Governance Foundations

A principle expresses a fundamental expectation.

For example:

> AI systems should be governed according to their level of risk and potential impact.

A policy may convert that principle into mandatory requirements.

A standard may define specific technical or control requirements.

A procedure may describe how employees perform the required activity.

The hierarchy can therefore be represented as:

**Principle**

→ Establishes direction.

**Policy**

→ Establishes mandatory organizational requirements.

**Standard**

→ Defines specific requirements.

**Procedure**

→ Defines how requirements are executed.

---

### 4. Enterprise-Wide Applicability

AI Security Governance principles should generally apply across the organization.

They should cover AI used by:

* Employees
* Business units
* IT
* Security
* Engineering
* Research
* Contractors
* Third parties

They should also apply to AI systems developed internally and acquired externally where appropriate.

---

### 5. Principle of Accountability

Every material AI system should have clearly defined accountability.

The organization should identify:

* Business owner
* Technical owner
* Risk owner
* Security responsibility
* Privacy responsibility where applicable
* Approval authority
* Monitoring responsibility

AI systems should not operate without identifiable human accountability.

---

### 6. Principle of Clear Ownership

AI governance should establish ownership throughout the AI lifecycle.

Ownership should cover:

* Use-case definition
* Data
* Model
* Application
* Infrastructure
* Security
* Risk
* Operations
* Performance
* Retirement

Ownership should remain clear even when AI services are provided by third parties.

---

### 7. Principle of Risk-Based Governance

AI governance should be proportional to risk.

Higher-risk AI should receive greater:

* Assessment
* Review
* Testing
* Approval
* Monitoring
* Documentation
* Oversight

Low-risk AI should not be subjected to unnecessary governance burdens that prevent productive use.

---

### 8. Principle of Security by Design

Security should be incorporated into AI systems from the beginning.

Security considerations should be addressed during:

* Planning
* Architecture
* Development
* Testing
* Deployment
* Operations
* Change
* Retirement

Security should not be treated solely as a post-deployment activity.

---

### 9. Principle of Secure by Default

AI systems should be configured to provide an appropriate level of security without requiring users to manually activate every important control.

Secure defaults may include:

* Restricted access
* Least privilege
* Protected credentials
* Safe configuration
* Logging
* Monitoring
* Data protection

Users should not be expected to compensate for insecure system design.

---

### 10. Principle of Least Privilege

AI systems, agents, applications, and users should receive only the permissions required to perform authorized activities.

Least privilege should apply to:

* Users
* AI agents
* APIs
* Service accounts
* Applications
* Model access
* Data access

The principle becomes particularly important when AI systems can perform actions autonomously.

---

### 11. Principle of Defense in Depth

AI security should use multiple layers of protection.

Controls may include:

* Identity
* Access control
* Network security
* Application security
* Data security
* Model security
* Monitoring
* Detection
* Incident response

No individual control should be assumed to provide complete protection.

---

### 12. Principle of Zero Trust

AI environments should not automatically trust users, applications, agents, models, APIs, or data sources.

Access should be evaluated based on:

* Identity
* Context
* Authorization
* Device or workload state
* Risk
* Requested resource

Trust should be continuously evaluated where appropriate.

---

### 13. Principle of Data Protection

AI systems should protect the confidentiality, integrity, and appropriate availability of data.

Governance should address:

* Data classification
* Access
* Encryption
* Data leakage
* Retention
* Data transfer
* Data provenance
* Data disposal

Data protection requirements should reflect data sensitivity.

---

### 14. Principle of Data Minimization

AI systems should use only the data necessary for their intended purpose.

Organizations should avoid unnecessary collection or exposure of:

* Personal data
* Confidential information
* Credentials
* Secrets
* Proprietary information
* Sensitive business data

Data minimization reduces both privacy and security exposure.

---

### 15. Principle of Data Integrity

AI governance should protect the integrity of data used by AI systems.

Controls should address risks such as:

* Unauthorized modification
* Data poisoning
* Corruption
* Incorrect data
* Manipulated training data

AI decisions can be compromised when underlying data cannot be trusted.

---

### 16. Principle of Data Provenance

Organizations should maintain appropriate knowledge of where important AI data originates.

Provenance may include:

* Source
* Collection method
* Owner
* Transformation
* Processing
* Usage
* Version

Strong provenance improves traceability and supports investigation.

---

### 17. Principle of AI System Integrity

AI systems should be protected against unauthorized or malicious modification.

Integrity controls may cover:

* Models
* Code
* Configurations
* Prompts
* Policies
* Plugins
* APIs
* Supporting infrastructure

Changes should be authorized and traceable.

---

### 18. Principle of Model Integrity

Models should be protected throughout their lifecycle.

Controls may address:

* Model storage
* Model access
* Model versions
* Model deployment
* Model updates
* Model provenance
* Model integrity verification

Unauthorized modification can alter AI behavior and create security risks.

---

### 19. Principle of Secure AI Development

AI development should incorporate secure development practices.

These may include:

* Secure coding
* Code review
* Dependency management
* Security testing
* Secrets management
* Configuration control
* Vulnerability management

Security requirements should apply throughout the AI development lifecycle.

---

### 20. Principle of Secure AI Deployment

AI systems should satisfy defined governance and security requirements before production deployment.

Deployment requirements may include:

* Risk assessment
* Security review
* Testing
* Approval
* Access control
* Monitoring
* Incident-response readiness

Technical readiness alone should not be sufficient for production deployment.

---

### 21. Principle of Continuous Monitoring

AI security governance should not end when an AI system enters production.

Organizations should continuously or periodically monitor:

* Security
* Performance
* Access
* Model behavior
* Data
* Incidents
* Risk
* Control effectiveness

Monitoring frequency should reflect system criticality and risk.

---

### 22. Principle of Continuous Risk Assessment

AI risks can change after deployment.

Risk may increase because of:

* New threats
* New vulnerabilities
* New data
* New users
* New integrations
* Model changes
* Increased autonomy
* Regulatory changes

Risk assessment should therefore be revisited when material changes occur.

---

### 23. Principle of Human Oversight

AI systems should have appropriate human oversight based on their risk and impact.

Human oversight may involve:

* Review
* Approval
* Intervention
* Override
* Escalation
* Manual fallback

The higher the potential impact, the greater the need for meaningful human control.

---

### 24. Principle of Human Accountability

AI should not eliminate organizational accountability.

Even when an AI system performs an action autonomously, the organization should identify who is accountable for:

* Its design
* Deployment
* Operation
* Monitoring
* Risk management
* Corrective action

AI autonomy does not transfer accountability to the machine.

---

### 25. Principle of Transparency

Organizations should maintain appropriate transparency about how AI is used.

Transparency may include:

* Purpose
* Capabilities
* Limitations
* Data use
* Decision involvement
* Human oversight

The level of transparency should be appropriate to the context and applicable requirements.

---

### 26. Principle of Traceability

Important AI activities should be traceable.

Traceability may cover:

* Data
* Model versions
* Configuration
* Changes
* Decisions
* Approvals
* Access
* Incidents

Traceability supports accountability, investigation, and assurance.

---

### 27. Principle of Auditability

AI governance processes should produce sufficient evidence to demonstrate that requirements were followed.

Evidence may include:

* Assessments
* Approvals
* Test results
* Logs
* Change records
* Risk decisions
* Monitoring results

Auditability supports both internal assurance and external accountability.

---

### 28. Principle of Explainability Where Appropriate

Organizations should seek an appropriate level of explainability for AI systems.

The requirement should depend on:

* Use case
* Risk
* Impact
* Regulatory requirements
* Stakeholder needs

Not every AI system requires the same degree or type of explanation.

---

### 29. Principle of Resilience

AI systems supporting important business processes should be designed and governed for resilience.

Resilience may include:

* Redundancy
* Recovery
* Backup
* Failover
* Manual fallback
* Alternative providers
* Incident response

AI availability requirements should correspond to business criticality.

---

### 30. Principle of Secure Failure

AI systems should fail in a manner that limits harm when they cannot operate normally.

Depending on the use case, secure failure may involve:

* Refusing an action
* Requiring human approval
* Switching to a safe state
* Restricting functionality
* Falling back to manual processing

The appropriate failure behavior should be defined before deployment.

---

### 31. Principle of Controlled Autonomy

AI autonomy should be explicitly governed.

Organizations should define:

* Permitted actions
* Restricted actions
* Approval requirements
* Transaction limits
* Access boundaries
* Escalation conditions
* Emergency shutdown mechanisms

The greater the autonomy, the stronger the need for controls.

---

### 32. Principle of Separation of Duties

Critical AI governance activities should be appropriately separated.

Where practical, different individuals or functions should perform:

* Development
* Approval
* Deployment
* Risk acceptance
* Monitoring
* Assurance

Separation reduces the risk of uncontrolled decisions and conflicts of interest.

---

### 33. Principle of Independent Challenge

AI decisions should be subject to appropriate challenge.

Independent challenge may come from:

* Cybersecurity
* Risk
* Privacy
* Legal
* Compliance
* Internal Audit

The purpose is to identify weaknesses that operational teams may overlook.

---

### 34. Principle of Third-Party Accountability

Using an external AI provider does not eliminate the organization's governance responsibilities.

The organization should understand:

* What the provider does
* What data is processed
* What controls exist
* What risks remain
* What contractual protections apply
* What happens if the provider fails

Outsourcing AI does not mean outsourcing accountability.

---

### 35. Principle of AI Supply-Chain Security

AI dependencies should be governed throughout the supply chain.

This may include:

* Models
* Datasets
* Libraries
* APIs
* Cloud services
* AI platforms
* Vendors
* Subprocessors

Organizations should understand material dependencies and associated risks.

---

### 36. Principle of Continuous Improvement

AI Security Governance should evolve as:

* AI technology changes
* Threats evolve
* Regulations change
* Business use changes
* Incidents occur
* Governance weaknesses are identified

Lessons learned should be incorporated into policies, standards, controls, training, and governance processes.

---

### 37. Principle of Proportionate Control

Controls should be proportionate to the AI system's:

* Risk
* Criticality
* Autonomy
* Data sensitivity
* Regulatory exposure
* Potential impact

Excessive controls can discourage legitimate AI adoption, while insufficient controls can expose the organization to unacceptable risk.

---

### 38. Principle of Enterprise Integration

AI Security Governance should integrate with existing governance disciplines.

These include:

* Enterprise risk management
* Cybersecurity
* Privacy
* Data governance
* Compliance
* Business continuity
* Third-party risk
* Internal audit

AI governance should strengthen existing governance rather than create unnecessary parallel structures.

---

### 39. Principle of Consistent Governance

Organizations should establish consistent minimum AI Security Governance requirements across business units.

Local teams may require additional controls based on:

* Jurisdiction
* Industry
* Risk
* Business process
* Regulatory obligations

However, decentralization should not result in uncontrolled differences in fundamental security requirements.

---

### 40. Principle of Accountability for Outcomes

AI Security Governance should ultimately focus on outcomes rather than documentation alone.

An effective governance system should help ensure that AI systems are:

* Secure
* Controlled
* Resilient
* Accountable
* Appropriate for their intended use
* Continuously monitored
* Managed throughout their lifecycle

Governance principles provide the foundation for translating these expectations into organizational policies, standards, procedures, controls, and decision-making practices.

## Part 2 — Risk, Security, Accountability and Control Principles

### 41. Principle of Risk Ownership

Every material AI risk should have an identified owner with sufficient authority to manage the risk.

Risk ownership should include responsibility for:

* Understanding the risk
* Evaluating its potential impact
* Implementing treatment
* Monitoring residual exposure
* Escalating material changes
* Accepting or transferring risk where authorized

Risk ownership should not remain ambiguous because multiple functions contribute to the assessment.

---

### 42. Principle of Risk-Based Decision-Making

AI decisions should be based on the level and nature of risk involved.

Risk-based decisions should consider:

* Likelihood
* Impact
* Threat exposure
* Vulnerability
* Business criticality
* Data sensitivity
* Regulatory requirements
* Degree of autonomy

The organization should avoid treating every AI system as having the same governance requirements.

---

### 43. Principle of Risk Appetite Alignment

AI activities should operate within the organization's approved risk appetite.

AI governance should establish mechanisms for determining whether:

* Inherent risk is acceptable
* Controls adequately reduce exposure
* Residual risk remains within tolerance
* Escalation is required
* Formal risk acceptance is necessary

AI initiatives should not silently exceed established risk boundaries.

---

### 44. Principle of Residual Risk Visibility

AI governance should distinguish between inherent risk and residual risk.

**Inherent risk**

→ Risk before controls.

**Control effectiveness**

→ The extent to which controls reduce exposure.

**Residual risk**

→ Risk remaining after controls.

Leadership should understand residual risk when making significant AI decisions.

---

### 45. Principle of Risk Acceptance Authority

Risk acceptance should be performed by an individual or governance body with appropriate authority.

The authority should correspond to:

* Risk magnitude
* Business impact
* Regulatory significance
* System criticality

Individuals should not accept risks beyond their delegated authority.

---

### 46. Principle of Time-Bounded Risk Acceptance

AI risk acceptance should normally have defined review or expiration dates where appropriate.

A risk acceptance record should identify:

* Accepted risk
* Business justification
* Compensating controls
* Approver
* Effective date
* Expiration or review date

This prevents temporary exceptions from becoming permanent weaknesses.

---

### 47. Principle of Compensating Controls

Where a required control cannot be implemented, appropriate compensating controls should be considered.

Compensating controls should:

* Address the underlying risk
* Be documented
* Have an identified owner
* Be monitored
* Have an appropriate duration

Compensating controls should not simply serve as justification for avoiding required security measures.

---

### 48. Principle of Preventive Security

AI governance should prioritize prevention where practical.

Preventive controls may include:

* Secure architecture
* Access control
* Data classification
* Input validation
* Secure configuration
* Approved AI platforms
* Restricted permissions

Preventive controls should be complemented by detective and corrective controls.

---

### 49. Principle of Detective Security

AI environments should contain mechanisms capable of identifying suspicious or unauthorized activity.

Detective controls may include:

* Logging
* Monitoring
* Alerts
* Anomaly detection
* Security analytics
* Model behavior monitoring

Detection should provide sufficient information to support investigation and response.

---

### 50. Principle of Corrective Security

AI governance should provide mechanisms for responding to identified weaknesses.

Corrective actions may include:

* Configuration changes
* Access removal
* Model rollback
* Data correction
* Vulnerability remediation
* System isolation
* Suspension

Corrective capabilities should be tested where appropriate.

---

### 51. Principle of Incident Readiness

AI systems should be incorporated into organizational incident-response planning.

Preparation should address:

* Detection
* Triage
* Containment
* Investigation
* Recovery
* Communication
* Regulatory reporting where applicable

AI incidents should not require the organization to create an entirely new response capability.

---

### 52. Principle of AI Incident Accountability

AI incidents should have clearly defined ownership.

The organization should establish:

* Who detects incidents
* Who investigates
* Who coordinates response
* Who makes business decisions
* Who communicates with stakeholders
* Who determines escalation
* Who validates recovery

Responsibilities should be defined before incidents occur.

---

### 53. Principle of AI Incident Learning

AI incidents and near misses should generate lessons that improve governance.

Post-incident analysis may examine:

* Root causes
* Control failures
* Governance weaknesses
* Human factors
* Technology weaknesses
* Process deficiencies

Lessons should be incorporated into appropriate controls and governance practices.

---

### 54. Principle of Secure Change Management

Material AI changes should be subject to appropriate change-management controls.

Changes may involve:

* Models
* Prompts
* Data
* Algorithms
* APIs
* Integrations
* Permissions
* Infrastructure
* Autonomous capabilities

The governance process should determine when a change requires reassessment.

---

### 55. Principle of Model Version Control

AI models should be appropriately versioned.

Version management should support:

* Identification
* Traceability
* Testing
* Approval
* Deployment
* Rollback
* Investigation

Organizations should be able to determine which model version was operating at a particular point in time.

---

### 56. Principle of Configuration Management

AI-related configurations should be controlled throughout the lifecycle.

Configuration may include:

* Model parameters
* System prompts
* Access policies
* API settings
* Tool permissions
* Security controls
* Deployment settings

Unauthorized configuration changes should be prevented or detected.

---

### 57. Principle of Controlled AI Interfaces

Interfaces between AI systems and other systems should be governed.

Interfaces may include:

* APIs
* Plugins
* Connectors
* Databases
* Enterprise applications
* External services

Each integration can introduce additional attack paths and should be assessed according to its risk.

---

### 58. Principle of Secure AI APIs

AI APIs should be protected against unauthorized access and misuse.

Controls may include:

* Authentication
* Authorization
* Rate limiting
* Input validation
* Monitoring
* Secret management
* Network restrictions

API security should be integrated into the organization's application-security practices.

---

### 59. Principle of Controlled Tool Access

AI agents that can use tools should receive only the tools necessary for their intended function.

Tool access should consider:

* Permission level
* Data sensitivity
* Action capability
* Transaction impact
* User context

High-impact actions may require explicit human authorization.

---

### 60. Principle of Transaction Control

Where AI can initiate transactions, governance should establish appropriate limits.

Controls may include:

* Monetary limits
* Frequency limits
* Approved destinations
* Human approval
* Transaction monitoring
* Automated blocking

The greater the potential impact of an action, the stronger the transaction controls should be.

---

### 61. Principle of Privileged AI Access

AI systems capable of privileged activity should receive enhanced governance.

Privileged capabilities may include:

* Administrative access
* Infrastructure modification
* Identity management
* Financial transactions
* Security configuration
* Production changes

Privileged AI access should be strictly controlled and monitored.

---

### 62. Principle of Identity for AI Systems

AI systems and agents should have identifiable digital identities where technically appropriate.

Identity management should support:

* Authentication
* Authorization
* Attribution
* Monitoring
* Revocation

An organization should be able to determine which AI system or agent performed a material action.

---

### 63. Principle of Non-Repudiation

For material AI activities, governance should provide sufficient evidence to establish who or what initiated an action.

This may involve:

* Identity
* Authentication records
* Logs
* Timestamps
* Model version
* System configuration

Non-repudiation supports accountability and investigation.

---

### 64. Principle of Logging

Material AI activities should generate appropriate logs.

Logging requirements may cover:

* User access
* AI access
* Administrative actions
* Model changes
* Configuration changes
* Tool invocation
* Security events
* Significant decisions

Logging requirements should reflect risk and applicable obligations.

---

### 65. Principle of Log Integrity

AI security logs should be protected against unauthorized modification or deletion.

Controls may include:

* Access restrictions
* Centralized collection
* Integrity protection
* Retention controls
* Monitoring

Logs should remain trustworthy enough to support investigations and assurance.

---

### 66. Principle of Appropriate Log Retention

AI-related logs should be retained for periods appropriate to:

* Risk
* Business requirements
* Security investigations
* Legal requirements
* Regulatory obligations

Retention should balance investigative needs against storage, privacy, and data-minimization considerations.

---

### 67. Principle of Secure AI Data Flows

Organizations should understand how sensitive data moves through AI environments.

Data-flow governance should identify:

* Sources
* AI applications
* Models
* APIs
* Vendors
* Storage locations
* Outputs

Uncontrolled data movement can create significant security and privacy exposure.

---

### 68. Principle of Data Boundary Protection

AI systems should have clearly defined data boundaries.

Controls should restrict inappropriate movement of data between:

* Users
* Applications
* Models
* Business units
* Environments
* Vendors

Data boundaries should correspond to organizational and regulatory requirements.

---

### 69. Principle of Environment Separation

AI development, testing, and production environments should be appropriately separated.

Separation may reduce the risk of:

* Unauthorized production access
* Sensitive-data exposure
* Unapproved model changes
* Testing activities affecting production

The degree of separation should reflect risk.

---

### 70. Principle of Secure AI Testing

AI testing environments should be governed to prevent testing activities from creating unnecessary security exposure.

Testing should consider:

* Test data
* Access
* Model versions
* Attack simulation
* Sensitive information
* Production connectivity

High-risk testing should be appropriately authorized.

---

### 71. Principle of Adversarial Testing

AI systems with significant security or safety implications should be subjected to adversarial testing appropriate to their risk.

Testing may explore:

* Prompt injection
* Data leakage
* Model manipulation
* Jailbreaks
* Unauthorized actions
* Malicious inputs

Testing should identify weaknesses before they become operational incidents.

---

### 72. Principle of Threat-Informed AI Security

AI security controls should reflect current and credible threats.

Threat intelligence should inform:

* Risk assessments
* Threat modeling
* Security architecture
* Testing
* Monitoring
* Incident response

AI security should evolve as threat techniques evolve.

---

### 73. Principle of Vulnerability Management

AI systems and their supporting infrastructure should be included in vulnerability-management processes where applicable.

Vulnerability management may cover:

* AI applications
* APIs
* Infrastructure
* Libraries
* Containers
* Operating systems
* Supporting services

Material vulnerabilities should be prioritized according to risk.

---

### 74. Principle of Dependency Management

AI systems should maintain visibility into significant technical dependencies.

Dependencies may include:

* Open-source packages
* Models
* APIs
* Cloud services
* Libraries
* Data sources
* Vendors

Material dependencies should be assessed for security and resilience risks.

---

### 75. Principle of AI Software Supply-Chain Security

AI software components should be obtained, developed, and maintained through controlled processes.

Controls may include:

* Trusted sources
* Dependency scanning
* Integrity verification
* Version control
* Vulnerability management
* Software bills of materials where appropriate

Supply-chain security should extend beyond the organization's own code.

---

### 76. Principle of Model Supply-Chain Security

Models obtained from external sources should be subject to appropriate scrutiny.

Assessment may consider:

* Model provenance
* Provider reputation
* Integrity
* Licensing
* Security characteristics
* Known vulnerabilities
* Update mechanisms

Organizations should understand the risks associated with externally sourced models.

---

### 77. Principle of Third-Party AI Due Diligence

Third-party AI providers should be assessed before material use.

Due diligence may consider:

* Security
* Privacy
* Resilience
* Compliance
* Data handling
* Subprocessors
* Incident response
* Business continuity

Assessment depth should correspond to the provider's importance and risk.

---

### 78. Principle of Contractual AI Security Requirements

Contracts with AI providers should address appropriate security and governance requirements.

Contractual provisions may cover:

* Data use
* Confidentiality
* Security controls
* Incident notification
* Audit rights
* Subprocessors
* Data deletion
* Service continuity
* Termination

Contracts should support the organization's governance requirements.

---

### 79. Principle of Exit and Portability

Organizations should consider how they will exit significant AI services.

Exit planning may address:

* Data retrieval
* Model portability
* Configuration
* Replacement providers
* Migration
* Contract termination
* Service continuity

Critical AI dependencies should not create unmanaged lock-in.

---

### 80. Principle of Governance Continuity

AI governance responsibilities should continue even when systems, vendors, models, or organizational structures change.

Governance should preserve:

* Accountability
* Risk visibility
* Security controls
* Privacy protections
* Documentation
* Monitoring
* Assurance

AI governance should remain durable across changes in technology and organizational circumstances.

## Part 3 — Governance, Lifecycle, Resilience, Assurance and Organizational Principles

### 81. Principle of Governance Integration

AI Security Governance should be integrated into the organization's existing governance architecture.

AI governance should connect with:

* Enterprise governance
* Enterprise risk management
* Cybersecurity governance
* Data governance
* Privacy governance
* Compliance
* Business continuity
* Third-party risk management
* Internal audit

Integration reduces duplication and helps ensure that AI risks are managed consistently.

---

### 82. Principle of Governance Consistency

Organizations should establish consistent minimum governance requirements for AI.

Business units may implement additional requirements based on:

* Risk
* Jurisdiction
* Industry
* Business criticality
* Regulatory obligations

Decentralized AI adoption should not result in inconsistent fundamental security expectations.

---

### 83. Principle of Policy Alignment

AI Security Governance policies should align with broader organizational policies.

Relevant policies may include:

* Information security
* Data protection
* Acceptable use
* Access management
* Secure development
* Third-party risk
* Incident management
* Business continuity

AI governance should complement these policies rather than contradict them.

---

### 84. Principle of Regulatory Alignment

AI governance should account for applicable laws, regulations, contractual obligations, and industry requirements.

Organizations should identify requirements relevant to:

* AI
* Cybersecurity
* Privacy
* Data protection
* Safety
* Consumer protection
* Sector-specific obligations

Regulatory requirements should be incorporated into appropriate governance controls.

---

### 85. Principle of Jurisdictional Awareness

AI systems operating across jurisdictions may be subject to different requirements.

Governance should consider:

* Data location
* Data transfers
* Applicable laws
* Regulatory authorities
* Sector requirements
* Local restrictions

Organizations should maintain sufficient visibility to identify jurisdiction-specific obligations.

---

### 86. Principle of Regulatory Change Management

AI governance should account for changing regulatory requirements.

Organizations should establish processes for:

* Regulatory monitoring
* Impact assessment
* Requirement mapping
* Policy updates
* Control changes
* Management reporting

Regulatory change should trigger governance reassessment where material.

---

### 87. Principle of Lifecycle Governance

AI governance should cover the complete AI lifecycle.

Lifecycle stages may include:

1. Strategy
2. Ideation
3. Design
4. Data preparation
5. Development
6. Testing
7. Approval
8. Deployment
9. Operation
10. Monitoring
11. Modification
12. Retirement

Governance should remain applicable throughout these stages.

---

### 88. Principle of Governance Before Development

AI governance should begin before significant development activity starts.

Early governance should determine:

* Intended purpose
* Business owner
* Risk classification
* Data requirements
* Security expectations
* Privacy considerations
* Regulatory requirements

Early governance reduces the likelihood of discovering fundamental problems late in the lifecycle.

---

### 89. Principle of Governance Before Deployment

AI systems should not enter production without satisfying applicable governance requirements.

Depending on risk, requirements may include:

* Risk assessment
* Security assessment
* Privacy assessment
* Testing
* Approval
* Monitoring
* Incident-response readiness

Production deployment should represent a controlled governance decision.

---

### 90. Principle of Continuous Governance

Governance should continue after deployment.

Organizations should periodically evaluate:

* Risk
* Security
* Performance
* Data
* Model behavior
* Compliance
* Business value

Deployment should not mark the end of governance activity.

---

### 91. Principle of Material Change Reassessment

Material changes to an AI system should trigger reassessment.

Examples include:

* New model
* New dataset
* New business purpose
* New user population
* New geographic region
* New integration
* Increased autonomy
* New third-party provider

The organization should determine whether the change alters the system's risk classification.

---

### 92. Principle of AI Inventory

Organizations should maintain an appropriate inventory of AI systems.

An inventory may identify:

* AI system
* Business owner
* Technical owner
* Purpose
* Model
* Data
* Provider
* Risk classification
* Lifecycle status
* Criticality

Inventory visibility is a fundamental governance capability.

---

### 93. Principle of AI Asset Identification

Organizations should identify the assets supporting AI systems.

These may include:

* Models
* Data
* Applications
* APIs
* Infrastructure
* Credentials
* Tools
* Agents
* Cloud services

Security controls should reflect the importance of these assets.

---

### 94. Principle of AI Dependency Visibility

Organizations should maintain visibility into significant dependencies.

Dependencies may include:

* Foundation models
* Cloud providers
* APIs
* Data providers
* Open-source components
* SaaS platforms
* Infrastructure

Unknown dependencies can create unmanaged security and resilience risks.

---

### 95. Principle of AI Criticality Classification

AI systems should be classified according to their business importance and potential impact.

Criticality may consider:

* Business disruption
* Financial impact
* Customer impact
* Safety implications
* Regulatory consequences
* Operational dependency

Critical AI systems should receive enhanced governance.

---

### 96. Principle of Business Continuity

Critical AI systems should be incorporated into business continuity planning.

Planning should consider:

* AI service outage
* Model unavailability
* Provider failure
* Data unavailability
* Cybersecurity incidents
* Infrastructure failure

Organizations should understand how critical business processes will continue if AI becomes unavailable.

---

### 97. Principle of Disaster Recovery

Where appropriate, AI systems should have recovery capabilities proportionate to their criticality.

Recovery planning may address:

* Data recovery
* Model recovery
* Infrastructure recovery
* Configuration recovery
* Service restoration
* Alternative environments

Recovery requirements should be aligned with business needs.

---

### 98. Principle of Manual Fallback

Where AI supports important decisions or operations, organizations should consider whether a manual fallback capability is necessary.

A fallback may allow operations to continue when:

* AI is unavailable
* AI produces unreliable results
* Security is compromised
* Data becomes unavailable
* Human intervention is required

The feasibility of manual fallback should be assessed during system design.

---

### 99. Principle of Safe Degradation

AI systems should be capable of reducing functionality safely when necessary.

Safe degradation may include:

* Restricting capabilities
* Removing autonomous actions
* Limiting data access
* Requiring human approval
* Switching to predefined rules

This can reduce the consequences of failures or emerging threats.

---

### 100. Principle of AI Recovery Testing

Recovery mechanisms should be tested where appropriate.

Testing may validate:

* Recovery procedures
* Backup availability
* Model restoration
* Configuration restoration
* Alternative services
* Manual fallback

Untested recovery capabilities should not automatically be considered reliable.

---

### 101. Principle of Security Resilience

AI Security Governance should consider the organization's ability to withstand and recover from attacks.

Resilience may include:

* Redundancy
* Isolation
* Recovery
* Threat detection
* Incident response
* Alternative capabilities

Security should be considered together with availability and continuity.

---

### 102. Principle of Availability Proportionality

Availability requirements should reflect the business criticality of the AI system.

A non-critical AI productivity tool may tolerate extended downtime.

An AI system supporting critical operations may require:

* High availability
* Redundancy
* Rapid recovery
* Alternative processing

Availability requirements should be formally defined where appropriate.

---

### 103. Principle of AI Safety Boundaries

AI systems should operate within clearly defined boundaries.

Boundaries may restrict:

* Data
* Users
* Actions
* Systems
* Geographic locations
* Transaction values
* Autonomous behavior

Boundaries should be technically enforced where feasible.

---

### 104. Principle of Human Override

Where appropriate, authorized humans should be able to override AI behavior.

Override mechanisms may be required when:

* AI produces unsafe results
* An unexpected action occurs
* A security incident is detected
* A critical decision requires human intervention

Override mechanisms should be accessible to authorized personnel.

---

### 105. Principle of Emergency Shutdown

High-impact AI systems should have appropriate mechanisms for rapid suspension or shutdown.

Emergency controls may include:

* Agent disablement
* API blocking
* Access revocation
* Model rollback
* Service isolation
* System shutdown

The mechanism should be tested where appropriate.

---

### 106. Principle of Monitoring for Unauthorized AI

Organizations should establish appropriate visibility into unauthorized AI usage.

Potential indicators include:

* Unapproved AI applications
* Unauthorized APIs
* Sensitive data submitted to external AI
* Personal AI accounts used for business purposes
* Unapproved AI agents

Monitoring should respect applicable privacy and employment requirements.

---

### 107. Principle of Approved AI Services

Organizations should establish a mechanism for identifying approved AI services.

An approved-service process may consider:

* Security
* Privacy
* Legal
* Risk
* Business requirements
* Technical suitability

Employees should have practical approved alternatives to reduce incentives for shadow AI.

---

### 108. Principle of Responsible AI Use

AI systems should be used consistently with organizational values, policies, laws, and intended purposes.

Responsible use should consider:

* Human impact
* Fairness
* Transparency
* Accountability
* Security
* Privacy
* Appropriate human oversight

Security governance should support responsible use rather than operate separately from it.

---

### 109. Principle of Purpose Limitation

AI should be used for defined and authorized purposes.

Organizations should avoid allowing systems to gradually expand into materially different uses without reassessment.

A new purpose may introduce:

* New risks
* New data requirements
* New regulatory obligations
* New security concerns

---

### 110. Principle of Appropriate Use

AI capabilities should be appropriate for the intended business purpose.

Organizations should evaluate whether:

* The model is suitable
* The data is appropriate
* The level of accuracy is sufficient
* The security controls are adequate
* Human oversight is appropriate

Technical capability alone does not establish suitability.

---

### 111. Principle of AI Output Validation

AI-generated outputs should be validated when errors could cause meaningful harm.

Validation requirements should reflect:

* Impact
* Accuracy requirements
* Decision significance
* User context
* Regulatory requirements

Higher-impact outputs should receive stronger validation.

---

### 112. Principle of Human Verification

Where AI outputs influence significant decisions, appropriate human verification should be established.

Human verification should be:

* Meaningful
* Competent
* Timely
* Capable of challenging the AI output

Human involvement should not become a purely symbolic approval step.

---

### 113. Principle of AI Literacy

Personnel using or governing AI should have sufficient knowledge to understand:

* AI capabilities
* AI limitations
* Security risks
* Privacy risks
* Governance requirements
* Appropriate use

Training should be proportional to the individual's role.

---

### 114. Principle of Role-Based AI Training

AI training should reflect different responsibilities.

For example:

**General Employees**

→ Safe and approved AI use.

**Developers**

→ Secure AI development.

**Security Teams**

→ AI threats and security controls.

**Risk Teams**

→ AI risk assessment.

**Executives**

→ AI strategy and governance.

**Board**

→ AI risk oversight.

---

### 115. Principle of Security Awareness

Employees should understand the security consequences of inappropriate AI use.

Awareness should address:

* Sensitive data exposure
* Prompt injection
* Social engineering
* AI-generated phishing
* Deepfakes
* Unauthorized AI services
* Malicious AI outputs

Security awareness should evolve with emerging AI threats.

---

### 116. Principle of Reporting

Employees should have clear mechanisms for reporting AI concerns.

Reports may involve:

* Security incidents
* Privacy concerns
* Unsafe AI behavior
* Unauthorized AI use
* Policy violations
* Unexpected outputs
* Potential bias or harm

Reporting channels should be accessible and appropriately protected.

---

### 117. Principle of Speak-Up Culture

Employees should be able to raise concerns without inappropriate retaliation.

Leadership should encourage reporting of:

* AI security weaknesses
* Governance failures
* Unsafe behavior
* Unauthorized use
* Control deficiencies

A strong speak-up culture supports early identification of AI risks.

---

### 118. Principle of Governance Awareness

Employees should understand that AI governance is an organizational responsibility.

AI governance should not be viewed solely as the responsibility of:

* IT
* Cybersecurity
* Legal
* Compliance
* AI teams

Every employee using AI can influence the organization's risk profile.

---

### 119. Principle of Ethical Escalation

Where employees identify AI behavior that may create serious ethical, legal, security, privacy, or safety concerns, appropriate escalation mechanisms should exist.

Employees should know:

* Where to report
* What information to provide
* How escalation works
* When immediate action is required

Governance should support early intervention.

---

### 120. Principle of Governance by Design

The fundamental objective of AI Security Governance is to make governance part of how AI is designed, acquired, developed, deployed, operated, changed, and retired.

AI governance should therefore be embedded into:

* Business processes
* Technology architecture
* Security controls
* Risk management
* Data governance
* Privacy management
* Procurement
* Lifecycle management
* Assurance
* Organizational culture

When governance is designed into the AI operating environment rather than added afterward, the organization is better positioned to maintain **security, accountability, resilience, compliance, and responsible control throughout the AI lifecycle**.

# 01.14 — AI Security Governance Principles

## Part 4 — Strategic, Operational and Assurance Principles

### 121. Principle of Strategic Alignment

AI Security Governance should be aligned with the organization's overall strategy.

AI security decisions should support:

* Business objectives
* Enterprise risk appetite
* Digital strategy
* Technology strategy
* Cybersecurity strategy
* Data strategy
* Regulatory obligations

AI governance should enable the organization to use AI securely while supporting legitimate business objectives.

---

### 122. Principle of Business Value and Security Balance

AI governance should balance business value with security and risk considerations.

Organizations should evaluate:

* Expected business value
* Security exposure
* Operational impact
* Regulatory requirements
* Implementation cost
* Residual risk

Security should not automatically prevent innovation, while business value should not automatically justify unacceptable security exposure.

---

### 123. Principle of Proportionate Governance Investment

Governance resources should be allocated according to risk and business significance.

Higher-risk systems may require greater investment in:

* Security
* Testing
* Monitoring
* Governance
* Documentation
* Assurance

Governance investment should therefore reflect the potential consequences of AI failure or misuse.

---

### 124. Principle of Governance Scalability

AI Security Governance should scale as AI adoption increases.

The governance model should support:

* Small numbers of AI systems
* Large enterprise AI portfolios
* Multiple business units
* Multiple geographic regions
* Multiple AI providers
* Multiple AI technologies

Governance processes should remain practical as the organization's AI environment expands.

---

### 125. Principle of Standardization

Organizations should establish common AI Security Governance standards where consistency provides value.

Standardization may cover:

* Risk classification
* Security requirements
* AI inventories
* Assessment processes
* Approval workflows
* Monitoring
* Incident reporting
* Documentation

Standardization improves comparability and reduces unnecessary variation.

---

### 126. Principle of Controlled Flexibility

Standardization should not eliminate legitimate flexibility.

Business units may require different controls because of:

* Business processes
* Risk levels
* Regulatory obligations
* Technology environments
* Geographic requirements

Flexibility should operate within defined governance boundaries.

---

### 127. Principle of Governance Automation

Organizations should automate AI governance activities where practical.

Automation may support:

* AI discovery
* Inventory management
* Risk classification
* Policy enforcement
* Access reviews
* Monitoring
* Compliance evidence
* Exception management

Automation can improve scalability and consistency.

---

### 128. Principle of Continuous Control Monitoring

Where technically feasible, AI security controls should be monitored continuously or at an appropriate frequency.

Monitoring may identify:

* Control failures
* Configuration drift
* Unauthorized access
* Unapproved models
* Excessive permissions
* Policy violations

Continuous monitoring can reduce the period between control failure and detection.

---

### 129. Principle of Governance Metrics

Organizations should establish metrics that demonstrate whether AI governance is functioning effectively.

Metrics may measure:

* AI inventory coverage
* Risk assessment completion
* Security assessment completion
* Policy compliance
* Exception volume
* Incident frequency
* Training completion
* Remediation performance

Metrics should support decision-making rather than become reporting exercises without action.

---

### 130. Principle of Risk-Based Key Performance Indicators

AI governance metrics should distinguish between activity and effectiveness.

For example:

**Activity metric**

→ Number of AI systems assessed.

**Effectiveness metric**

→ Percentage of material AI systems with identified high-risk findings remediated within the required timeframe.

Effectiveness-oriented metrics provide stronger evidence of governance performance.

---

### 131. Principle of Key Risk Indicators

Organizations should establish indicators that identify increasing AI risk.

Potential indicators include:

* Growth in unauthorized AI usage
* Increase in critical vulnerabilities
* Increase in security incidents
* Excessive exceptions
* Increasing third-party dependency
* Significant model changes
* Increasing data exposure

KRIs should support early intervention.

---

### 132. Principle of Management Accountability for Metrics

AI governance metrics should have accountable owners.

Each significant metric should have:

* Definition
* Data source
* Calculation method
* Reporting frequency
* Threshold
* Owner
* Escalation requirement

Metrics without accountability may not produce meaningful governance outcomes.

---

### 133. Principle of Governance Transparency

Governance decisions should be sufficiently transparent to authorized stakeholders.

Transparency should support understanding of:

* Why decisions were made
* Who made them
* What risks were considered
* What controls were required
* What conditions apply

Transparency should be balanced with confidentiality and security requirements.

---

### 134. Principle of Evidence-Based Governance

AI governance decisions should be supported by appropriate evidence.

Evidence may include:

* Risk assessments
* Security testing
* Privacy reviews
* Architecture assessments
* Vendor assessments
* Monitoring results
* Audit findings

Evidence should be relevant to the decision being made.

---

### 135. Principle of Traceable Governance Decisions

Material AI governance decisions should be traceable.

Organizations should be able to determine:

* What was decided
* Who approved it
* When it was approved
* What information supported the decision
* What conditions were imposed
* When reassessment is required

Traceability supports accountability and auditability.

---

### 136. Principle of Governance Record Retention

Governance records should be retained according to organizational, legal, regulatory, and business requirements.

Records may include:

* Approvals
* Risk assessments
* Exceptions
* Security assessments
* Privacy assessments
* Audit results
* Incident records

Retention periods should be appropriate to the nature and significance of the records.

---

### 137. Principle of Independent Assurance

Material AI governance processes should receive independent assurance appropriate to their risk.

Assurance may come from:

* Internal Audit
* Independent security assessments
* External auditors
* Specialized assessors
* Regulatory examinations

The level of assurance should reflect the potential impact of the AI system.

---

### 138. Principle of Auditability Across the Lifecycle

AI systems should remain auditable throughout their lifecycle.

Auditability should support examination of:

* Governance
* Risk
* Security
* Data
* Models
* Changes
* Access
* Incidents
* Retirement

Lifecycle auditability helps establish accountability over time.

---

### 139. Principle of Control Effectiveness

Having a documented control does not necessarily mean the control is effective.

Organizations should evaluate whether controls are:

* Properly designed
* Implemented
* Operating
* Monitored
* Maintained

Control effectiveness should be evaluated according to risk.

---

### 140. Principle of Remediation Accountability

Identified weaknesses should have clear remediation ownership.

Remediation records should identify:

* Finding
* Risk
* Owner
* Corrective action
* Target date
* Status
* Validation

High-risk findings should receive appropriate priority.

---

### 141. Principle of Timely Remediation

AI security weaknesses should be remediated within timeframes appropriate to their risk.

Factors may include:

* Severity
* Exploitability
* Business criticality
* Exposure
* Regulatory requirements

Risk acceptance may be required when remediation cannot occur within the defined timeframe.

---

### 142. Principle of Root-Cause Remediation

Organizations should address the underlying causes of recurring AI governance weaknesses.

Root causes may involve:

* Inadequate processes
* Weak architecture
* Poor training
* Insufficient ownership
* Inadequate technology
* Governance gaps

Fixing the immediate symptom without addressing the root cause may allow the problem to recur.

---

### 143. Principle of Lessons Learned

AI governance should incorporate lessons from:

* Incidents
* Near misses
* Audit findings
* Security assessments
* Regulatory findings
* Operational failures
* Successful implementations

Lessons should inform improvements to governance requirements.

---

### 144. Principle of Maturity Improvement

AI Security Governance should mature over time.

Maturity improvements may involve progression from:

**Ad hoc**

→ Informal and reactive practices.

**Repeatable**

→ Consistent processes.

**Defined**

→ Formal governance and standards.

**Managed**

→ Metrics, monitoring, and management oversight.

**Optimized**

→ Continuous improvement and proactive governance.

The objective is sustained improvement rather than achieving a maturity label for its own sake.

---

### 145. Principle of Governance Adaptability

AI governance should adapt as AI capabilities evolve.

Governance should be capable of addressing emerging technologies such as:

* Generative AI
* AI agents
* Multimodal systems
* Autonomous systems
* AI-assisted software development
* AI-enabled security operations

Governance frameworks should be adaptable without requiring complete redesign for every technological development.

---

### 146. Principle of Threat Adaptability

AI security controls should evolve in response to changing threats.

Threat-informed governance should account for developments involving:

* Prompt injection
* Model manipulation
* Data poisoning
* Model theft
* AI-enabled social engineering
* Automated attacks
* Agent exploitation

Threat intelligence should influence security priorities.

---

### 147. Principle of Technology-Neutral Governance

AI Security Governance principles should remain sufficiently technology-neutral to survive changes in implementation.

Governance should focus on:

* Risk
* Accountability
* Security
* Data
* Controls
* Decision rights
* Assurance

Detailed technical standards can then address specific platforms and technologies.

---

### 148. Principle of Vendor-Neutral Governance

Core governance requirements should not depend unnecessarily on a specific AI provider.

Organizations should establish requirements that apply regardless of whether AI is provided through:

* Cloud services
* SaaS
* Internal platforms
* Open-source models
* Commercial models
* Managed services

Vendor-specific requirements should supplement, rather than replace, enterprise governance principles.

---

### 149. Principle of Avoiding Governance Gaps

Organizations should identify areas where responsibility may fall between functions.

Potential gaps may occur between:

* AI and cybersecurity
* AI and privacy
* AI and data governance
* AI and enterprise risk
* AI and technology
* AI and procurement

Governance should explicitly assign responsibility for cross-functional risks.

---

### 150. Principle of Avoiding Governance Duplication

AI governance should avoid unnecessary duplication of existing control activities.

Where existing processes adequately address an AI risk, organizations should integrate AI requirements into those processes where practical.

Examples include:

* Existing access management
* Existing vulnerability management
* Existing incident response
* Existing third-party risk management
* Existing business continuity
* Existing internal audit

This promotes efficiency and consistency.

---

### 151. Principle of Governance Clarity

AI governance requirements should be understandable to the people expected to follow them.

Requirements should clearly communicate:

* What is required
* Who is responsible
* When it applies
* What evidence is needed
* What happens when requirements cannot be met

Ambiguous governance creates inconsistent implementation.

---

### 152. Principle of Governance Accessibility

AI governance resources should be accessible to relevant personnel.

Employees should be able to find:

* Policies
* Standards
* Procedures
* Approved AI services
* Reporting mechanisms
* Training
* Guidance

Governance that cannot be easily accessed is difficult to consistently implement.

---

### 153. Principle of Governance Usability

AI governance processes should be practical enough for employees and technical teams to use correctly.

Complex governance processes may encourage:

* Workarounds
* Shadow AI
* Unauthorized deployments
* Incomplete documentation

Usability should therefore be considered when designing governance controls.

---

### 154. Principle of Governance by Risk Tier

Governance requirements should be structured according to defined risk tiers.

For example:

**Low Risk**

→ Basic controls.

**Moderate Risk**

→ Formal assessment and additional safeguards.

**High Risk**

→ Multidisciplinary review and enhanced controls.

**Critical or Restricted**

→ Executive approval, intensive oversight, or prohibition where appropriate.

Risk tiers should be clearly defined.

---

### 155. Principle of Prohibited AI Activities

Organizations should identify AI activities that are prohibited or restricted.

Restrictions may apply where AI creates unacceptable:

* Security risk
* Privacy risk
* Legal risk
* Safety risk
* Ethical risk
* Regulatory exposure

Prohibited activities should be clearly communicated and enforced where appropriate.

---

### 156. Principle of Controlled Exceptions

Governance exceptions should be formally managed.

An exception process should identify:

* Requirement
* Reason
* Risk
* Compensating controls
* Approver
* Duration
* Review date

Exceptions should not become an informal method for bypassing governance.

---

### 157. Principle of Executive Sponsorship

Effective AI Security Governance requires visible executive support.

Executive leaders should:

* Establish expectations
* Allocate resources
* Resolve conflicts
* Support accountability
* Review material risks
* Promote responsible AI use

Without executive sponsorship, AI governance may remain fragmented.

---

### 158. Principle of Board Oversight

The Board should receive appropriate oversight information regarding material AI risks and opportunities.

Board-level reporting may address:

* Strategic AI exposure
* Significant cybersecurity risks
* Material incidents
* Regulatory developments
* Critical third-party dependencies
* AI governance maturity

Board involvement should reflect the organization's governance structure and risk profile.

---

### 159. Principle of Organizational Accountability

AI Security Governance is ultimately an enterprise responsibility.

No single function can manage every AI-related risk.

Effective governance requires coordinated participation from:

* Leadership
* Business functions
* AI teams
* Technology
* Cybersecurity
* Risk
* Privacy
* Legal
* Compliance
* Internal Audit
* Employees

Accountability should be distributed according to clearly defined responsibilities.

---

### 160. Principle of Sustainable AI Security Governance

AI Security Governance should be designed to remain effective as the organization's AI capabilities, risks, technologies, regulations, and business objectives evolve.

A sustainable governance model combines:

* Clear accountability
* Risk-based decision-making
* Security by design
* Lifecycle governance
* Resilience
* Human oversight
* Continuous monitoring
* Independent assurance
* Regulatory awareness
* Organizational learning
* Continuous improvement

The objective is not simply to control AI, but to establish a durable governance system that enables the organization to **adopt, operate, secure, monitor, and evolve AI capabilities while maintaining appropriate control over enterprise risk and accountability**.
