# 01.10 — AI Governance Operating Models

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Governance Operating Models

### 1. Introduction to AI Governance Operating Models

An **AI Governance Operating Model** defines how an organization organizes people, processes, decision rights, technology, controls, and oversight to govern artificial intelligence consistently across the enterprise.

AI governance requires more than policies and principles. An organization must determine:

* Who makes AI-related decisions
* Who owns AI risks
* Who operates governance processes
* Who provides oversight
* Who approves high-risk AI use
* Who monitors compliance
* Who performs assurance
* How governance activities are integrated into existing enterprise processes

The operating model provides the organizational structure through which AI governance becomes operational.

---

### 2. Purpose of an AI Governance Operating Model

The primary purpose of an AI governance operating model is to translate AI governance requirements into an executable organizational structure.

It establishes:

**Authority**

→ Who has the right to make decisions.

**Accountability**

→ Who is answerable for outcomes.

**Responsibility**

→ Who performs specific activities.

**Oversight**

→ Who challenges and monitors decisions.

**Assurance**

→ Who independently evaluates effectiveness.

Without these elements, an AI governance framework may remain theoretical.

---

### 3. AI Governance Framework vs AI Governance Operating Model

An AI governance framework and an AI governance operating model are related but different.

The **framework** establishes:

* Principles
* Policies
* Standards
* Requirements
* Control objectives
* Governance expectations

The **operating model** establishes:

* Organizational structure
* Roles
* Decision rights
* Processes
* Committees
* Escalation paths
* Reporting relationships

A framework defines **what should be governed**.

An operating model defines **how governance will actually operate**.

---

### 4. Why AI Requires an Operating Model

AI can affect many organizational functions simultaneously.

A single AI system may involve:

* Business operations
* IT
* Cybersecurity
* Data management
* Privacy
* Legal
* Compliance
* Procurement
* Risk management
* Internal audit

Without an operating model, these functions may make disconnected decisions.

An operating model establishes coordination across these stakeholders.

---

### 5. Enterprise AI Governance Operating Model

An enterprise AI governance operating model provides a common structure for governing AI across business units and technology environments.

It should support:

* Enterprise-wide consistency
* Risk-based decision-making
* Clear accountability
* Central oversight
* Business ownership
* Technical execution
* Independent assurance

The model should be sufficiently centralized to establish consistency while remaining sufficiently flexible to support different business requirements.

---

### 6. Centralized AI Governance Model

In a **centralized model**, AI governance authority and operational capabilities are concentrated within a central organizational function.

A centralized AI governance function may establish:

* Enterprise AI policies
* AI standards
* Risk methodology
* Approval processes
* AI inventory
* Governance tooling
* Central reporting
* Enterprise oversight

Business units operate within the centralized governance structure.

---

### 7. Advantages of a Centralized Model

A centralized model can provide:

* Consistent governance
* Standardized processes
* Centralized expertise
* Strong enterprise visibility
* Consistent risk classification
* Easier regulatory reporting
* Reduced duplication

It can be particularly useful when an organization is beginning to establish formal AI governance.

---

### 8. Limitations of a Centralized Model

A highly centralized model can also create challenges.

Potential issues include:

* Governance bottlenecks
* Slow approvals
* Limited business-unit flexibility
* Excessive dependence on a central team
* Difficulty scaling across large organizations

A centralized model should therefore avoid becoming a single administrative gatekeeper for every AI activity.

---

### 9. Decentralized AI Governance Model

In a **decentralized model**, business units or organizational divisions maintain significant responsibility for AI governance.

Each business unit may have its own:

* AI governance capability
* Risk management
* AI oversight
* Subject-matter expertise
* Approval processes

Enterprise-level functions establish minimum requirements and oversight.

---

### 10. Advantages of a Decentralized Model

A decentralized model can provide:

* Faster local decision-making
* Strong business alignment
* Domain-specific expertise
* Greater flexibility
* Better understanding of local operational risks

It may be suitable for organizations with highly independent business units.

---

### 11. Limitations of a Decentralized Model

Decentralization can create:

* Inconsistent controls
* Duplicate processes
* Fragmented AI inventories
* Different risk classifications
* Uneven governance maturity
* Difficulty obtaining enterprise-wide visibility

Strong enterprise standards are therefore important in decentralized environments.

---

### 12. Federated AI Governance Model

A **federated model** combines centralized governance with distributed execution.

A central AI governance function establishes:

* Policies
* Standards
* Risk taxonomy
* Minimum controls
* Enterprise reporting
* Governance methodology

Business units maintain responsibility for:

* AI use cases
* Local implementation
* Operational decisions
* Local risk management
* Business outcomes

This model is often appropriate for large enterprises with multiple business units.

---

### 13. Central Governance and Local Execution

A federated operating model can be represented as:

**Enterprise AI Governance**

↓

**Policies and Standards**

↓

**Enterprise Risk Methodology**

↓

**Business Unit Governance**

↓

**AI System Teams**

This structure establishes enterprise consistency while retaining operational flexibility.

---

### 14. Hybrid AI Governance Model

A hybrid model combines characteristics of centralized, decentralized, and federated governance.

For example:

**Centralized**

* Policy
* Standards
* Risk methodology
* Enterprise inventory
* Governance technology

**Distributed**

* Business ownership
* Technical implementation
* Operational monitoring
* Local risk management

**Independent**

* Internal audit
* Assurance
* Independent validation

Hybrid models can be designed according to organizational complexity and risk.

---

### 15. Selecting an AI Governance Operating Model

There is no universal operating model that is appropriate for every organization.

Selection should consider:

* Organizational size
* Business complexity
* Geographic distribution
* Regulatory environment
* AI maturity
* AI risk profile
* Number of AI systems
* Degree of AI adoption
* Existing GRC structure
* Existing technology governance

The operating model should reflect the organization's actual environment.

---

### 16. AI Governance Operating Model Design Principles

A governance operating model should follow several principles.

**Clarity**

Roles and responsibilities should be clearly defined.

**Accountability**

Decision ownership should be explicit.

**Proportionality**

Governance should reflect AI risk.

**Independence**

Oversight should have sufficient independence.

**Scalability**

The model should support increasing AI adoption.

**Integration**

AI governance should connect with existing enterprise processes.

**Transparency**

Important decisions should be documented and explainable.

* **Responsible**
* **Accountable**

* **Informed**

RACI mapping can be applied to:

* AI intake
* Risk assessment
* Security assessment
* Privacy assessment

* Deployment
* Monitoring
* Change
* Retirement

---

### 39. AI Governance Decision Rights Matrix

A decision-rights matrix should identify which organizational level can make specific AI decisions.

For example:

| Decision                 | System Owner | AI Governance | Risk | Security | Executive |
| ------------------------ | ------------ | ------------- | ---- | -------- | --------- |
| Low-risk use case        | A            | C             | I    | C        | I         |
| High-risk use case       | C            | R             | C    | C        | A         |
| Security exception       | C            | C             | C    | A        | I         |
| Material risk acceptance | C            | C             | R    | C        | A         |
| Major AI suspension      | R            | C             | C    | C        | A         |

The precise allocation should be adapted to the organization's governance structure.

---

### 40. AI Governance Operating Model Effectiveness

An operating model should ultimately be evaluated by whether it enables the organization to:

* Make timely AI decisions
* Maintain clear accountability
* Identify and manage AI risks
* Implement effective controls
* Provide independent oversight
* Scale AI responsibly
* Respond to incidents
* Adapt governance as AI evolves

An effective AI Governance Operating Model therefore connects **organizational authority, business ownership, technical execution, risk oversight, security, privacy, compliance, and independent assurance into one coordinated governance structure**.


## Part 2 — AI Governance Roles, Decision Rights and Organizational Coordination

### 41. AI Governance Role Architecture

An effective AI Governance Operating Model requires a defined role architecture.

The role architecture should identify:

* Executive accountability
* Governance authority
* Business ownership
* Technical ownership
* Risk ownership
* Security responsibilities
* Privacy responsibilities
* Data responsibilities
* Compliance responsibilities
* Independent assurance

Roles should be defined before governance processes are operationalized.

---

### 42. AI Governance Accountability Model

AI governance accountability should exist at multiple organizational levels.

**Board**

→ Oversight of significant AI-related risks and strategic exposure.

**Executive Management**

→ Accountability for enterprise AI strategy and governance.

**AI Governance Leadership**

→ Coordination of the enterprise AI governance capability.

**Business Leadership**

→ Accountability for AI use and business outcomes.

**AI System Owners**

→ Accountability for individual AI capabilities.

**Control Owners**

→ Accountability for specific governance controls.

**Internal Audit**

→ Independent assurance.

This layered model prevents AI accountability from becoming concentrated in one function.

---

### 43. Board-Level AI Governance

The board should understand the organization's material AI exposure.

Board oversight may include:

* AI strategy
* Significant AI risks
* Regulatory exposure
* Major AI incidents
* Risk appetite
* Governance maturity
* Critical third-party dependencies
* Material business reliance on AI

The board generally does not manage individual AI systems.

Its role is strategic oversight and challenge.

---

### 44. Executive Management AI Accountability

Executive management should ensure that AI governance is adequately supported through:

* Funding
* Resources
* Policies
* Organizational authority
* Technology capabilities
* Skilled personnel
* Risk management

Executive management should also ensure that material AI risks are appropriately escalated.

### 66. AI Governance and Autonomous Decision Rights

Organizations should define which decisions AI systems may make autonomously.

Possible categories include:

**Informational**

→ AI provides information only.

**Advisory**

→ AI recommends an action.

**Human-Approved**

→ AI prepares an action that requires approval.

**Delegated**

→ AI can execute predefined actions within limits.

**Autonomous**

→ AI executes actions without case-by-case human approval.

Governance requirements should increase with the level of autonomy and impact.

---

### 67. AI Governance Authority Boundaries

Governance authority should be clearly separated from technical capability.

An AI system may technically be capable of performing an action that it is not authorized to perform.

For example:

**Technical Capability**

→ Can access a database.

**Governance Authorization**

→ May access only specific approved records.

This distinction is fundamental to AI governance.

---

### 68. AI Governance Access Authority

Access to AI systems should be governed according to:

* Role
* Business need
* Data sensitivity
* System criticality
* Level of autonomy
* Privilege

Access should be reviewed periodically.

---

### 69. AI Governance Privileged Access

Privileged AI capabilities require enhanced governance.

Examples include AI systems capable of:

* Administrative actions
* Infrastructure changes
* Database modification
* Security configuration
* Financial transactions
* Production deployment

Privileged AI access should generally have stronger authorization, monitoring, and oversight.

---

### 70. AI Governance Delegation of Authority

AI governance should define what decisions may be delegated to lower organizational levels.

Delegation may depend on:

* Risk classification
* Business impact
* Regulatory requirements
* Financial impact
* Security impact
* Degree of autonomy

Delegation should never remove accountability.

---

### 71. AI Governance Escalation Rights

Governance participants should have the ability to escalate concerns without requiring permission from the team operating the AI system.

Escalation rights may apply to:

* Security concerns
* Privacy concerns
* Compliance issues
* Ethical concerns
* Model failures
* Control weaknesses

This supports effective challenge.

A mature operating model creates an environment where governance functions can challenge AI decisions.

Challenge may involve questions such as:

* Is the use case justified?
* Is the risk acceptable?
* Is the control sufficient?
* Is the model appropriate?
* Is the data authorized?
* Is human oversight adequate?

Challenge should be constructive and evidence-based.

* Separate reporting lines

* Standardization
* Reusable controls

* Approved platforms

* Distributed accountability

### 174. AI Governance Automation Strategy

Potential automation areas include:

* AI discovery
* Inventory updates
* Risk questionnaires
* Control checks
* Evidence collection
* Approval routing
* Monitoring
* Compliance reporting
* Review notifications

Human judgment should remain involved where risk decisions require contextual assessment.

Business and technology teams may access:

* Training materials

Self-service governance can accelerate responsible AI adoption.

### 176. AI Governance Governance-as-Code

Where appropriate, governance requirements can be expressed through technical policies and automated controls.

Examples include:

* Access policies
* Deployment gates
* Infrastructure policies
* Data policies
* Configuration controls
* Logging requirements

Governance-as-code can make selected governance requirements enforceable within technology environments.

### 177. AI Governance Policy-as-Code

Policies that can be translated into machine-enforceable rules may be implemented directly within technology platforms.

For example:

**Policy**

→ Sensitive data cannot be sent to an unauthorized AI service.

**Technical Enforcement**

→ Automated policy prevents the transfer.

Policy-as-code can reduce dependence on manual enforcement.

* Unexpected changes

This allows governance teams to respond more rapidly to emerging issues.

The operating model should continuously evolve based on:

* Incidents

* Governance metrics

### 180. AI Governance Operating Model Optimization

Optimization should seek to balance:

**Risk Control**

→ Preventing unacceptable AI exposure.

**Business Agility**

→ Enabling useful AI adoption.

**Operational Efficiency**

→ Avoiding unnecessary governance effort.

**Accountability**

→ Maintaining clear ownership.

**Assurance**

→ Providing confidence that controls work.

A mature operating model achieves this balance through proportional governance rather than through maximum control over every AI activity.
