# 01.13 — CISO, CIO, CTO, CRO, DPO and AI Leadership Responsibilities

## Part 1 — Executive Technology, Security, Risk, Privacy and AI Leadership

### 1. Introduction to AI Leadership Responsibilities

AI governance requires coordinated leadership across multiple executive and specialist functions.

AI does not belong exclusively to:

* Information technology
* Cybersecurity
* Data
* Risk
* Privacy
* Compliance
* Legal
* Business operations

Instead, AI creates responsibilities that cross organizational boundaries.

The **CISO, CIO, CTO, CRO, DPO, Chief Data Officer, Chief AI Officer where applicable, and business leadership** each contribute different forms of authority, expertise, and oversight.

---

### 2. Why Multiple Leadership Functions Are Required

AI systems combine several domains simultaneously.

A single AI system may involve:

* Business processes
* Software
* Infrastructure
* Data
* Models
* Cybersecurity
* Privacy
* Regulatory requirements
* Third-party services
* Human decision-making

No single executive function normally possesses all the expertise required to govern these dimensions.

---

### 3. AI Leadership as a Shared Responsibility

AI governance should therefore operate through **distributed but coordinated accountability**.

For example:

**CIO**

→ Technology strategy and enterprise IT.

**CTO**

→ Technology architecture, engineering, and technical innovation.

**CISO**

→ Cybersecurity and AI security.

**CRO**

→ Enterprise risk and risk governance.

**DPO**

→ Privacy and personal-data protection.

**AI Leadership**

→ AI strategy, capabilities, and AI operating model.

**Business Leadership**

→ Business outcomes and operational risk ownership.

The exact structure depends on the organization's size and operating model.

---

### 4. CISO Responsibilities in AI Governance

The Chief Information Security Officer is responsible for providing cybersecurity leadership across the organization's AI environment.

The CISO's responsibilities may include:

* AI cybersecurity strategy
* AI security policies
* Security risk assessment
* AI threat modeling
* Security architecture
* Security testing
* Monitoring
* AI incident response
* Security awareness

The CISO should integrate AI security into the broader cybersecurity program.

---

### 5. CISO and AI Security Strategy

The CISO should ensure that AI is incorporated into the organization's cybersecurity strategy.

The strategy may address:

* AI attack surfaces
* AI infrastructure
* Model security
* Data security
* Identity
* Access
* Application security
* Supply-chain security
* AI-enabled threats

AI security should evolve as AI capabilities and threats change.

---

### 6. CISO and AI Security Risk

The CISO should help identify and assess cybersecurity risks associated with AI.

These may include:

* Prompt injection
* Model manipulation
* Data poisoning
* Sensitive-data leakage
* Model extraction
* Unauthorized access
* AI-enabled attacks
* Insecure AI integrations

The CISO provides cybersecurity expertise, while business owners remain accountable for risks within their systems.

---

### 7. CISO and AI Threat Modeling

AI systems should be included in security threat modeling.

Threat modeling may consider:

* Assets
* Actors
* Trust boundaries
* Attack surfaces
* Attack paths
* Threat scenarios
* Security controls

AI-specific threats should be incorporated alongside traditional application and infrastructure threats.

---

### 8. CISO and AI Security Architecture

The CISO should establish security architecture expectations for AI environments.

These may address:

* Identity
* Authentication
* Authorization
* Network segmentation
* Secrets management
* Encryption
* API security
* Logging
* Monitoring

Security architecture should reflect the risk and criticality of the AI system.

---

### 9. CISO and AI Access Control

AI systems may require access to:

* Data
* Applications
* APIs
* Databases
* Cloud services
* Enterprise tools

The CISO should ensure that access is:

* Authorized
* Limited
* Monitored
* Reviewed
* Revoked when no longer required

AI agents with autonomous access may require additional controls.

---

### 10. CISO and Agentic AI Security

Agentic AI creates additional cybersecurity concerns because agents may act on behalf of users or organizations.

The CISO should consider:

* Tool permissions
* Privilege levels
* Action boundaries
* Credential management
* Human approval
* Transaction limits
* Monitoring
* Emergency shutdown

Autonomous capability should be matched with appropriate security controls.

---

### 11. CISO and AI Data Security

AI systems can process large amounts of organizational information.

Security governance should address:

* Data classification
* Data access
* Encryption
* Data loss prevention
* Data transfer
* Data retention
* Sensitive-data handling

The security requirements should correspond to the sensitivity and business importance of the data.

---

### 12. CISO and AI Security Testing

The CISO should establish expectations for security testing.

Testing may include:

* Vulnerability assessment
* Penetration testing
* Adversarial testing
* Prompt injection testing
* Access-control testing
* API testing
* Configuration assessment

Testing depth should correspond to the AI system's risk.

---

### 13. CISO and AI Security Monitoring

AI environments should be monitored for suspicious activity.

Monitoring may include:

* Authentication
* API activity
* Model access
* Prompt activity
* Data movement
* Privilege changes
* Unusual behavior

Monitoring should support both detection and investigation.

---

### 14. CISO and AI Incident Response

The CISO should ensure that AI-related security incidents are incorporated into incident-response processes.

Potential incidents include:

* AI data leakage
* Prompt injection compromise
* Model manipulation
* Unauthorized agent activity
* Credential abuse
* AI-enabled cyberattack

Response procedures should define escalation and containment responsibilities.

---

### 15. CISO and AI Security Incident Escalation

Significant AI security incidents should be escalated according to defined criteria.

Escalation may involve:

* Security leadership
* Business owners
* Risk
* Privacy
* Legal
* Executive management
* Regulators where required

The CISO should ensure that security-related escalation paths are clearly defined.

---

### 16. CISO and AI Third-Party Security

Third-party AI providers may create security dependencies.

The CISO should evaluate:

* Vendor security
* API security
* Data protection
* Authentication
* Encryption
* Logging
* Incident notification
* Supply-chain risk

Critical AI providers should receive appropriate security scrutiny.

---

### 17. CISO and AI Supply-Chain Security

AI supply chains may include:

* Foundation models
* Open-source libraries
* Datasets
* APIs
* Cloud infrastructure
* AI development tools
* Third-party components

The CISO should ensure that material security risks within these dependencies are assessed and managed.

---

### 18. CISO and Shadow AI

Unauthorized AI use can create cybersecurity exposure.

The CISO should help establish controls for:

* Unauthorized AI applications
* Sensitive-data submission
* Unapproved APIs
* Personal accounts
* Uncontrolled AI extensions

Security controls should be combined with employee education and approved alternatives.

---

* Enterprise IT strategy

This may include decisions concerning:

* Cloud
* Infrastructure
* Enterprise applications
* Data platforms
* AI platforms
* Integration
* Technology standards

AI should be integrated into the organization's overall technology roadmap.

---

### 21. CIO and AI Technology Governance

The CIO may establish technology governance requirements covering:

* Approved platforms
* Architecture
* Technology standards
* Integration
* Lifecycle management
* Technology risk
* Service management

These requirements should complement AI-specific governance.

---

### 22. CIO and AI Infrastructure

AI systems may require substantial infrastructure.

The CIO may oversee:

* Cloud infrastructure
* Computing resources
* Storage
* Networking
* Platforms
* Enterprise integration

Infrastructure decisions should account for security, availability, scalability, and cost.

### 23. CIO and AI Service Management

AI services should be incorporated into appropriate IT service-management processes.

These may include:

### 28. CTO and Emerging AI Technologies

The CTO may evaluate emerging technologies such as:

* Generative AI
* Multimodal AI
* Agentic AI
* AI agents

* New model architectures

Technology experimentation should occur within appropriate governance boundaries.

---

### 29. CTO and AI Innovation

The CTO may help identify opportunities where AI can improve:

* Products
* Services
* Operations
* Engineering
* Automation
* Customer experience

Innovation should be balanced with security, privacy, compliance, and risk considerations.

---

### 30. CTO and Technical AI Risk

The CTO should ensure that technical risks are understood before major AI deployment.

Technical risks may include:

* Architecture weaknesses
* Scalability
* Reliability
* Model limitations
* Integration dependencies
* Technical debt
* Vendor lock-in

Technical risk should be connected to broader enterprise risk management.

---

### 31. CRO Responsibilities in AI Governance

The Chief Risk Officer provides enterprise risk-management leadership.

The CRO may oversee:

* AI risk framework
* Risk taxonomy
* Risk appetite
* Risk reporting
* Risk aggregation
* Risk escalation
* Enterprise risk integration

The CRO should ensure that AI risk is incorporated into the organization's overall risk-management system.

---

### 32. CRO and AI Risk Taxonomy

The CRO may establish or integrate AI risks into the enterprise risk taxonomy.

Categories may include:

* Strategic risk
* Operational risk
* Cybersecurity risk
* Privacy risk
* Compliance risk
* Model risk
* Third-party risk
* Reputational risk
* Safety risk

This creates a common language for AI risk reporting.

---

### 33. CRO and AI Risk Appetite

The CRO may help translate enterprise risk appetite into AI-specific risk thresholds.

Examples include:

* Maximum acceptable high-risk AI exposure
* Limits on autonomous AI
* Third-party concentration thresholds
* Acceptable residual risk
* Escalation thresholds

Risk appetite should guide decisions rather than simply document them.

---

### 34. CRO and AI Risk Aggregation

The CRO can aggregate AI risks across business units.

This can identify:

* Concentrated exposure
* Common dependencies
* Repeated control failures
* Emerging enterprise-wide risks
* Correlated risks

Aggregation provides leadership with an enterprise view of AI risk.

---

### 35. CRO and AI Risk Reporting

The CRO may provide AI risk reporting to:

* Executive management
* Risk committees
* Board
* Audit Committee
* Other governance bodies

Reports should focus on material risks, trends, and decisions.

---

### 36. DPO Responsibilities in AI Governance

The Data Protection Officer provides specialized privacy oversight where AI processing involves personal data.

The DPO may provide guidance on:

* Privacy requirements
* Data protection
* Privacy risk
* Data-subject rights
* Privacy assessments
* Regulatory obligations

The DPO should maintain appropriate independence under applicable privacy frameworks.

---

### 37. DPO and AI Data Processing

The DPO may evaluate whether AI processing involves:

* Personal data
* Sensitive personal data
* Special categories of data where applicable
* Employee data
* Customer data
* Third-party data

The purpose is to determine applicable privacy obligations and risks.

---

### 38. DPO and AI Privacy Risk Assessment

The DPO may participate in privacy risk assessments for AI systems.

Assessment areas may include:

* Purpose
* Lawful basis where applicable
* Data minimization
* Transparency
* Retention
* Data subject rights
* Third-party processing
* International transfers

Privacy requirements should be considered during AI design rather than after deployment.

---

### 39. DPO and AI Transparency

The DPO may advise on transparency obligations relating to AI processing.

This may include explaining:

* What data is processed
* Why it is processed
* How AI is used
* Who receives the data
* Applicable rights

Transparency requirements depend on the applicable legal and regulatory framework.

---

### 40. DPO and AI Privacy Incident Management

Where AI causes a personal-data incident, the DPO should coordinate or advise on privacy-related response obligations.

This may include:

* Incident assessment
* Regulatory notification
* Data-subject communication
* Privacy remediation
* Documentation

Security and privacy incident response should operate together rather than independently.

## Part 2 — Functional Leadership Responsibilities for AI Governance

### 41. DPO and AI Data Governance

The DPO should work with data governance, legal, security, and business teams to ensure that personal data used by AI systems is appropriately governed.

* Data classification
* Data minimization
* Purpose limitation

### 42. DPO and AI Data Subject Rights

AI governance may need to address:

* Access requests
* Correction
* Deletion
* Restriction
* Objection
* Portability
* Other applicable rights

The DPO should help ensure that AI processes do not unintentionally prevent the organization from fulfilling applicable rights.

AI systems that influence or make decisions about individuals may require additional privacy governance.

The DPO should help assess:

* Degree of automation
* Human involvement

* Customer segmentation
* Fraud detection

The DPO should help determine applicable privacy requirements and associated risks.

* Subprocessors

* International transfers

* Access restrictions
* Retention controls
* Purpose limitation
* Privacy-enhancing technologies

## Part 3 — Cross-Functional Accountability, Decision Rights and Governance Coordination

### 81. The Need for Cross-Functional AI Governance

AI systems frequently cross traditional organizational boundaries.

A single AI initiative may involve:

* Business operations
* AI engineering
* Data management
* Information technology
* Cybersecurity
* Privacy
* Legal
* Compliance
* Enterprise risk
* Procurement
* Internal Audit

Consequently, effective AI governance requires coordinated responsibilities rather than isolated functional ownership.

---

### 82. Functional Accountability Versus Enterprise Accountability

Functional leaders are accountable for their respective areas of expertise.

However, AI risks can affect the entire organization.

For example:

* The **CISO** may identify a security vulnerability.
* The **DPO** may identify a privacy concern.
* The **CRO** may identify an enterprise risk.
* The **CTO** may identify an architectural weakness.
* The **business owner** may identify an operational impact.

These perspectives must be brought together before significant AI decisions are made.

---

### 83. The Principle of Shared Expertise

AI governance should distinguish between **accountability** and **expertise**.

A specialist function may provide advice, challenge, oversight, or assurance without becoming the ultimate owner of the business risk.

For example, the CISO may advise that an AI system has unacceptable cybersecurity exposure, while the business executive remains accountable for the decision to deploy, modify, restrict, or discontinue the system.

---

### 84. The Principle of Clear Accountability

Shared governance should never mean shared ambiguity.

Every material AI system should have clearly identified:

* Business owner
* Technical owner
* Security responsibility
* Privacy responsibility where applicable
* Risk ownership
* Governance authority
* Approval authority

The organization should be able to identify who is accountable for a decision without relying on informal relationships.

---

### 85. AI Governance Decision Rights

Decision rights define who has authority to make particular AI-related decisions.

They may cover:

* AI strategy
* Use-case approval
* Risk classification
* Development
* Production deployment
* Model changes
* Data usage
* Vendor selection
* Risk acceptance
* Exceptions
* Suspension
* Retirement

Decision rights should be documented and communicated.

---

### 86. AI Decision Authority Based on Risk

Decision authority should generally increase with AI risk.

A low-risk productivity assistant may require routine management approval.

A high-risk AI system affecting customers, employees, financial decisions, or critical operations may require review by multiple control functions and senior governance bodies.

Material enterprise risks may require executive or Board involvement.

---

### 87. AI Governance Escalation Framework

Organizations should establish defined escalation paths.

A simplified structure may be:

**Operational Issue**

→ Business or technical owner.

**Significant Risk**

→ Senior management and relevant control functions.

**High-Risk or Cross-Enterprise Issue**

→ AI governance committee or executive management.

**Material Enterprise Risk**

→ Executive leadership and, where appropriate, Board-level governance.

Escalation criteria should be defined before an incident or dispute occurs.

---

### 88. CISO Decision Rights

The CISO should have clearly defined authority regarding cybersecurity requirements.

This may include authority to:

* Establish security standards
* Require security assessments
* Require remediation
* Escalate unacceptable security risks
* Restrict insecure deployments within delegated authority
* Require compensating controls

The CISO should not normally become the business owner of every AI system.

---

### 89. CIO Decision Rights

The CIO may have authority concerning enterprise technology decisions.

This may include:

* Technology standards
* Enterprise platforms
* Infrastructure
* IT services
* Technology architecture
* Technology investment

AI technology decisions should remain coordinated with security, privacy, risk, and business requirements.

---

### 90. CTO Decision Rights

The CTO may have decision authority over technical architecture and engineering.

This may include:

* AI architecture
* Engineering standards
* Technical platforms
* Research
* Innovation
* Development practices

Technical authority should remain subject to enterprise governance and risk requirements.

---

### 91. CRO Decision Rights

The CRO may establish or oversee enterprise risk-management requirements.

This may include:

* Risk methodology
* Risk taxonomy
* Risk appetite implementation
* Risk reporting
* Risk escalation

The CRO should not automatically become the owner of every AI risk.

Business and operational leaders generally remain accountable for risks generated by their activities.

---

### 92. DPO Decision Rights

The DPO's authority depends on applicable privacy laws and the organization's governance structure.

The DPO may have responsibilities relating to:

* Privacy advice
* Privacy assessments
* Data protection requirements
* Monitoring
* Regulatory engagement
* Privacy incident response

The DPO should maintain the independence required by applicable law.

---

### 93. AI Leadership Decision Rights

AI leadership may have authority over:

* AI strategy
* AI portfolio
* AI standards
* AI operating model
* AI capability development
* AI investment recommendations

AI leadership should not override specialized legal, privacy, cybersecurity, or risk requirements.

---

### 94. Business Leadership Decision Rights

Business leadership should retain authority over business outcomes.

This may include:

* Business use-case selection
* Business requirements
* Operational deployment
* Business performance
* Business risk ownership

Business leaders should make decisions with appropriate specialist input.

---

### 95. The AI Governance Committee

An AI Governance Committee can provide a formal mechanism for cross-functional decision-making.

Membership may include:

* AI leadership
* CISO
* CIO
* CTO
* CRO
* DPO
* Legal
* Compliance
* Data leadership
* Business representatives

Membership should reflect the organization's risk profile and operating model.

---

### 96. AI Governance Committee Authority

The committee should have clearly defined authority.

It may:

* Review high-risk AI
* Approve governance standards
* Resolve cross-functional issues
* Review material exceptions
* Review significant incidents
* Monitor AI risk
* Escalate material matters

A committee without decision authority may become only a discussion forum.

---

### 97. AI Governance Committee Versus Management Accountability

The existence of an AI Governance Committee does not eliminate individual accountability.

The committee provides:

* Coordination
* Challenge
* Decision-making
* Escalation

Business and functional leaders remain accountable for executing decisions within their respective areas.

---

### 98. AI Governance Committee and Risk Acceptance

The committee may approve risk acceptance where authority has been delegated to it.

Risk acceptance should document:

* Risk
* Business justification
* Existing controls
* Residual risk
* Decision
* Approver
* Expiration
* Review date

Risks exceeding committee authority should be escalated.

---

### 99. AI Governance Committee and Exceptions

Exceptions should be reviewed using consistent criteria.

The committee may consider:

* Reason for exception
* Risk level
* Duration
* Compensating controls
* Business impact
* Alternatives

Repeated exceptions should trigger review of the underlying governance requirement.

---

### 100. AI Governance Committee and High-Risk AI

High-risk AI may require multidisciplinary review.

The committee may evaluate:

* Security
* Privacy
* Safety
* Legal
* Compliance
* Business impact
* Ethical considerations
* Operational resilience

The depth of review should correspond to potential harm and organizational exposure.

---

### 101. AI Governance Committee and AI Deployment

Production deployment of high-impact AI should require evidence that required governance activities have been completed.

Evidence may include:

* Risk assessment
* Security assessment
* Privacy assessment
* Testing
* Business approval
* Control implementation
* Monitoring plan

Deployment should not depend solely on technical readiness.

---

### 102. AI Governance Committee and Material AI Changes

Material changes may require renewed governance review.

Examples include:

* New foundation model
* Significant model version change
* New data source
* New business purpose
* New geographic deployment
* New autonomous capability
* New external integration

Change significance should be determined using defined criteria.

---

### 103. AI Governance Committee and AI Retirement

The committee may oversee retirement of material AI systems where appropriate.

Retirement considerations may include:

* Business impact
* Data retention
* Security
* Privacy
* Regulatory obligations
* Vendor termination
* Replacement capability

Retirement should be treated as a governance decision rather than simply a technical activity.

---

### 104. Conflict Between Business and Security

AI governance may produce conflicts between business objectives and security requirements.

For example:

**Business**

→ Wants rapid deployment.

**Security**

→ Identifies unacceptable exposure.

The organization should have a defined process for resolving the conflict rather than allowing informal pressure to determine the outcome.

---

### 105. Conflict Between AI Innovation and Risk

Innovation teams may seek rapid experimentation while risk functions require additional controls.

Effective governance should provide controlled mechanisms for experimentation, such as:

* Sandboxes
* Limited pilots
* Restricted data
* Controlled environments
* Time-limited approvals

This allows innovation without removing governance.

---

### 106. Conflict Between AI and Privacy Requirements

AI initiatives may require data that creates privacy concerns.

The organization should determine whether:

* The data is necessary
* The processing is permitted
* The purpose is appropriate
* Controls are sufficient
* Alternatives exist

Privacy requirements should be addressed before significant deployment.

---

### 107. Conflict Between AI and Regulatory Requirements

AI capabilities may create opportunities that conflict with regulatory obligations.

Leadership should establish processes for:

* Regulatory interpretation
* Legal review
* Compliance assessment
* Risk escalation
* Decision documentation

Business objectives should not automatically override regulatory obligations.

---

### 108. Conflict Between Technical and Business Requirements

Technical teams may identify limitations that conflict with business expectations.

Examples include:

* Insufficient model reliability
* Scalability limitations
* Security weaknesses
* Data-quality problems
* Integration limitations

Such conflicts should be resolved through formal governance rather than bypassing technical controls.

---

### 109. AI Governance and Segregation of Duties

AI governance should maintain appropriate separation between:

* Development
* Approval
* Deployment
* Monitoring
* Risk acceptance
* Assurance

The same individual should not control every stage of a high-risk AI lifecycle.

---

### 110. AI Governance and Independence

Certain functions require sufficient independence to challenge management.

Examples include:

* Risk
* Compliance
* Privacy
* Internal Audit

Their ability to provide independent challenge should not be compromised by operational ownership.

---

### 111. First-Line AI Responsibilities

The first line generally consists of business and operational functions that own and manage AI activities.

Responsibilities include:

* Operating AI systems
* Identifying risks
* Implementing controls
* Monitoring
* Reporting incidents
* Remediating issues

The first line owns the risks generated by its activities.

---

### 112. Second-Line AI Responsibilities

Second-line functions provide oversight, challenge, guidance, and monitoring.

They may include:

* Risk
* Compliance
* Privacy
* Cybersecurity governance
* AI governance

The second line does not normally operate the business process it oversees.

---

### 113. Third-Line AI Responsibilities

Internal Audit provides independent assurance.

It may evaluate whether:

* Governance operates effectively
* Risks are properly managed
* Controls are appropriately designed
* Controls operate effectively
* Management actions address identified weaknesses

Internal Audit should not assume management responsibility for AI governance.

---

### 114. CISO and the Three Lines Model

The CISO's position may vary depending on organizational structure.

Cybersecurity can contain both:

* First-line security responsibilities
* Second-line security governance responsibilities

The organization should clearly define which activities belong to operational security and which represent independent oversight.

---

### 115. CRO and the Three Lines Model

The CRO and enterprise risk function generally provide second-line risk oversight.

Their responsibilities may include:

* Risk methodology
* Risk challenge
* Risk aggregation
* Risk reporting
* Risk appetite monitoring

Business management remains responsible for managing risks within its operations.

---

### 116. DPO and the Three Lines Model

The DPO's role does not necessarily fit neatly into a generic three-lines structure.

Privacy responsibilities depend on applicable legal requirements and organizational arrangements.

The DPO may provide:

* Independent advice
* Monitoring
* Privacy assessments
* Regulatory engagement

Operational teams remain responsible for implementing privacy requirements.

---

### 117. AI Leadership and the Three Lines Model

AI leadership may operate primarily as a first-line or coordinating function depending on the organization's structure.

It may:

* Develop AI capabilities
* Manage AI programs
* Coordinate governance
* Define AI standards

Its exact position should be documented to prevent conflicts of interest.

---

### 118. Cross-Functional AI Risk Assessment

Material AI systems should receive multidisciplinary risk assessment where appropriate.

Participants may include:

* Business
* AI
* Technology
* Security
* Privacy
* Risk
* Legal
* Compliance

The assessment should produce an integrated view of AI risk.

---

### 119. Cross-Functional AI Approval

Approval for high-risk AI should be based on evidence from relevant functions.

Approval may require confirmation that:

* Business objectives are valid
* Risks are understood
* Security controls are sufficient
* Privacy requirements are addressed
* Legal requirements are satisfied
* Monitoring is established

No single function should approve matters outside its authority.

---

### 120. Cross-Functional Accountability for AI Governance

Effective AI governance depends on a clear relationship between **business ownership, functional expertise, independent oversight, and executive authority**.

The **CISO** protects against cybersecurity threats.

The **CIO and CTO** govern technology and technical architecture.

The **CRO** provides enterprise risk oversight.

The **DPO** provides privacy expertise and oversight.

**AI leadership** coordinates AI strategy and capabilities.

**Business leadership** remains accountable for business outcomes and operational risks.

Together, these functions create a coordinated governance structure capable of addressing the multidimensional risks created by enterprise AI.

* Legal

Business leaders should determine:

* What business problem it addresses
* What outcomes are expected
* What business risks are acceptable
* Whether the AI system continues to provide value

Specialist functions provide expertise and challenge, but business ownership should not disappear.

* AI security architecture
* Threat modeling
* Security testing
* Identity and access
* Data protection
* Monitoring
* Incident response

The CIO may govern:

* Enterprise platforms
* IT services
* Infrastructure
* Technology investment
* Technology operations
* Enterprise architecture

### 125. CTO as Technical Innovation Authority

The CTO may provide leadership for:

* AI engineering
* Technical architecture
* Emerging technology
* Research
* Innovation
* Development standards

### 126. CRO as Enterprise Risk Authority

The CRO may oversee:

* AI risk taxonomy
* Risk assessment methodology
* Risk appetite
* Risk aggregation
* Risk reporting
* Risk escalation

The CRO helps ensure that AI risk is treated consistently with other enterprise risks.

### 127. DPO as Privacy Authority

The DPO provides specialized privacy leadership where AI involves personal data.

The DPO may advise on:

* Privacy risk
* Data protection
* Data-subject rights
* Automated decision-making
* Privacy assessments
* Regulatory obligations

The DPO's responsibilities should remain consistent with applicable privacy law and independence requirements.

---

### 128. AI Leadership as Enterprise AI Coordinator

Where a dedicated AI leadership role exists, it can provide coordination across AI strategy and execution.

Responsibilities may include:

* AI strategy
* AI portfolio
* AI operating model
* AI standards
* AI capability development
* AI literacy

AI leadership should coordinate with established control functions rather than replace them.

### 129. Data Leadership and AI Governance

Data leadership provides the foundation for trustworthy AI data practices.

Responsibilities may include:

* Data quality
* Data ownership
* Data lineage
* Data classification
* Data architecture
* Data governance

AI governance should recognize that poor data governance can become an AI governance problem.

### 130. Legal Leadership and AI Governance

Legal functions provide advice concerning:

* Contracts
* Intellectual property
* Liability
* Regulatory requirements
* Litigation
* Vendor agreements
* Terms of service

Legal review should be proportionate to the AI system's risk and intended use.

### 131. Compliance Leadership and AI Governance

Compliance functions help determine whether AI activities satisfy applicable obligations.

They may support:

* Regulatory mapping
* Compliance assessments
* Control requirements
* Regulatory monitoring
* Compliance reporting

### 132. Procurement Leadership and AI Governance

Procurement should incorporate AI governance requirements into acquisition processes.

Vendor evaluation may include:

* Security
* Privacy
* Risk
* Contractual requirements
* Data processing
* Service availability
* Exit provisions

AI procurement should not bypass governance because a business unit urgently wants access to a capability.

### 133. Internal Audit and Executive AI Governance

AI audit coverage may include:

* Governance
* Risk management
* Security
* Privacy
* Data governance
* Third-party risk
* Lifecycle management
* Control effectiveness

Internal Audit should remain independent from AI operational ownership.

* User training

A high-risk autonomous system may require:

* Formal risk assessment
* Security testing
* Privacy assessment
* Human oversight
* Executive approval
* Continuous monitoring

Governance should be proportional to risk.

* Review date

* AI incidents

* Exceptions
* Unauthorized AI
* Regulatory changes
* Vendor issues

### 142. AI Governance Review

AI governance decisions should be periodically reassessed.

Review triggers may include:

* Significant model changes
* New data
* New use cases
* New regulations
* New threats
* Material incidents
* Changes in business criticality

AI governance should therefore remain dynamic.

### 143. AI Governance and Continuous Risk Assessment

AI risk is not static.

Risk can change because:

* Models evolve
* Threats change
* Data changes
* User populations expand
* Integrations increase
* Regulations change
* AI capabilities improve

Leadership should ensure that risk assessments remain current.

### 144. AI Governance and Emerging Technology

Leadership should establish mechanisms for evaluating emerging technologies such as:

* Generative AI
* Multimodal AI
* AI agents
* Autonomous systems
* AI-generated code
* Advanced reasoning systems

Governance should be capable of responding before widespread deployment.

### 145. AI Governance and Organizational Change

AI adoption may change:

* Roles
* Processes
* Skills
* Decision-making
* Workforce requirements
* Operating models

Leadership should consider these organizational effects when approving major AI initiatives.

---

### 146. AI Governance and Workforce Accountability

AI may change how employees perform their responsibilities.

Leadership should ensure that:

* Roles remain clearly defined
* Human accountability remains established
* Employees understand AI limitations
* AI-generated outputs are appropriately reviewed
* Workforce changes are managed responsibly

Automation should not create accountability gaps.

---

### 147. AI Governance and Executive Communication

Executive leaders should communicate clear expectations regarding AI use.

Communications should reinforce:

* Approved use
* Security
* Privacy
* Responsible AI
* Risk escalation
* Human accountability

Employees should understand that AI governance is part of normal organizational behavior.

### 148. AI Governance and Organizational Culture

Leadership should support an environment where employees can raise concerns about:

* Unsafe AI
* Security weaknesses
* Privacy issues
* Bias
* Unauthorized use
* Unexpected AI behavior

Effective governance depends on both formal controls and organizational behavior.

### 149. AI Governance and Accountability Without Blame

AI incidents should be investigated to identify:

* Root causes
* Control failures
* Process weaknesses
* Governance gaps
* Human factors

The objective should be accountability and improvement rather than automatically assigning blame to individuals.

### 150. AI Governance and Management Information

→ Material enterprise risks and strategic issues.

**Executives**

→ Enterprise performance, risk, investment, and governance.

**Functional Leaders**

→ Domain-specific risks and controls.

**Operational Management**

→ Detailed system-level information.

Information should become more detailed as it moves closer to operational execution.

### 151. AI Governance Metrics by Leadership Level

Different leadership levels require different metrics.

**Board Metrics**

* Material AI risks
* Major incidents
* Regulatory exposure
* Strategic AI investments

**Executive Metrics**

* Risk trends

* Investment performance

### 152. AI Governance Reporting Frequency

Reporting frequency should reflect risk.

Low-risk AI may be reviewed periodically.

High-risk AI may require:

* More frequent monitoring
* Regular management reporting
* Defined escalation thresholds
* Immediate incident notification

Critical AI systems may require continuous monitoring.

---

### 153. AI Governance Escalation Thresholds

Escalation thresholds should be measurable wherever practical.

Examples include:

* Risk appetite breach
* Critical security vulnerability
* Material privacy incident
* Significant service disruption
* Major regulatory concern
* Significant financial impact

Clearly defined thresholds reduce delays in escalation.

---

### 154. AI Governance and Risk Acceptance Boundaries

Each leadership level should understand the risks it is authorized to accept.

For example:

**Operational Management**

→ Routine operational risks.

**Senior Management**

→ Significant business risks within delegated authority.

**Executive Management**

→ Material enterprise risks within executive authority.

**Board**

→ Matters requiring Board oversight or approval.

The exact thresholds should be established by organizational policy.

---

### 155. AI Governance and Accountability Documentation

Organizations should maintain records showing:

* Who owns an AI system
* Who approved it
* Who assessed the risk
* Who accepted residual risk
* Who monitors it
* Who can suspend it
* Who approves material changes

Clear documentation makes accountability operational rather than theoretical.

### 156. AI Governance and Assurance Mapping

Leadership should understand which risks are covered by assurance activities.

Assurance mapping can identify:

* Covered risks
* Control testing
* Audit coverage
* Monitoring
* Independent assessments
* Assurance gaps

This helps prevent significant AI risks from remaining unexamined.

---

### 157. AI Governance and Management Accountability

Specialist functions should advise, challenge, and monitor, but management should retain responsibility for operating the business.

This distinction is essential.

The CISO does not become responsible for every AI business outcome.

The CRO does not own every operational risk.

The DPO does not become responsible for every business process involving personal data.

AI leadership does not automatically own every AI-related risk.

Accountability should remain with the appropriate business and operational owners.

### 158. AI Governance and Executive Coordination

Executives should maintain mechanisms for resolving conflicts between functions.

Examples include conflicts involving:

* Security versus speed
* Privacy versus data availability
* Innovation versus control
* Cost versus resilience
* Business value versus regulatory requirements

Defined escalation mechanisms prevent these conflicts from being resolved informally.

Ultimately, AI governance should create a clear chain of accountability:

**Executive Management**

**AI Leadership**

**CIO / CTO**

**CISO**

→ Oversees enterprise risk.

**DPO**

**Operational Teams**

**Internal Audit**

→ Provides independent assurance.

### 160. Integrated AI Leadership Model

An effective AI governance structure does not depend on a single executive owning everything related to AI.

Instead, it establishes **clear accountability supported by specialized expertise, independent challenge, defined decision rights, coordinated escalation, and continuous oversight**.

The CISO, CIO, CTO, CRO, DPO, AI leadership, business leadership, and assurance functions must operate as connected components of the enterprise governance system while preserving their respective responsibilities and independence.
