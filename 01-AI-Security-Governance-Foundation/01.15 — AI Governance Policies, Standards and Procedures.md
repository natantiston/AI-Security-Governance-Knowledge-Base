# 01.15 — AI Governance Policies, Standards and Procedures

## Part 1 — Governance Documentation Hierarchy and Policy Foundations

### 1. Introduction to AI Governance Policies, Standards and Procedures

AI Security Governance principles establish the fundamental expectations for governing AI. Those principles must then be translated into formal organizational requirements that employees, technology teams, management, and control functions can consistently apply.

The primary governance instruments are:

* Policies
* Standards
* Procedures
* Guidelines
* Frameworks
* Control requirements
* Governance records

Together, these instruments transform high-level governance expectations into operational requirements.

---

### 2. Purpose of AI Governance Documentation

AI governance documentation provides a structured mechanism for communicating:

* What the organization expects
* What is mandatory
* Who is responsible
* What controls must exist
* How activities should be performed
* What evidence must be retained
* How exceptions are managed

Documentation also provides a foundation for:

* Accountability
* Consistency
* Training
* Compliance
* Audit
* Risk management
* Continuous improvement

---

### 3. Governance Documentation Hierarchy

A typical hierarchy can be represented as:

**Governance Principles**

→ Establish fundamental direction.

**Policies**

→ Establish mandatory organizational requirements.

**Standards**

→ Define specific mandatory requirements.

**Procedures**

→ Define how requirements are performed.

**Guidelines**

→ Provide recommended practices.

**Work Instructions**

→ Provide detailed operational steps.

This hierarchy allows organizations to move from strategic intent to operational execution.

---

### 4. AI Governance Principles Versus Policies

A principle describes what the organization fundamentally believes or expects.

For example:

> AI systems should be governed according to their level of risk.

A policy converts that expectation into an organizational requirement.

For example:

> All AI systems classified as high risk must undergo a documented risk assessment before production deployment.

The principle provides direction; the policy creates an enforceable organizational requirement.

---

### 5. AI Governance Policy

An AI governance policy is a formal statement of organizational requirements concerning the management and use of AI.

A policy typically establishes:

* Scope
* Objectives
* Responsibilities
* Mandatory requirements
* Governance authority
* Compliance expectations
* Exceptions
* Enforcement

Policies should be approved by an appropriately authorized governance body.

---

* Data protection

* AI application security
* Monitoring
* Incident response
* Third-party AI security
* Vulnerability management

The AI Security Policy should align with the broader information security policy framework.

---

It may address:

* AI strategy

* Security

* Compliance

Supporting policies can then address specialized areas.

---

* Employees

* AI developers
* Technology teams
* Third parties

* AI models
* AI agents
* Data
* Infrastructure

The policy should clearly identify where its requirements apply.

---

### 9. Policy Objectives

AI governance policies should establish clear objectives.

Objectives may include:

* Protecting AI systems
* Managing AI risk
* Establishing accountability
* Protecting data
* Supporting regulatory compliance
* Preventing unauthorized AI use
* Establishing lifecycle controls
* Supporting secure AI adoption

Objectives should be consistent with organizational strategy.

---

### 10. Policy Authority

Every policy should have an identified authority.

This may include:

* Board
* Executive management
* CIO
* CISO
* AI Governance Committee
* Risk Committee
* Other delegated authority

The approval authority should correspond to the policy's organizational importance.

---

### 11. Policy Ownership

Every AI governance policy should have an accountable owner.

The policy owner is responsible for:

* Maintaining the policy
* Coordinating reviews
* Managing updates
* Addressing questions
* Monitoring compliance
* Coordinating approvals

Ownership should be distinct from the authority that formally approves the policy where appropriate.

---

### 12. Policy Review

AI governance policies should be reviewed periodically.

Reviews may be triggered by:

* Regulatory changes
* Significant AI developments
* Security incidents
* Audit findings
* Organizational changes
* Changes in risk appetite
* New business requirements

Review frequency should be defined according to organizational needs and risk.

---

### 13. Policy Version Control

Policies should be version controlled.

A policy record may include:

* Policy name
* Version
* Owner
* Approval authority
* Effective date
* Review date
* Change history
* Classification

Version control helps establish which requirements were applicable at a particular time.

---

### 14. Policy Effective Date

Policies should identify when their requirements become effective.

This is important when:

* New requirements are introduced
* Existing requirements change
* Transition periods are provided
* Systems need time for remediation

Effective dates should be communicated to affected stakeholders.

---

### 15. Policy Review Date

Policies should have a defined review date.

The review should determine whether:

* The policy remains relevant
* Requirements remain appropriate
* Regulatory changes occurred
* Technology changed
* Risks changed
* Responsibilities changed

A review date should not necessarily imply that the policy expires automatically.

---

### 16. Policy Exception Management

Policies should define how exceptions are handled.

An exception process should identify:

* Who can request an exception
* What information is required
* Who assesses the risk
* Who can approve the exception
* How long it remains valid
* What compensating controls apply
* When it must be reviewed

Exceptions should be formally documented.

---

### 17. Policy Enforcement

AI governance policies should define consequences for non-compliance where appropriate.

Enforcement mechanisms may include:

* Corrective action
* Access restriction
* Remediation
* Escalation
* Disciplinary processes
* Contractual remedies

Enforcement should be consistent with organizational policies and applicable law.

---

### 18. Policy Communication

Policies should be communicated to the people who are expected to comply with them.

Communication may use:

* Corporate portals
* Training
* Awareness campaigns
* Management briefings
* Technical documentation
* Onboarding programs

Publishing a policy without communicating it may result in weak adoption.

### 19. Policy Acknowledgment

For certain AI governance policies, organizations may require personnel to acknowledge that they have:

* Received the policy
* Read the policy
* Understood their responsibilities
* Agreed to comply

Acknowledgment requirements should be proportionate to the nature of the policy.

* What evidence is created?

* What happens when requirements cannot be met?

7. Obtain appropriate approval.

### 40. Relationship Between Policies, Standards and Procedures

The relationship can be represented as:

**Policy**

→ Establishes **what the organization requires**.

**Standard**

→ Establishes **what specific requirements must be satisfied**.

**Procedure**

→ Establishes **how those requirements are implemented**.

**Work Instruction**

→ Establishes **the detailed operational steps**.

Together, they create a governance chain from organizational intent to practical execution.

## Part 2 — Policy Structure, Standards Development and Governance Requirements

### 41. AI Governance Policy Structure

A well-designed AI governance policy should follow a consistent structure.

A typical structure may include:

* Policy title
* Purpose
* Scope
* Definitions
* Policy statements
* Roles and responsibilities
* Governance requirements
* Compliance requirements
* Exceptions
* Enforcement
* Review requirements
* References

A consistent structure makes policies easier to understand, maintain, and audit.

---

### 42. Policy Purpose Statement

The purpose section should explain why the policy exists.

For example, an AI Security Governance Policy may seek to:

* Establish accountability
* Protect AI systems
* Manage AI-related risk
* Support secure AI adoption
* Protect organizational data
* Meet applicable obligations

The purpose should be concise while clearly explaining the policy's governance objective.

---

### 43. Policy Scope Statement

The scope should identify who and what the policy covers.

It may include:

* Employees
* Contractors
* Third parties
* Business units
* AI systems
* AI applications
* AI models
* AI agents
* AI services
* Supporting infrastructure

Scope should also clarify whether externally hosted or third-party AI services are included.

---

### 44. AI Governance Definitions

AI governance documentation should define important terminology.

Definitions may include:

* Artificial intelligence
* AI system
* AI model
* AI agent
* Generative AI
* High-risk AI
* AI owner
* AI risk owner
* AI provider
* AI incident
* AI asset

Definitions should be consistent across related policies and standards.

---

### 45. Policy Statements

Policy statements should express mandatory organizational requirements.

Examples include:

> AI systems must have an identified business owner.

> High-risk AI systems must undergo appropriate risk assessment before deployment.

> AI systems must comply with applicable security and privacy requirements.

Policy statements should be sufficiently clear to determine whether compliance has been achieved.

---

### 46. Avoiding Ambiguous Policy Language

Policies should avoid vague statements such as:

* "AI should be secure."
* "AI should be appropriately governed."
* "Users should take reasonable precautions."

Such statements may be difficult to enforce or audit.

Where appropriate, requirements should identify:

* Obligation
* Responsible party
* Applicability
* Expected outcome
* Evidence

---

### 47. Mandatory Versus Advisory Language

Governance documentation should distinguish between mandatory and advisory requirements.

Common distinctions include:

**Must / Shall**

→ Mandatory requirement.

**Should**

→ Expected practice with defined justification for deviation.

**May**

→ Permitted option.

**Should not**

→ Strongly discouraged practice.

**Must not / Shall not**

→ Prohibited activity.

Consistent terminology reduces interpretation differences.

---

### 48. Policy Roles and Responsibilities

Policies should define responsibilities for relevant functions.

Responsibilities may be assigned to:

* Board
* Executive management
* AI leadership
* CISO
* CIO
* CTO
* CRO
* DPO
* Business owners
* Technical owners
* Employees
* Third parties
* Internal Audit

Responsibilities should align with the organization's governance model.

---

### 49. Policy Accountability

Policy requirements should have accountable owners.

For each major requirement, organizations should be able to identify:

* Accountable party
* Responsible party
* Supporting functions
* Approval authority
* Assurance function

This prevents policy requirements from becoming ownerless obligations.

---

### 50. Policy Authority Matrix

A policy may be supported by an authority matrix showing who can:

* Approve
* Reject
* Escalate
* Accept risk
* Grant exceptions
* Suspend AI systems
* Approve material changes

Authority should be aligned with organizational delegation.

---

### 51. AI Governance RACI

A RACI model can clarify responsibilities.

**R — Responsible**

Performs the activity.

**A — Accountable**

Owns the outcome.

**C — Consulted**

Provides expertise.

**I — Informed**

Receives relevant information.

A RACI model can be particularly useful for cross-functional AI governance.

---

### 52. Policy-to-Control Mapping

AI governance policies should be mapped to relevant controls.

For example:

**Policy Requirement**

→ AI systems must implement appropriate access control.

**Control**

→ Role-based access is implemented.

**Evidence**

→ Access-control configuration and review records.

This creates traceability from governance intent to operational control.

---

### 53. Policy-to-Risk Mapping

Policies should also be connected to the risks they are intended to address.

For example:

**Risk**

→ Unauthorized access to sensitive AI data.

**Policy Requirement**

→ Sensitive AI data must be protected through approved access controls.

**Control**

→ Least-privilege access.

**Evidence**

→ Access review records.

Risk mapping helps demonstrate why a requirement exists.

---

### 54. Policy-to-Regulation Mapping

Where applicable, governance requirements should be mapped to regulatory obligations.

Mapping may identify:

* Regulation
* Requirement
* Policy statement
* Control
* Evidence
* Responsible owner

This supports compliance management and regulatory change analysis.

---

### 55. Policy-to-Framework Mapping

Organizations may also map AI governance policies to recognized frameworks and standards.

Potential reference sources include:

* NIST AI Risk Management Framework
* NIST Cybersecurity Framework
* ISO/IEC 27001
* ISO/IEC 42001
* ISO/IEC 23894
* CIS Controls
* COBIT
* Other applicable frameworks

Framework mapping should support governance rather than become an exercise in documentation alone.

---

### 56. Avoiding Framework Overlap

Multiple frameworks may contain overlapping requirements.

Organizations should avoid creating separate controls for every framework when one control can satisfy multiple requirements.

A single enterprise control may support:

* AI governance
* Cybersecurity
* Privacy
* Risk management
* Regulatory compliance

This reduces duplication.

---

### 57. AI Governance Control Objectives

Control objectives describe what a governance control is intended to achieve.

Examples include:

* AI systems are authorized before deployment.
* AI access is appropriately restricted.
* AI changes are controlled.
* AI risks are periodically reassessed.
* AI incidents are detected and managed.

Control objectives should connect policy requirements to measurable outcomes.

---

### 58. AI Governance Control Requirements

Control requirements translate objectives into specific expectations.

For example:

**Objective**

→ AI production access is appropriately controlled.

**Requirement**

→ Production AI systems must use approved identity and access-management mechanisms.

The requirement should be sufficiently precise to support implementation and testing.

---

### 59. AI Governance Evidence Requirements

Policies and standards should identify where evidence is necessary.

Evidence may include:

* Approval records
* Risk assessments
* Test results
* Access reviews
* Logs
* Monitoring reports
* Training records
* Exception approvals

Evidence requirements should be proportionate to risk.

The process may include:

1. Identify governance requirement.
2. Determine risk.
3. Identify applicable regulations.
4. Review existing controls.
5. Define standard requirements.
6. Consult stakeholders.
7. Obtain approval.
8. Publish the standard.
9. Monitor implementation.
10. Review periodically.

Each standard should have an accountable owner.

Possible owners include:

* CISO
* CIO
* CTO
* CRO
* DPO
* AI governance function

### 68. AI Governance Procedures

A procedure should normally define:

* Trigger
* Inputs
* Responsible roles
* Activities
* Decision points
* Outputs
* Evidence
* Escalation

### 69. Procedure Inputs

For an AI risk assessment, inputs may include:

* AI system description
* Data classification
* Model information
* Architecture

### 70. Procedure Activities

Procedures should define the activities necessary to achieve the desired governance outcome.

Activities may include:

* Review
* Assessment
* Testing
* Validation
* Approval
* Documentation

Activities should be ordered logically.

---

### 71. Procedure Decision Points

Procedures should identify decisions that require judgment or escalation.

For example:

Decision points should be documented clearly.

---

### 72. Procedure Outputs

Each governance procedure should define its expected outputs.

Examples include:

* Risk assessment
* Approval record
* Security assessment
* Privacy assessment

* Deployment authorization

### 73. Procedure Evidence

Procedures should specify what evidence must be produced.

Evidence should demonstrate:

* Activity occurred
* Appropriate person performed it
* Required decisions were made
* Required controls were considered

---

### 74. Procedure Escalation

Procedures should establish when issues must be escalated.

Escalation may be required when:

* Risk exceeds tolerance
* A control fails
* A material incident occurs
* A regulatory concern exists
* A high-risk AI system is identified
* A required approval cannot be obtained

Escalation paths should identify the appropriate decision authority.

---

### 75. Procedure Performance Measurement

Organizations should monitor whether governance procedures operate effectively.

Measures may include:

* Processing time
* Assessment completion
* Approval delays
* Exception volume
* Rework
* Control failures
* SLA compliance

Process performance should be balanced against governance quality.

---

### 76. AI Governance Workflow Standardization

Common AI governance workflows should be standardized where appropriate.

Examples include:

* AI intake
* AI risk assessment
* AI security assessment
* AI approval
* AI change management
* AI incident management
* AI retirement

Standard workflows improve consistency across business units.

---

### 77. Governance Documentation as a Control

Policies, standards, and procedures are themselves governance controls.

Effective documentation can:

* Establish expectations
* Assign responsibility
* Define boundaries
* Support compliance
* Enable assurance
* Reduce ambiguity

However, documentation alone does not prove that controls operate effectively.

---

### 78. Documentation Versus Implementation

An organization may have an excellent AI policy while still having weak governance.

For example:

**Documented requirement**

→ High-risk AI must undergo security assessment.

**Actual environment**

→ High-risk AI is deployed without assessment.

The existence of the policy does not demonstrate effective governance.

---

### 79. Documentation Versus Control Effectiveness

Governance maturity requires three related elements:

**Defined**

→ Requirement exists.

**Implemented**

→ Requirement is actually applied.

**Effective**

→ Requirement produces the intended result.

AI governance assessments should distinguish among these conditions.

---

### 80. Integrated Policy, Standard and Procedure Architecture

A mature AI Security Governance documentation architecture creates a clear chain:

**AI Governance Principles**

→ Define fundamental expectations.

**AI Governance Policies**

→ Establish mandatory organizational requirements.

**AI Governance Standards**

→ Define specific mandatory controls and requirements.

**AI Governance Procedures**

→ Define repeatable implementation processes.

**Work Instructions**

→ Provide detailed operational execution.

**Controls and Evidence**

→ Demonstrate that requirements are implemented and operating.

This architecture creates traceability from **governance intent to operational execution and assurance**, allowing the organization to manage AI consistently across its lifecycle.

## Part 3 — AI Governance Policy Domains and Control Requirements

### 81. AI Acceptable Use Policy

An AI Acceptable Use Policy establishes what employees and other authorized users may and may not do with AI technologies.

It may address:

* Approved AI services
* Prohibited AI services
* Business use of public AI platforms
* Personal use of AI tools
* Sensitive information
* Confidential information
* Intellectual property
* Customer information
* AI-generated content
* Human review
* Security requirements

The policy should distinguish between legitimate business use and activities that create unacceptable organizational risk.

---

### 82. Shadow AI Policy

A Shadow AI Policy addresses the use of AI technologies that have not been approved or assessed by the organization.

Shadow AI can occur when employees:

* Create accounts with external AI providers
* Upload organizational information to public AI services
* Deploy AI applications without approval
* Connect AI tools to enterprise systems
* Develop personal AI agents for business activities

Governance should address shadow AI through a combination of policy, awareness, technical controls, monitoring, and approved alternatives.

---

### 83. AI Data Protection Policy

It may address:

* Data classification
* Data minimization
* Data access
* Data transfer
* Data retention
* Data deletion
* Encryption
* Data leakage prevention
* Third-party data processing

Requirements should align with enterprise information-security and data-governance policies.

---

### 84. AI Privacy Policy

* Personal data

# 01.15 — AI Governance Policies, Standards and Procedures

## Part 4 — Policy Lifecycle, Governance Integration, Compliance and Continuous Improvement

### 121. AI Policy Lifecycle

AI governance policies should be managed through a defined lifecycle.

A typical policy lifecycle includes:

1. Identify the governance requirement.
2. Draft the policy.
3. Consult stakeholders.
4. Review legal and regulatory requirements.
5. Assess operational impact.
6. Obtain approval.
7. Publish the policy.
8. Communicate requirements.
9. Implement supporting controls.
10. Monitor compliance.
11. Review effectiveness.
12. Update or retire the policy.

The lifecycle should ensure that policies remain relevant throughout changes in technology, regulation, and organizational strategy.

---

### 122. Policy Initiation

A new AI governance policy may be initiated because of:

* New AI technology
* Emerging threats
* Regulatory requirements
* Audit findings
* Security incidents
* Business requirements
* Risk assessments
* Organizational changes
* Existing control deficiencies

The organization should have a defined mechanism for identifying when a new policy is required.

---

### 123. Policy Requirements Analysis

Before drafting a policy, the organization should understand the requirements that the policy must address.

Analysis may consider:

* Business objectives
* AI risks
* Security requirements
* Privacy requirements
* Regulatory obligations
* Existing policies
* Existing controls
* Industry practices
* Organizational risk appetite

This prevents policies from being developed in isolation.

---

### 124. Policy Gap Assessment

Existing governance should be evaluated before creating new requirements.

A gap assessment may identify:

* Missing policies
* Outdated requirements
* Duplicate requirements
* Conflicting requirements
* Weak controls
* Unclear ownership

The objective is to address genuine governance gaps rather than continuously increasing documentation.

---

### 125. Policy Drafting

AI policies should be drafted using consistent organizational standards.

Drafting should ensure that requirements are:

* Clear
* Relevant
* Actionable
* Enforceable
* Risk-based
* Auditable

Policy language should avoid unnecessary technical detail when the technical requirement belongs in a supporting standard.

---

### 126. Stakeholder Consultation

AI policies often affect multiple organizational functions.

Consultation may involve:

* Business units
* AI teams
* Cybersecurity
* IT
* Data governance
* Privacy
* Legal
* Compliance
* Risk
* Procurement
* Internal Audit

Consultation helps identify conflicts and implementation challenges before approval.

---

### 127. Legal and Regulatory Review

Policies with legal or regulatory implications should receive appropriate legal and compliance review.

The review may determine:

* Applicable obligations
* Required controls
* Regulatory terminology
* Reporting requirements
* Contractual implications
* Jurisdictional considerations

Legal review should inform policy development without replacing business and security risk analysis.

---

### 128. Security Review

AI governance policies with cybersecurity implications should receive appropriate security review.

Security review may evaluate:

* Threat exposure

* Access requirements

Privacy review may examine:

* Data collection
* Data usage
* Data sharing
* Data retention
* Data transfers
* Individual rights
* Automated decision-making

Privacy requirements should be incorporated into the policy and supporting controls.

### 130. Business Impact Review

Policies should be evaluated for their potential operational impact.

The review may consider:

* Implementation cost
* Business disruption
* Technology requirements
* Staffing
* Training
* Process changes
* Time requirements

Governance requirements should be strong enough to manage risk while remaining operationally practical.

---

### 131. Policy Approval

Policies should be formally approved before becoming mandatory.

Approval authority should correspond to:

* Organizational importance
* Risk
* Scope
* Regulatory significance

Approval records should be retained as governance evidence.

---

### 132. Policy Publication

Approved policies should be published through controlled organizational channels.

Publication should ensure:

* Current version availability
* Controlled access
* Version identification
* Effective date visibility
* Retirement of obsolete versions where appropriate

Employees should have a reliable source for determining which policy is currently applicable.

---

### 133. Policy Communication

Policy publication should be accompanied by appropriate communication.

Communication may include:

* Employee announcements
* Management briefings
* Training
* Awareness campaigns
* Technical workshops
* Targeted communications

The communication approach should reflect the policy's importance and affected population.

---

### 134. Policy Implementation

Policy approval does not automatically produce compliance.

Implementation may require:

* New controls
* Process changes
* Technology changes
* Training
* Role assignment
* System configuration
* Monitoring
* Documentation

Implementation plans should identify accountable owners and target dates.

---

### 135. Policy-to-Control Implementation

Every significant policy requirement should be translated into appropriate controls.

For example:

**Policy**

→ High-risk AI must be assessed before production.

**Control**

→ A formal AI risk assessment is completed and approved.

**Evidence**

→ Approved risk assessment record.

This creates a traceable relationship between policy and operational control.

---

### 136. Policy-to-Procedure Implementation

Policies should be supported by procedures where operational execution requires repeatable steps.

For example:

**Policy**

→ AI systems must undergo appropriate security assessment.

**Standard**

→ High-risk AI systems must complete specified security testing.

**Procedure**

→ Security teams perform the assessment using an approved workflow.

This separation prevents policies from becoming overly detailed operational manuals.

---

### 137. Policy Compliance Monitoring

Organizations should monitor whether applicable policies are being followed.

Monitoring may use:

* Compliance assessments
* Automated controls
* Management attestations
* Security monitoring
* Internal reviews
* Audit activities

Monitoring frequency should reflect the risk and importance of the requirement.

* Policy violations
* Overdue remediation
* Training completion
* Unauthorized AI usage

Metrics should provide meaningful insight into governance effectiveness.

### 139. Policy Exceptions and Deviations

A controlled exception process should determine:

* Why the requirement cannot be met
* What risk results
* What compensating controls exist
* Who approves the deviation
* How long it is valid

Exceptions should remain visible to appropriate governance authorities.

### 140. Policy Exception Monitoring

Organizations should monitor exceptions for patterns.

Analysis may identify:

* Repeated exceptions
* Business units with high exception rates
* Expired exceptions
* Weak compensating controls
* Requirements that are consistently impractical

Recurring exceptions may indicate that a policy, standard, process, or technology capability needs improvement.

* Regulatory requirements

### 150. Policy Effectiveness Review

* Is the policy understood?
* Is it being followed?
* Are controls operating?
* Are risks being reduced?
* Are exceptions increasing?
* Are incidents occurring despite the policy?
* Is the policy practical?

* The control operates effectively

### 159. AI Governance Policy Maturity

Policy maturity can develop through several stages:

**Ad Hoc**

→ Policies are incomplete or inconsistent.

**Repeatable**

→ Basic policies exist and are reused.

**Defined**

→ Policies, standards, and procedures are formally structured.

**Managed**

→ Compliance, metrics, and assurance are established.

**Optimized**

→ Governance documentation is continuously improved using risk, performance, and assurance data.

Maturity should be evaluated based on actual governance capability rather than document volume.

### 160. Integrated AI Governance Documentation System

A mature AI governance documentation environment creates an integrated chain:

**AI Governance Principles**

→ Define the fundamental governance philosophy.

**Policies**

→ Establish mandatory organizational requirements.

**Standards**

→ Define specific mandatory requirements and control baselines.

**Procedures**

→ Define repeatable implementation processes.

**Work Instructions**

→ Provide detailed operational execution.

**Controls**

→ Implement the requirements.

**Evidence**

→ Demonstrates implementation and operation.

**Monitoring and Assurance**

→ Determine whether the governance system remains effective.

This creates a complete governance architecture in which **policy establishes direction, standards establish measurable requirements, procedures establish execution, controls establish protection, and assurance establishes confidence that the governance system is actually working**.
