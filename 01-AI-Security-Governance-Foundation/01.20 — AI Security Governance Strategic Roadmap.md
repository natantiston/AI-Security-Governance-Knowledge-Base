# 01.20 — AI Security Governance Strategic Roadmap

## Part 1 — Strategic Direction, Current State, Target State and Roadmap Foundations

### 1. Introduction to the AI Security Governance Strategic Roadmap

An AI Security Governance Strategic Roadmap defines how an organization will develop, implement, mature, and continuously improve its AI Security Governance capabilities over time.

The roadmap translates the organization's:

* AI strategy
* Security strategy
* Risk appetite
* Governance objectives
* Regulatory requirements
* Current maturity
* Future-state ambitions

into a structured sequence of strategic initiatives.

The roadmap provides the connection between **where the organization is today and where it needs to be in the future**.

---

### 2. Purpose of the Strategic Roadmap

The primary purpose of the roadmap is to establish a practical path for building sustainable AI Security Governance.

The roadmap should help the organization:

* Establish governance foundations
* Identify capability gaps
* Prioritize security improvements
* Allocate resources
* Establish implementation timelines
* Define ownership
* Measure progress
* Coordinate multiple stakeholders
* Align governance investments with business priorities

A roadmap prevents AI Security Governance from developing through disconnected initiatives.

---

### 3. Strategic Roadmap Versus Implementation Plan

A strategic roadmap provides the **direction and sequence** of major capability development.

An implementation plan provides the **specific execution details**.

For example:

**Strategic Roadmap**

→ Establish enterprise AI inventory capability.

**Implementation Plan**

→ Select inventory technology, define data fields, assign owners, configure workflows, migrate existing records, and establish operating procedures.

The roadmap therefore operates at a higher strategic level while remaining sufficiently detailed to guide implementation.

---

### 4. Strategic Roadmap Objectives

The roadmap should establish measurable strategic objectives.

Examples include:

* Achieve enterprise-wide AI visibility.
* Establish risk-based AI governance.
* Integrate AI security into the development lifecycle.
* Establish AI third-party governance.
* Implement AI security monitoring.
* Establish AI incident-response capabilities.
* Improve AI governance maturity.
* Establish continuous assurance.
* Integrate AI governance with enterprise GRC.

Each objective should contribute to the organization's overall AI security strategy.

---

### 5. Strategic Alignment

The AI Security Governance roadmap should align with broader organizational strategies.

Relevant strategies may include:

* Corporate strategy
* Digital transformation strategy
* AI strategy
* Cybersecurity strategy
* Cloud strategy
* Data strategy
* Risk strategy
* Privacy strategy
* Technology strategy

Strategic alignment ensures that AI governance supports organizational objectives rather than operating as an isolated security initiative.

---

### 6. Business Alignment

AI Security Governance should support business outcomes.

The roadmap should consider:

* Revenue objectives
* Customer experience
* Operational efficiency
* Innovation
* Digital transformation
* Business resilience
* Market expansion

Governance investments should be connected to the organization's business priorities.

---

### 7. Risk Alignment

The roadmap should be driven by the organization's AI risk profile.

Risk considerations may include:

* Business criticality
* Data sensitivity
* Regulatory exposure
* AI autonomy
* Security threats
* Third-party dependency
* Customer impact
* Operational impact

Higher-risk areas should generally receive earlier and stronger governance investment.

---

### 8. Regulatory Alignment

The roadmap should consider applicable regulatory and legal requirements.

Regulatory considerations may influence:

* AI classification
* Risk assessments
* Documentation
* Transparency
* Human oversight
* Security controls
* Monitoring
* Incident management

Regulatory requirements should be translated into practical roadmap initiatives.

---

### 9. Current-State Assessment

The first major step in developing the roadmap is understanding the current state.

The assessment should examine:

* AI governance structure
* AI inventory
* AI risk management
* Security controls
* AI lifecycle processes
* Third-party governance
* AI monitoring
* Incident response
* Training
* Metrics
* Assurance

The current state establishes the baseline from which the roadmap begins.

---

### 10. Current AI Governance Maturity

The organization should determine its current maturity across relevant capabilities.

For example:

| Capability                | Current State |
| ------------------------- | ------------- |
| AI Inventory              | Developing    |
| AI Risk Management        | Basic         |
| AI Security Controls      | Developing    |
| AI Monitoring             | Limited       |
| Third-Party AI Governance | Basic         |
| AI Incident Response      | Developing    |
| AI Assurance              | Limited       |

The maturity assessment provides evidence for roadmap prioritization.

---

### 11. Current-State Capability Mapping

Current capabilities should be mapped across the organization.

The mapping may cover:

* People
* Processes
* Technology
* Governance
* Controls
* Data
* Skills
* Reporting

This helps identify where capabilities already exist and where new investment is required.

---

### 12. Existing Capability Reuse

Organizations should reuse existing capabilities wherever possible.

Existing capabilities may include:

* Enterprise GRC
* Cybersecurity governance
* IAM
* SIEM
* SOC
* Vendor risk management
* Data governance
* Privacy management
* Secure development
* Internal audit

The roadmap should extend existing capabilities rather than unnecessarily creating parallel systems.

---

### 13. Current-State Challenges

The assessment should identify obstacles affecting AI governance.

Common challenges may include:

* Unknown AI usage
* Shadow AI
* Unclear ownership
* Fragmented processes
* Inconsistent controls
* Limited AI security expertise
* Weak third-party visibility
* Lack of monitoring
* Insufficient executive reporting

These challenges become inputs into roadmap development.

---

### 14. Target-State Definition

The organization should define the desired future state.

The target state should describe:

* Governance capabilities
* Operating model
* Risk processes
* Security controls
* Technology capabilities
* Reporting
* Assurance
* Maturity

The target state should represent a realistic future capability rather than an abstract ideal.

---

### 15. Target-State AI Governance Capability

A mature target state may include:

* Complete or sufficiently reliable AI inventory
* Risk-based classification
* Integrated AI lifecycle governance
* Strong AI security controls
* Third-party AI oversight
* AI-specific monitoring
* AI incident response
* Continuous assurance
* Executive reporting
* Continuous improvement

The target state should be aligned with the organization's risk profile.

---

### 16. Target-State Maturity

The organization should define the maturity level it intends to achieve.

Not every capability necessarily needs the highest maturity level.

For example:

**Low-Risk AI**

→ Standardized governance.

**High-Risk AI**

→ Managed and continuously monitored governance.

**Critical AI**

→ Advanced governance with enhanced assurance.

This prevents unnecessary investment in low-risk areas.

---

### 17. Current-State to Target-State Gap Analysis

The organization should compare current capabilities with target capabilities.

The analysis may identify gaps in:

* Governance
* People
* Process
* Technology
* Controls
* Skills
* Data
* Reporting

Each gap should be evaluated according to its business and security significance.

---

### 18. Strategic Capability Gaps

Not all gaps are equally important.

Strategic gaps are weaknesses that could materially affect the organization's ability to govern AI.

Examples include:

* No enterprise AI inventory
* No AI risk methodology
* No AI security standards
* No AI ownership model
* No high-risk AI approval process
* No AI incident-response capability

These gaps should receive higher roadmap priority.

---

### 19. Roadmap Prioritization

Roadmap initiatives should be prioritized according to factors such as:

* Risk reduction
* Regulatory urgency
* Business impact
* Security impact
* AI adoption
* Implementation effort
* Dependencies
* Organizational readiness

Prioritization prevents the roadmap from becoming an unmanageable list of initiatives.

---

### 20. Risk-Based Roadmap Prioritization

Risk should be one of the strongest prioritization factors.

For example:

**High Risk + High Business Impact**

→ Immediate priority.

**High Risk + Low Business Impact**

→ High priority.

**Low Risk + High Business Impact**

→ Strategic priority.

**Low Risk + Low Business Impact**

→ Lower priority.

This creates a rational basis for sequencing investments.

---

### 21. Regulatory-Driven Prioritization

Some initiatives may receive priority because they address regulatory requirements.

Examples include:

* AI risk classification
* Documentation
* Human oversight
* Security controls
* Transparency
* Monitoring
* Incident management

Regulatory priorities should be integrated with broader risk and business priorities.

---

### 22. Business-Driven Prioritization

Business priorities may accelerate certain governance capabilities.

For example, if an organization plans rapid adoption of:

* Generative AI
* AI agents
* Customer-facing AI
* AI-enabled automation

the roadmap should prioritize governance capabilities associated with those technologies.

Governance should evolve ahead of significant AI adoption where possible.

---

### 23. Dependency Analysis

Roadmap initiatives often depend on other capabilities.

For example:

may depend on:

→ AI Inventory

→ AI Classification

→ Risk Assessment

→ Risk Data

→ Reporting Infrastructure

Dependencies should be identified before establishing implementation timelines.

---

### 24. Capability Sequencing

Capabilities should generally be developed in a logical sequence.

For example:

**Discover**

→ Identify AI systems.

**Classify**

→ Determine risk.

**Assess**

→ Evaluate security requirements.

**Control**

→ Implement safeguards.

**Monitor**

→ Observe ongoing risk.

**Assure**

→ Validate effectiveness.

This sequencing prevents advanced capabilities from being built on weak foundations.

---

### 25. Roadmap Horizons

A strategic roadmap can be divided into different time horizons.

**Near Term**

→ Establish foundational capabilities.

**Medium Term**

→ Standardize and integrate governance.

**Long Term**

→ Optimize, automate, and continuously improve.

The actual duration of each horizon should reflect organizational circumstances.

---

Near-term priorities may include:

* Program charter
* AI policy

* AI classification
* AI risk methodology
* Basic security requirements
* AI intake process

---

### 27. Medium-Term Governance Priorities

Medium-term priorities may include:

* Third-party AI governance
* AI security architecture
* AI security testing
* AI monitoring
* Incident response
* GRC integration
* Metrics
* Assurance

These capabilities transform governance from foundational to operational.

---

### 28. Long-Term Governance Priorities

Long-term priorities may include:

* Continuous AI discovery
* Continuous control monitoring
* Automated risk assessment
* Advanced AI security analytics
* Continuous assurance
* Advanced AI agent governance
* Predictive risk management

These capabilities support optimized and adaptive governance.

---

### 29. Strategic Roadmap Workstreams

The roadmap can be organized into strategic workstreams.

Potential workstreams include:

1. Governance and policy

Each roadmap initiative should have a clearly defined structure.
