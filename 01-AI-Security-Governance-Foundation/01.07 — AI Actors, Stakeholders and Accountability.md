# 01.07 — AI Actors, Stakeholders and Accountability

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Actors, Stakeholders and Accountability

### 1. Introduction

AI Security Governance requires clear understanding of **who participates in the AI ecosystem, what role each party performs, what authority each party possesses, and who is accountable for the resulting risks and decisions**.

An AI system rarely belongs to or operates within a single organizational function.

Depending on its complexity, an AI system may involve:

* Board members
* Executive management
* Business owners
* AI developers
* Data scientists
* Data owners
* Security teams
* Privacy teams
* Risk teams
* Legal and compliance teams
* IT teams
* AI users
* Customers
* Suppliers
* Cloud providers
* AI model providers
* Regulators
* Auditors

The presence of multiple participants creates a fundamental governance challenge:

> **Who is responsible for what?**

---

### 2. What Is an AI Actor?

An **AI actor** is an individual, group, organization, or technical entity that participates in or influences an AI system or its lifecycle.

AI actors may:

* Design AI systems
* Develop models
* Provide data
* Deploy AI
* Operate AI
* Use AI
* Monitor AI
* Govern AI
* Audit AI
* Regulate AI
* Supply AI technology

An actor may have one role or several roles simultaneously.

---

### 3. What Is an AI Stakeholder?

An **AI stakeholder** is an individual or organization that can:

* Influence an AI system
* Be affected by an AI system
* Have an interest in the AI system
* Have authority over the AI system
* Bear responsibility for its outcomes

Stakeholders therefore include both people who operate AI and people who may be affected by its decisions.

---

### 4. Actors and Stakeholders Are Not Identical

The terms **actor** and **stakeholder** are related but should not automatically be treated as interchangeable.

An actor generally participates in the AI ecosystem.

A stakeholder may have an interest in or be affected by that ecosystem without directly operating it.

For example:

**AI Developer**

is an actor because the developer builds the system.

A:

**Customer**

may be a stakeholder because the customer is affected by how the AI system processes or uses their information.

---

### 5. What Is Accountability?

**Accountability** is the obligation of an individual or organizational role to answer for decisions, actions, outcomes, and compliance within its assigned area of responsibility.

Accountability answers:

> **Who is ultimately answerable for the outcome?**

This is different from simply asking:

> **Who performed the activity?**

---

### 6. Accountability Versus Responsibility

These concepts should be distinguished.

**Responsibility** generally refers to performing or managing an activity.

**Accountability** refers to being answerable for the outcome.

For example:

**AI Security Team**

→ Responsible for performing a security assessment.

**AI Business Owner**

→ Accountable for accepting the resulting business risk.

The exact allocation will vary by organizational structure.

---

### 7. Accountability Cannot Be Delegated Indefinitely

An executive may delegate activities to employees or external providers.

However, governance should maintain a clearly identified accountable owner.

For example:

**Organization**

→ AI System Owner

→ Vendor

→ Cloud Provider

A vendor may perform technical activities, but the organization still needs to establish who remains accountable for the business outcome and risk.

---

### 8. Why AI Creates Accountability Challenges

Traditional systems often have relatively clear ownership.

AI can involve:

* A model from one company
* Cloud infrastructure from another
* Data from another department
* An application developed internally
* A third-party AI service
* An employee using the system

When an AI system produces an unexpected result, accountability can become unclear.

---

### 9. The AI Accountability Chain

A simplified accountability chain may look like:

**Board**

↓

**Executive Management**

↓

**AI Governance Leadership**

↓

**Business Owner**

↓

**AI System Owner**

↓

**Technical Teams**

↓

**AI Users**

Each level may have different decision rights and responsibilities.

---

### 10. Organizational Accountability

AI accountability ultimately exists within the organizational governance structure.

The organization should establish:

* AI ownership
* Risk ownership
* Security ownership
* Data ownership
* Approval authority
* Escalation authority
* Monitoring responsibility
* Reporting responsibility

These should be defined before AI systems become operationally critical.

---

### 11. AI Actor Categories

AI actors can broadly be divided into:

1. Internal organizational actors
2. External technology actors
3. Data-related actors
4. AI development actors
5. AI operational actors
6. Governance actors
7. Assurance actors
8. External affected stakeholders
9. Regulatory actors
10. Malicious or unauthorized actors

This classification provides a foundation for understanding the wider AI ecosystem.

---

### 12. Internal Organizational Actors

Internal AI actors may include:

* Board of Directors
* Executive Management
* CISO
* CIO
* CTO
* CRO
* DPO
* AI leadership
* Business units
* IT
* Cybersecurity
* Data teams
* Risk management
* Legal
* Compliance
* Internal Audit

The specific structure depends on the organization's size and operating model.

---

### 13. Board of Directors

The board provides organizational oversight of significant risks.

For AI, board-level responsibilities may include:

* Understanding material AI risks
* Reviewing AI strategy
* Understanding risk appetite
* Challenging management
* Monitoring significant incidents
* Ensuring appropriate governance

The board generally does not operate AI systems directly.

Its role is oversight and accountability at the highest governance level.

---

### 14. Executive Management

Executive management translates board-level expectations into organizational action.

Executives may establish:

* AI strategy
* Governance structures
* Risk appetite
* Investment priorities
* Policies
* Accountability structures

Executives also need sufficient visibility into significant AI risks.

---

### 15. Business Owner

The **business owner** is generally responsible for ensuring that the AI capability serves a legitimate business purpose.

The business owner may determine:

* Why AI is being used
* What business outcome is expected
* What users require
* What level of risk is acceptable
* Whether the AI capability remains necessary

The business owner is therefore a critical governance participant.

---

### 16. AI System Owner

The **AI system owner** is responsible for the ongoing management of a particular AI system.

Responsibilities may include:

* System lifecycle
* Security coordination
* Risk assessment
* Access management
* Change management
* Monitoring
* Documentation
* Retirement

The system owner provides an identifiable point of accountability for the AI capability.

---

### 17. AI Product Owner

In organizations using product-oriented operating models, an AI product owner may oversee the AI capability as a product.

Responsibilities may include:

* Product objectives
* User requirements
* Prioritization
* Product lifecycle
* Performance
* Risk coordination

The product owner may work closely with the AI system owner, security teams, and business leadership.

---

### 18. AI Developer

AI developers build or integrate AI functionality.

Their responsibilities may include:

* Secure coding
* Application integration
* API security
* Input validation
* Output handling
* Authentication
* Authorization
* Security testing

Developers are important actors but should not automatically become the sole owners of AI risk.

---

### 19. Data Scientist

Data scientists may develop and evaluate models.

Their activities can include:

* Model development
* Feature engineering
* Data analysis
* Model evaluation
* Experimentation
* Performance analysis

They should understand relevant security, privacy, and governance requirements.

---

### 20. Machine Learning Engineer

Machine learning engineers may operationalize models and machine-learning pipelines.

Their responsibilities can include:

* Model deployment
* Pipeline engineering
* Model monitoring
* Infrastructure integration
* Model version management

They connect model development with operational environments.

---

### 21. MLOps and AI Platform Teams

MLOps or AI platform teams may operate shared AI infrastructure.

Responsibilities can include:

* Model deployment platforms
* Training environments
* Model repositories
* Infrastructure
* CI/CD pipelines
* Monitoring
* Platform security

Because these platforms support multiple AI systems, their governance significance can be substantial.

---

### 22. Data Owner

The **data owner** determines how organizational data may be used.

For AI, the data owner may be responsible for decisions concerning:

* Data classification
* Data access
* Permitted use
* Retention
* Sharing
* Sensitivity

AI systems should not automatically receive access to all data available to the organization.

---

### 23. Data Custodian

The data custodian typically manages the technical implementation of data protection requirements.

Activities may include:

* Access provisioning
* Storage controls
* Backup
* Technical safeguards
* Monitoring

The data owner determines appropriate use, while the custodian helps implement the required controls.

---

### 24. AI User

AI users interact directly with AI systems.

Users may include:

* Employees
* Contractors
* Administrators
* Analysts
* Developers
* Customers
* Partners

Users can introduce risk through:

* Unsafe prompts
* Unauthorized data disclosure
* Misuse
* Excessive reliance on AI
* Circumvention of controls

---

### 25. AI Administrator

AI administrators may manage:

* User access
* Configuration
* Integrations
* Model settings
* Tools
* Monitoring
* Security controls

Administrative privileges should be carefully controlled because configuration changes can materially affect AI behavior.

---

### 26. Security Architect

The security architect helps determine how AI systems should be securely designed.

Responsibilities may include:

* Threat modeling
* Security architecture
* Trust boundaries
* Identity design
* Network segmentation
* Data protection
* Security controls

The security architect helps translate AI risks into technical security requirements.

---

### 27. Cybersecurity Team

Cybersecurity teams may provide:

* Threat intelligence
* Security monitoring
* Vulnerability management
* Incident response
* Security assessments
* Threat modeling
* Security architecture

AI security should become integrated with enterprise cybersecurity rather than operate separately.

---

### 28. CISO

The **Chief Information Security Officer** provides executive leadership for cybersecurity risk.

In relation to AI, the CISO may oversee:

* AI security strategy
* AI cyber risk
* Security controls
* AI security architecture
* AI incident response
* Security monitoring
* Security assurance

The CISO should coordinate with other AI governance leaders rather than assume sole ownership of every AI risk.

---

### 29. CIO

The **Chief Information Officer** typically oversees enterprise information technology.

AI governance responsibilities may involve:

* AI technology strategy
* IT integration
* Enterprise architecture
* Technology investment
* AI platform management
* Operational resilience

The CIO often works closely with the CISO and CTO.

---

### 30. CTO

The **Chief Technology Officer** may lead technology strategy, innovation, engineering, or product technology depending on the organization.

AI responsibilities may include:

* AI technology strategy
* Model and platform architecture
* Engineering capabilities
* Innovation
* Technical standards

The exact division between CIO and CTO responsibilities depends on the organizational operating model.

## Part 2 — AI Governance, Risk, Security, Privacy and Operational Stakeholders

### 31. Chief Risk Officer

The **Chief Risk Officer (CRO)** provides leadership over enterprise risk management.

In an AI environment, the CRO may oversee:

* AI risk integration into enterprise risk management
* AI risk appetite
* Risk assessment methodologies
* Aggregated AI risk reporting
* Risk acceptance processes
* Material AI risk escalation

The CRO does not replace the business owner as the owner of individual business risks.

---

### 32. AI Risk Manager

An AI Risk Manager may coordinate the identification, assessment, treatment, and monitoring of AI-related risks.

Activities may include:

* Maintaining AI risk registers
* Coordinating risk assessments
* Tracking remediation
* Reporting risk exposure
* Supporting risk acceptance
* Monitoring changes in AI risk

This role may exist as a dedicated position or as part of broader enterprise risk management.

---

### 33. AI Governance Officer

An **AI Governance Officer** may coordinate the organization's AI governance framework.

Responsibilities may include:

* AI policies
* AI inventories
* Governance processes
* Risk classification
* Approval workflows
* Governance reporting
* Stakeholder coordination

The role provides organizational structure around AI governance activities.

---

### 34. AI Governance Committee

An AI Governance Committee may provide cross-functional oversight of AI.

Membership may include representatives from:

* Business
* Technology
* Cybersecurity
* Risk
* Privacy
* Legal
* Compliance
* Data
* Internal Audit

The committee can provide a mechanism for resolving cross-functional AI governance decisions.

---

### 35. AI Steering Committee

An AI Steering Committee may focus more heavily on AI strategy and organizational direction.

It may evaluate:

* AI investment
* Strategic priorities
* Major AI initiatives
* Enterprise risks
* AI operating models
* Transformation programs

The committee should have clearly defined authority so that it does not become merely an advisory forum without decision rights.

---

### 36. Chief AI Officer

Some organizations establish a **Chief AI Officer (CAIO)** or equivalent AI leadership position.

Depending on the organization, this role may oversee:

* AI strategy
* AI adoption
* AI governance
* AI capabilities
* AI transformation
* AI portfolio management

The CAIO does not necessarily replace existing technology, security, risk, privacy, or business responsibilities.

---

### 37. AI Leadership

AI leadership may exist as a formal executive role or as a distributed leadership structure.

Its responsibilities may include:

* Establishing AI strategy
* Coordinating AI investments
* Defining AI priorities
* Supporting responsible AI adoption
* Coordinating governance

The structure should reflect organizational size, complexity, and AI maturity.

---

### 38. Data Protection Officer

The **Data Protection Officer (DPO)** may have an important role where AI systems process personal data.

The DPO may advise on:

* Privacy obligations
* Data protection impact assessments
* Data subject rights
* Data minimization
* Lawful processing
* International data transfers
* Privacy risks

The DPO's role should remain appropriately independent where required by applicable law.

---

### 39. Privacy Team

Privacy professionals may assess how AI systems collect, process, store, retrieve, and disclose personal information.

AI privacy considerations may include:

* Training data
* Prompts
* Conversation history
* Retrieval data
* Model outputs
* Logs
* Memory
* Third-party processing

Privacy governance should be integrated into AI lifecycle governance.

---

### 40. Legal Counsel

Legal teams may advise on:

* Contracts
* Intellectual property
* Liability
* Regulatory obligations
* Data rights
* AI-related disputes
* Vendor agreements

Legal review may be particularly important for high-impact or externally facing AI systems.

---

### 41. Compliance Function

Compliance teams may assess whether AI systems meet applicable:

* Laws
* Regulations
* Regulatory guidance
* Internal requirements
* Industry obligations

Compliance should translate applicable requirements into practical governance and control expectations.

---

### 42. Regulatory Affairs

Organizations operating across multiple jurisdictions may have dedicated regulatory affairs functions.

These teams may monitor:

* New AI legislation
* Regulatory guidance
* Sector requirements
* Regulatory consultations
* Enforcement developments

Regulatory intelligence can help organizations anticipate changes to AI governance requirements.

---

### 43. Internal Audit

Internal Audit provides independent assurance over governance, risk management, and controls.

AI-related audit activities may assess:

* AI governance
* AI risk management
* AI controls
* Policy compliance
* Accountability
* Monitoring
* Evidence
* Control effectiveness

Internal Audit should maintain appropriate independence from the activities it evaluates.

---

### 44. External Auditor

External auditors may assess AI-related controls when AI forms part of a relevant audit scope.

Depending on the engagement, this may include:

* IT controls
* Financial controls
* Regulatory compliance
* Data controls
* Security controls

The specific role depends on the audit objective and applicable standards.

---

### 45. AI Security Auditor

An AI security auditor may focus specifically on the security aspects of AI systems.

Potential assessment areas include:

* AI attack surfaces
* Access controls
* Model security
* Data protection
* Prompt security
* Agent controls
* Monitoring
* Incident response

The auditor provides independent assessment rather than operating the controls being audited.

---

### 46. Model Risk Management

Organizations using AI for material decisions may establish model risk management capabilities.

These may address:

* Model development
* Validation
* Performance
* Limitations
* Model changes
* Model use
* Model retirement

Model risk management is particularly relevant in highly regulated or high-impact environments.

---

### 47. Model Validator

A model validator independently evaluates whether a model is appropriate for its intended use.

Validation may examine:

* Accuracy
* Robustness
* Assumptions
* Data
* Limitations
* Performance
* Testing

Independent validation helps reduce the risk of self-assessment by the model development team.

---

### 48. AI Safety Team

Organizations developing advanced or high-impact AI systems may establish AI safety capabilities.

The team may evaluate:

* Unsafe behavior
* Failure modes
* Guardrails
* Human oversight
* High-impact use cases
* Safety testing

AI safety should coordinate with cybersecurity and AI governance rather than operate in isolation.

---

### 49. AI Ethics Function

An ethics function may examine issues involving:

* Fairness
* Human autonomy
* Discrimination
* Social impact
* Transparency
* Responsible use

Ethical review may be particularly relevant for AI systems affecting individuals or society.

---

### 50. AI Compliance Officer

An AI Compliance Officer may coordinate compliance obligations specifically associated with AI.

Responsibilities may include:

* AI regulatory requirements
* Compliance assessments
* Regulatory documentation
* AI registers
* Compliance monitoring
* Regulatory reporting

The role may be combined with broader compliance responsibilities.

---

### 51. Enterprise Architect

The enterprise architect considers how AI fits within the organization's broader technology architecture.

Responsibilities may include:

* AI architecture principles
* Integration patterns
* Technology standards
* Application dependencies
* Data architecture
* Cloud architecture

Enterprise architecture helps prevent fragmented AI adoption.

---

### 52. AI Solution Architect

The AI Solution Architect designs specific AI solutions.

The role may address:

* Model selection
* Application architecture
* Data flows
* Retrieval
* APIs
* Tools
* Security boundaries
* Deployment

Security requirements should be incorporated into solution design from the beginning.

### 53. Cloud Architect

Relevant areas include:

* Cloud security
* Network architecture
* Identity
* Storage
* Compute
* Kubernetes

AI security must therefore be integrated with cloud-security architecture.

### 54. DevSecOps Team

DevSecOps teams integrate security into development and deployment pipelines.

For AI systems, this may include:

* Secure code
* Dependency scanning
* Container security
* Infrastructure-as-code security

* Deployment controls

AI development pipelines should not bypass established DevSecOps practices.

### 55. AI Platform Owner

An AI Platform Owner may manage shared infrastructure used by multiple AI applications.

Responsibilities may include:

* Platform availability
* Platform security
* Model hosting
* Common services
* Access management
* Platform standards

A shared AI platform can create centralized governance opportunities as well as concentration risk.

### 56. AI Operations Team

AI Operations teams may manage AI systems after deployment.

Responsibilities may include:

* Availability
* Performance
* Monitoring
* Incident handling
* Capacity
* Operational changes

Operational teams become particularly important when AI supports business-critical processes.

---

### 57. Security Operations Center

Potential telemetry includes:

* AI authentication
* API activity
* Tool usage
* Data access
* Suspicious prompts
* Agent activity
* Configuration changes

AI monitoring should be integrated into existing security operations where practical.

### 58. Incident Response Team

Incident responders may investigate AI-related security events such as:

* Data leakage
* Compromised AI accounts
* Prompt-based attacks
* Malicious tool activity
* Model compromise
* AI service abuse

Incident-response procedures should account for AI-specific evidence and dependencies.

### 59. Threat Intelligence Team

Threat intelligence teams can monitor emerging AI threats.

These may include:

* New attack techniques
* Prompt-injection campaigns
* AI-enabled attacks
* Vulnerable AI components
* Malicious AI tools
* Threat actor use of AI

Threat intelligence can inform AI risk assessments and security controls.

### 60. Security Engineering

Activities can include:

* Identity controls
* Network controls
* Application security
* Secrets management
* Monitoring
* Security tooling
* Data protection

Security engineering converts governance requirements into technical implementations.

---

### 61. AI Red Team

An AI red team tests AI systems from an adversarial perspective.

Testing may target:

* Prompt injection
* Jailbreaks
* Data leakage
* Model manipulation
* Agent abuse
* Tool misuse
* Authorization bypass

The red team provides an adversarial view of the AI security posture.

---

### 62. AI Blue Team

An AI blue team focuses on defending AI systems.

Activities may include:

* Monitoring
* Detection
* Prevention
* Incident response
* Hardening
* Threat hunting

In some organizations these capabilities may be incorporated into existing cybersecurity teams.

---

### 63. AI Purple Team

The objective is to use attack findings to improve:

* Detection
* Prevention
* Response
* Architecture
* Governance

This creates a continuous feedback loop between testing and defense.

## Part 3 — AI Roles, Decision Rights, Accountability and Governance Relationships

### 86. Role Clarity in AI Governance

AI governance becomes ineffective when organizational roles are unclear.

A mature governance structure should clearly identify:

* Who owns the AI system
* Who owns the data
* Who owns the risk
* Who approves deployment
* Who approves exceptions
* Who implements controls
* Who monitors the system
* Who can stop the system
* Who reports incidents
* Who provides independent assurance

Role clarity prevents responsibility from being distributed so broadly that no one is ultimately accountable.

---

### 87. AI Role Definition

Each significant AI role should have a documented definition.

The definition should establish:

* Purpose of the role
* Authority
* Responsibilities
* Required competencies
* Decision rights
* Escalation responsibilities
* Reporting relationships

This creates a consistent governance structure across AI initiatives.

---

### 88. AI Decision Rights

**Decision rights** define who has authority to make specific AI-related decisions.

Examples include authority to:

* Approve an AI use case
* Approve AI deployment
* Approve access to sensitive data
* Approve autonomous actions
* Accept residual risk
* Approve exceptions
* Suspend an AI system
* Retire an AI system

Decision rights should be explicit rather than inferred from job titles.

---

### 89. AI Decision Authority

Decision authority should correspond to the significance of the decision.

For example:

**Low-risk AI configuration**

→ AI System Owner

**High-risk AI deployment**

→ Appropriate governance authority

**Material enterprise risk acceptance**

→ Authorized executive or risk authority

**Critical AI shutdown**

→ Designated emergency authority

The precise structure depends on organizational governance.

---

### 90. AI Risk Ownership

Every material AI risk should have an identifiable **risk owner**.

The risk owner is accountable for ensuring that the risk is:

* Understood
* Assessed
* Treated
* Monitored
* Escalated
* Accepted or transferred where appropriate

Risk ownership should not automatically be assigned to the cybersecurity department simply because the risk involves technology.

---

### 91. AI Control Ownership

A **control owner** is responsible for ensuring that a specific control is appropriately designed, implemented, and maintained.

For example:

**Risk**

→ Unauthorized AI data access

**Control**

→ AI access-control review

**Control Owner**

→ Appropriate identity or system owner

The control owner and risk owner may be different people.

---

### 92. AI Process Ownership

AI governance depends on clearly owned processes.

Examples include:

* AI intake
* AI risk assessment
* AI approval
* AI security review
* AI privacy review
* AI deployment
* AI monitoring
* AI incident response
* AI change management
* AI retirement

Each process should have an accountable owner.

---

### 93. AI System Lifecycle Ownership

Ownership should continue throughout the AI lifecycle.

A system may move through:

**Idea**

→ **Development**

→ **Testing**

→ **Approval**

→ **Deployment**

→ **Operation**

→ **Change**

→ **Retirement**

Accountability should remain clear during every stage.

### 94. AI Use-Case Owner

Before an AI system is developed, the organization should identify the owner of the intended use case.

The use-case owner should establish:

* Business purpose
* Expected benefits
* Intended users
* Intended outputs
* Acceptable use
* Business impact

This prevents organizations from deploying AI merely because the technology is available.

### 95. AI Approval Authority

Organizations should define who has authority to approve an AI system for production use.

Approval may consider:

* Business value
* Security risk
* Privacy risk
* Compliance requirements
* Operational risk
* Model performance
* Residual risk

The approval authority should be appropriate to the AI system's risk classification.

### 96. AI Exception Authority

AI systems may occasionally require exceptions to established policies or controls.

Organizations should define:

* Who may request an exception
* Who evaluates the exception
* Who approves it
* How long it remains valid
* What compensating controls apply
* When it must be reviewed

Exceptions should not become permanent alternatives to governance.

### 97. AI Risk Acceptance Authority

Risk acceptance should be performed by an appropriately authorized party.

The authority should understand:

* The identified risk
* Potential impact
* Likelihood
* Existing controls
* Residual risk
* Available alternatives

AI developers or technical administrators should not automatically have authority to accept business risk merely because they operate the system.

### 98. AI Suspension Authority

Organizations should establish who can suspend an AI system when significant risk is identified.

Possible triggers include:

* Data leakage
* Security compromise
* Unsafe behavior
* Regulatory concern
* Critical model failure
* Unauthorized activity

The authority to suspend should be practical and executable during an incident.

---

### 99. AI Shutdown Authority

For highly autonomous or critical AI systems, the organization may need explicit emergency shutdown authority.

The shutdown process should define:

* Who can initiate it
* What conditions justify it
* What systems are affected
* What alternative process takes over
* How the system is restored

This is particularly important where AI can perform consequential actions autonomously.

### 100. AI Escalation Authority

AI governance should establish escalation paths.

For example:

**AI User**

→ **AI System Owner**

→ **Security / Risk / Privacy**

The exact path depends on the severity and nature of the issue.

### 101. AI Accountability Matrix

Organizations can use an accountability matrix to document responsibilities.

A simplified example:

| Activity            | Business Owner | AI Owner | Security | Privacy | Risk |

### 102. RACI for AI Governance

A RACI model can help distinguish:

* **Responsible** — performs the activity
* **Accountable** — owns the outcome
* **Consulted** — provides expertise
* **Informed** — receives relevant information

RACI is useful, but it should not become a substitute for meaningful governance decisions.

### 103. Responsible Versus Accountable in AI

Consider an AI security assessment.

The security team may be:

**Responsible**

for conducting the assessment.

The AI system owner may be:

**Accountable**

for ensuring identified risks are addressed.

The risk function may be:

**Consulted**

on risk treatment.

Executive management may be:

**Informed**

about material residual risk.

This distinction prevents technical execution from being confused with organizational accountability.

### 104. AI Segregation of Duties

AI governance should consider **Segregation of Duties (SoD)**.

Where appropriate, the same person should not control every stage of a high-risk AI process.

For example:

**Developer**

→ Builds the AI system.

**Security**

→ Assesses security.

**Business Owner**

→ Determines business need.

**Approver**

→ Authorizes deployment.

Separating key responsibilities can reduce conflicts of interest.

### 105. AI Four-Eyes Principle

High-impact AI decisions may require two-person approval.

This can be appropriate for:

* High-value financial transactions
* Privileged actions
* Critical configuration changes
* High-impact decisions
* Irreversible operations

The objective is to reduce the likelihood that one individual or one AI system can independently cause significant harm.

---

### 106. AI Dual Control

Dual control requires two independent parties to participate in a sensitive activity.

For example:

**AI Agent**

→ Creates a high-value transaction.

**Human Approver**

→ Reviews and approves.

**Transaction System**

→ Executes.

The AI system does not independently possess the authority to complete the entire transaction.

### 107. AI Human Oversight

Human oversight should be designed according to risk.

Oversight may range from:

**Human-in-the-loop**

A human approves individual actions.

to:

**Human-on-the-loop**

A human supervises automated operations.

to:

**Human-out-of-the-loop**

The AI operates without direct human intervention.

Higher-risk systems generally require stronger forms of oversight.

---

### 108. Meaningful Human Oversight

Human oversight should be more than a formal approval button.

A human reviewer should have:

* Sufficient information
* Appropriate expertise
* Adequate time
* Authority to reject the AI output
* Ability to intervene
* Understanding of the consequences

Otherwise, human oversight may provide only the appearance of control.

---

### 109. Human Override

Organizations should define when a human can override an AI decision or action.

The override mechanism should be:

* Accessible
* Documented
* Auditable
* Secure
* Tested

Users should not be prevented from overriding an AI system when organizational policy requires human judgment.

### 110. AI Accountability for Automated Decisions

When AI influences or makes decisions, organizations should identify who is accountable for the decision process.

The existence of an AI model does not eliminate organizational accountability.

A statement such as:

does not by itself identify an accountable party.

Autonomy does not remove accountability.

The greater the autonomy, the greater the importance of clearly defined accountability.

---

### 178. Accountability for AI Memory

AI systems may retain:

* Conversations
* User preferences
* Context
* Retrieved information
* Operational history

The owner should determine:

* What is stored
* Why it is stored
* Who can access it
* How long it is retained
* How it is deleted

Memory can therefore create both privacy and security accountability requirements.

---

### 179. Accountability for AI Logs

Logs should have an identifiable owner responsible for:

* Collection
* Protection
* Retention
* Access
* Monitoring
* Investigation

Logging should support security and accountability without creating unnecessary data exposure.

---

### 180. AI Governance Documentation Owner

Organizations should identify who maintains core AI governance documentation.

This may include:

* AI inventory
* Risk assessments
* System documentation
* Model documentation
* Approval records
* Security assessments
* Privacy assessments
* Vendor records

Documentation ownership prevents governance information from becoming outdated.

---

### 181. AI Governance Record Retention

AI governance records should be retained according to:

* Legal requirements
* Regulatory requirements
* Corporate policies
* Audit requirements
* Business needs

Records may provide evidence of how important AI decisions were made.

---

### 182. AI Accountability Reporting

AI governance reporting may be provided to:

* Management
* Risk committees
* AI governance committees
* Executive leadership
* Board committees
* Board of Directors

Reports may cover:

* AI inventory
* Risk exposure
* High-risk systems
* Incidents
* Exceptions
* Control performance
* Regulatory developments

### 183. AI Governance Metrics

Accountability can be supported by measurable indicators.

Examples include:

* Percentage of AI systems inventoried
* Percentage with assigned owners
* Percentage risk-assessed
* Percentage security-assessed
* Number of unresolved high-risk findings
* Number of policy exceptions
* Percentage with documented decision rights
* Percentage with current documentation

Metrics should measure governance effectiveness rather than simply activity volume.

### 184. AI Accountability Key Risk Indicators

KRIs may identify increasing AI risk.

Examples include:

* Growth in unmanaged AI systems
* Increase in high-risk AI use cases
* Increasing vendor dependencies
* Excessive privileged AI access
* Rising security incidents
* Increasing policy exceptions
* Increasing model changes

These indicators can help management identify emerging risk.

---

### 185. AI Governance Key Performance Indicators

KPIs can measure execution of governance processes.

Examples include:

* AI assessments completed on time
* Training completion
* Control implementation
* Vendor reviews completed
* AI inventory accuracy
* Incident response performance

KPIs and KRIs should be used together.

---

### 186. AI Governance Assurance

Governance assurance should determine whether the accountability framework operates effectively.

Assurance may assess:

* Role clarity
* Decision rights
* Control ownership
* Risk ownership
* Escalation
* Evidence
* Oversight
* Independence

The objective is to determine whether the governance model works in practice.

---

### 187. Testing AI Accountability

Organizations can test accountability by selecting an AI system and asking:

1. Who owns it?
2. Who owns the data?
3. Who owns the risk?
4. Who approved it?
5. Who can change it?
6. Who monitors it?
7. Who can stop it?
8. Who responds to incidents?
9. Who accepts residual risk?
10. Who independently assures it?

If these questions cannot be answered quickly, the accountability structure may be insufficient.

---

### 188. AI Accountability Gap

An **accountability gap** exists when a material AI activity or decision does not have a clearly assigned accountable party.

Examples include:

* An AI system with no owner
* Data used without a clear data owner
* Risk without a risk owner
* Vendor activity without contractual responsibility
* Autonomous action without an approval authority

Accountability gaps should be treated as governance risks.

---

### 189. AI Accountability Overlap

The opposite problem can also occur.

Multiple functions may believe they own the same decision.

For example:

**CISO**

and

**AI Governance Committee**

and

**Business Owner**

may each assume they have final authority over AI deployment.

Governance should therefore define both:

* Who is accountable
* Who has decision authority

---

### 190. AI Accountability Conflict

Conflicts may arise when organizational objectives differ.

For example:

**Business Owner**

may prioritize speed.

**Security**

may prioritize risk reduction.

**Privacy**

may prioritize data minimization.

**Legal**

may prioritize regulatory certainty.

The governance framework should provide a formal mechanism for resolving these conflicts.

---

### 191. AI Governance Decision Escalation

A structured escalation model may be:

**Operational Issue**

→ System Owner

**Security / Privacy / Risk Issue**

→ Relevant Second-Line Function

**Material Cross-Functional Issue**

→ AI Governance Committee

**Significant Enterprise Risk**

→ Executive Management

**Strategic or Material Risk**

→ Board or Board Committee

The actual escalation thresholds should be defined by organizational policy.

---

### 192. AI Accountability During Crisis

During a major AI incident, normal decision-making may become too slow.

Organizations should establish emergency authority for:

* Suspending AI
* Revoking access
* Disabling tools
* Isolating systems
* Activating manual processes
* Notifying executives
* Initiating regulatory response

Emergency authority should be documented and tested.

---

### 193. AI Accountability and Business Resilience

Critical AI systems should have clear ownership of:

* Recovery
* Backup
* Failover
* Manual fallback
* Disaster recovery
* Service restoration

AI accountability therefore extends beyond security into operational resilience.

---

### 194. AI Accountability and Third-Party Failure

When an external AI provider fails, the organization should already know:

* Who owns the vendor relationship
* Who activates contingency plans
* Who assesses business impact
* Who communicates with stakeholders
* Who determines whether the provider can continue to be used

Third-party accountability should therefore be integrated with business continuity planning.

---

### 195. AI Accountability and Model Failure

When a model behaves unexpectedly, governance should determine:

* Who investigates
* Who validates the failure
* Who assesses business impact
* Who decides whether to suspend the model
* Who approves remediation
* Who accepts residual risk

Model failure should not automatically be treated as merely a technical problem.

---

### 196. AI Accountability and Security Failure

When an AI system is compromised, responsibility should be distributed according to established governance.

For example:

**System Owner**

→ Business and system accountability.

**CISO**

→ Cybersecurity oversight.

**SOC**

→ Detection.

**Incident Response**

→ Investigation and containment.

**Privacy**

→ Personal-data implications.

**Legal**

→ Legal implications.

**Risk**

→ Enterprise risk escalation.

This creates coordinated rather than fragmented response.

---

### 197. AI Accountability and Regulatory Examination

Regulators or external assessors may ask:

* Who owns this AI system?
* Who approved it?
* What risk assessment was performed?
* Who accepted the residual risk?
* Who monitors it?
* Who can stop it?
* What evidence exists?

A mature organization should be able to answer these questions with documented evidence.

---

### 198. AI Accountability Maturity

Accountability maturity can progress through several stages.

**Level 1 — Unclear**

AI ownership is largely informal.

**Level 2 — Defined**

Basic AI roles are documented.

**Level 3 — Managed**

Decision rights and accountability are integrated into governance processes.

**Level 4 — Measured**

Accountability performance is monitored and tested.

**Level 5 — Optimized**

Accountability continuously evolves with AI technology, organizational risk, and regulatory expectations.

---

### 199. Characteristics of Effective AI Accountability

An effective AI accountability model should be:

* Clear
* Documented
* Risk-based
* Proportionate
* Traceable
* Enforceable
* Auditable
* Independent where necessary
* Adaptable
* Integrated with enterprise governance

Most importantly, accountability should exist **before** an AI-related failure occurs.

---

### 200. Enterprise AI Accountability Model

A mature enterprise AI accountability structure can be viewed as a connected system:

**Board**

→ Oversight

**Executive Management**

→ Strategy and risk direction

**AI Governance**

→ Framework and coordination

**Business Owners**

→ Business accountability

**AI System Owners**

→ Lifecycle accountability

**Data Owners**

→ Data accountability

**Security**

→ Cybersecurity oversight and controls

**Privacy**

→ Privacy oversight

**Risk and Compliance**

→ Second-line challenge

**Users and Operators**

→ Operational execution

**Vendors and Providers**

→ Contractual and service obligations

**Internal Audit**

→ Independent assurance

The purpose of this structure is not to create unnecessary bureaucracy. Its purpose is to ensure that every significant AI decision, system, risk, action, and outcome has a **clearly identifiable owner, appropriate decision authority, effective oversight, and sufficient evidence of accountability**.
