# 01.12 — Board, Executive and Senior Management Responsibilities

## Part 1 — Foundations of Leadership Accountability for AI Governance

### 1. Introduction to Board and Executive Responsibilities

AI governance ultimately depends on effective leadership.

Boards, executives, and senior management establish the organizational environment in which AI is developed, acquired, deployed, monitored, and retired.

Their responsibilities include establishing:

* Strategic direction
* Accountability
* Risk appetite
* Governance structures
* Decision rights
* Resource allocation
* Oversight
* Organizational culture

AI governance should therefore be treated as a leadership responsibility rather than solely as a technology or cybersecurity function.

---

### 2. Why Leadership Accountability Matters

AI can affect areas that extend far beyond technology.

An AI system may influence:

* Customers
* Employees
* Financial decisions
* Operations
* Security
* Privacy
* Regulatory compliance
* Reputation
* Strategic decisions

Because these impacts can extend across the enterprise, leadership must ensure that AI risks are considered alongside broader organizational risks.

---

### 3. Board-Level AI Accountability

The Board has ultimate oversight responsibility for the organization's strategic direction and risk environment.

In relation to AI, the Board should have sufficient visibility into:

* AI strategy
* Significant AI risks
* Material AI investments
* Major AI incidents
* Regulatory exposure
* AI-related cybersecurity risks
* Significant third-party dependencies
* Governance maturity

The Board does not need to manage individual AI systems, but it should understand the organization's overall AI exposure.

---

### 4. Board Oversight Versus Management Responsibility

The Board and management have different responsibilities.

**Board**

→ Provides oversight, direction, and challenge.

**Executive Management**

→ Implements strategy and manages the organization.

**Operational Management**

→ Operates AI systems and manages associated risks.

This distinction prevents the Board from becoming involved in operational decisions that should remain with management.

---

### 5. Board AI Competence

Board members do not need to become AI engineers.

However, they should possess sufficient understanding to challenge management effectively.

Relevant knowledge includes:

* AI opportunities
* AI limitations
* AI risks
* Cybersecurity
* Privacy
* Regulatory exposure
* Model risk
* AI governance

The objective is informed oversight rather than technical specialization.

---

### 6. Board AI Literacy

AI literacy at Board level should enable directors to understand questions such as:

* Where is the organization using AI?
* What are the most significant AI risks?
* Which AI systems are business-critical?
* What information is being provided to AI systems?
* What decisions are influenced by AI?
* Where is AI being used autonomously?
* What happens when an AI system fails?

These questions help the Board exercise meaningful oversight.

---

### 7. Board Oversight of AI Strategy

The Board should understand how AI supports organizational strategy.

Oversight may include:

* AI investment priorities
* Business transformation
* Competitive advantage
* Innovation
* Operational efficiency
* Risk reduction

AI adoption should support the organization's strategic objectives rather than become technology adoption for its own sake.

---

### 8. Board Oversight of AI Risk

The Board should understand the organization's most significant AI risks.

These may include:

* Cybersecurity risk
* Privacy risk
* Regulatory risk
* Operational risk
* Strategic risk
* Third-party risk
* Reputational risk
* Safety risk

Board reporting should focus on material risks rather than overwhelming directors with technical detail.

---

### 9. Board and AI Risk Appetite

The Board may establish or approve the organization's overall risk appetite.

AI-related risk appetite may define boundaries concerning:

* High-risk AI applications
* Autonomous AI
* Sensitive data
* Regulatory exposure
* Critical operations
* Third-party dependency

Management should translate these principles into operational requirements.

---

### 10. Board Oversight of AI Governance Framework

The Board should receive assurance that an appropriate AI governance framework exists.

The framework may include:

* Policies
* Standards
* Roles
* Risk management
* Controls
* Approval processes
* Monitoring
* Assurance

The Board should ask whether the framework is operating effectively rather than simply whether documentation exists.

---

### 11. Board Oversight of AI Governance Operating Model

The Board should understand how AI governance responsibilities are distributed.

This may include:

* Board
* Executive management
* AI governance
* CISO
* CIO
* CTO
* CRO
* DPO
* Business leadership
* Internal Audit

Clear responsibility reduces the possibility of governance gaps.

---

### 12. Board Oversight of AI Decision Rights

Leadership should ensure that important AI decisions have clearly defined decision rights.

Decision rights may cover:

* AI adoption
* High-risk classification
* Deployment
* Risk acceptance
* Exceptions
* Autonomous operation
* Material changes
* Retirement

Decision authority should correspond to risk and organizational accountability.

---

### 13. Board Oversight of High-Risk AI

The Board should receive appropriate information about high-risk AI systems.

Reporting may include:

* Number of high-risk systems
* Business functions affected
* Significant risks
* Control status
* Exceptions
* Incidents
* Regulatory exposure

High-risk AI should receive greater leadership attention than routine, low-risk AI use.

---

### 14. Board Oversight of AI Incidents

Material AI incidents should be escalated to appropriate leadership.

Examples include:

* Significant data exposure
* Major security compromise
* Regulatory violation
* Material financial loss
* Significant customer harm
* Critical operational disruption
* Serious AI safety event

The Board should understand both the incident and the organization's response.

---

### 15. Board Oversight of AI Resilience

AI-dependent business processes should be considered in organizational resilience planning.

The Board may ask:

* Which critical processes depend on AI?
* What happens if the AI service becomes unavailable?
* Are manual alternatives available?
* Are critical vendors replaceable?
* Can systems be recovered?
* Have recovery plans been tested?

AI resilience is part of enterprise resilience.

---

### 16. Board Oversight of Third-Party AI

Third-party AI services can create significant strategic and operational dependencies.

The Board should understand material exposure to:

* Foundation model providers
* Cloud providers
* AI SaaS providers
* Data providers
* Specialized AI vendors

Particular attention may be required where there is significant concentration risk.

---

### 17. Board Oversight of AI Vendor Concentration

An organization may become dependent on a small number of AI providers.

Concentration risk can arise when multiple business functions depend on:

* The same model provider
* The same cloud platform
* The same AI API
* The same data provider

Leadership should understand the consequences of provider disruption or major contractual changes.

---

### 18. Board Oversight of AI Regulatory Risk

AI regulation is evolving across jurisdictions.

The Board should receive appropriate information about:

* Applicable requirements
* Regulatory changes
* Compliance exposure
* Material regulatory investigations
* Significant compliance gaps

Management should maintain mechanisms for translating regulatory requirements into operational controls.

---

### 19. Board Oversight of AI Cybersecurity

AI introduces cybersecurity risks that may require Board attention.

Examples include:

* Prompt injection
* Data leakage
* Model manipulation
* AI-enabled attacks
* Unauthorized AI usage
* Agent misuse
* Supply-chain vulnerabilities

The Board should understand how AI affects the organization's broader cybersecurity risk profile.

---

### 20. Board Oversight of AI Privacy

Where AI processes personal information, the Board should understand significant privacy exposure.

Oversight may include:

* Sensitive data use
* Privacy incidents
* Regulatory exposure
* Data governance
* Third-party processing
* Privacy risk management

Privacy should be considered alongside security and broader AI governance.

---

### 21. Board Oversight of AI Ethics and Responsible AI

Leadership should consider the broader consequences of AI deployment.

Potential areas include:

* Fairness
* Transparency
* Human oversight
* Accountability
* Potential discrimination
* Explainability
* Social impact

Responsible AI should be connected to enterprise risk management rather than treated solely as a public-relations activity.

---

### 22. Board Oversight of AI Culture

Leadership sets expectations for how employees use AI.

The Board should encourage a culture where employees:

* Use approved AI tools
* Protect organizational information
* Report concerns
* Follow governance requirements
* Understand AI limitations
* Escalate significant risks

A weak AI culture can undermine even well-designed technical controls.

---

### 23. Board Oversight of AI Literacy

Organizations need appropriate AI literacy at different levels.

Board and executive education may cover:

* AI capabilities
* AI limitations
* AI risks
* Governance
* Security
* Privacy
* Regulatory developments

The level of education should correspond to leadership responsibilities.

---

### 24. Executive Management Responsibility

Executive management translates Board direction into operational reality.

Executives are responsible for:

* Implementing AI strategy
* Establishing governance
* Allocating resources
* Assigning accountability
* Managing risk
* Monitoring performance
* Escalating material issues

Executives therefore serve as the bridge between Board oversight and operational execution.

---

### 25. Executive AI Strategy

Executive leadership should establish how AI will be used across the enterprise.

Strategic decisions may include:

* Where AI should be adopted
* Where AI should not be used
* Which capabilities should be centralized
* Which should be decentralized
* Which AI investments should be prioritized

Strategy should consider both opportunity and risk.

---

### 26. Executive AI Governance Sponsorship

An executive sponsor should provide organizational support for AI governance.

The sponsor may:

* Establish authority
* Resolve conflicts
* Secure resources
* Support governance committees
* Escalate major issues
* Reinforce accountability

Without executive sponsorship, AI governance may remain fragmented.

---

### 27. Executive Accountability for AI Risk

Executives should ensure that material AI risks are actively managed.

They should understand:

* Risk exposure
* Risk appetite
* Significant exceptions
* Material incidents
* Control weaknesses
* Remediation progress

AI risk should be integrated into existing executive risk-management processes.

---

### 28. Executive AI Governance Committee

Organizations may establish an AI Governance Committee or equivalent management forum.

Membership may include:

* Business leadership
* CIO
* CTO
* CISO
* CRO
* DPO
* Legal
* Compliance
* AI leadership

The committee should have clearly defined authority and responsibilities.

---

### 29. Executive AI Governance Committee Responsibilities

The committee may:

* Review high-risk AI
* Approve governance standards
* Review significant risks
* Review exceptions
* Resolve cross-functional conflicts
* Review incidents
* Monitor governance maturity

The committee should not replace operational ownership.

---

### 30. Executive Resource Allocation

Effective governance requires adequate resources.

Executives should ensure availability of:

* Skilled personnel
* Security capabilities
* Governance tools
* Monitoring
* Training
* Audit support
* Compliance resources

AI governance without adequate resources may exist formally but fail operationally.

---

### 31. Executive Accountability for AI Talent

AI governance requires appropriate expertise.

Executives should ensure access to skills in:

* AI
* Cybersecurity
* Data
* Privacy
* Risk
* Compliance
* Legal
* Internal Audit

Skills should reflect the organization's AI risk profile.

---

### 32. Executive Oversight of AI Portfolio

Executives should maintain visibility across the organization's AI portfolio.

Portfolio oversight can include:

* Number of AI systems
* Business value
* Risk levels
* Investment
* Critical dependencies
* Incidents
* Governance status

This prevents AI adoption from becoming fragmented across departments.

---

### 33. Senior Management Responsibilities

Senior management translates governance requirements into day-to-day business execution.

Responsibilities may include:

* Implementing policies
* Assigning owners
* Managing risks
* Monitoring controls
* Reporting issues
* Managing exceptions
* Supporting remediation

Senior management is generally closest to the operational consequences of AI decisions.

---

### 34. Senior Management AI Ownership

Each significant AI capability should have an accountable management owner.

The owner should understand:

* Purpose
* Risk
* Controls
* Dependencies
* Performance
* Compliance
* Lifecycle status

Ownership should remain clear even when multiple departments contribute to the system.

---

### 35. Senior Management AI Control Environment

Management should establish an environment where AI controls are:

* Defined
* Implemented
* Tested
* Monitored
* Improved

Control responsibility should be assigned to specific individuals or teams.

---

### 36. Senior Management and AI Exceptions

Management should ensure that exceptions are:

* Justified
* Documented
* Approved
* Time-limited
* Monitored

Repeated exceptions should trigger reassessment of the underlying governance or control design.

---

### 37. Senior Management and AI Incidents

Senior management should ensure that AI incidents receive appropriate attention.

Responsibilities may include:

* Escalation
* Business decisions
* Resource allocation
* Risk acceptance
* Communications
* Recovery priorities

Incident response should align with the organization's broader crisis-management structure.

---

### 38. Senior Management and AI Performance

AI governance should not focus exclusively on risk.

Senior management should also evaluate:

* Business value
* Accuracy
* Efficiency
* Reliability
* User adoption
* Strategic outcomes

AI systems that create insufficient value may require modification or retirement even when they are technically secure.

---

### 39. Leadership Balance Between Innovation and Control

Leadership must balance two objectives:

**Enable AI Innovation**

→ Allow the organization to capture legitimate business value.

**Control AI Risk**

→ Prevent unacceptable security, privacy, operational, regulatory, and reputational exposure.

Effective AI governance should enable responsible innovation rather than simply restrict AI adoption.

---

### 40. Leadership Accountability as the Foundation of AI Governance

The Board provides **oversight and strategic direction**.

Executive management provides **leadership, resources, and enterprise implementation**.

Senior management provides **operational accountability and risk ownership**.

Together, these leadership layers establish the foundation upon which the organization's AI governance structure operates.


# 01.12 — Board, Executive and Senior Management Responsibilities

## Part 2 — Board Responsibilities for AI Governance, Risk and Oversight

### 41. Board Responsibility for AI Governance

The Board should provide appropriate oversight of how the organization governs AI.

This includes ensuring that management has established:

* Clear AI accountability
* Appropriate governance structures
* Effective risk management
* Adequate internal controls
* Appropriate reporting
* Independent assurance

The Board should focus on whether AI governance is effective at the enterprise level rather than managing individual AI implementations.

---

### 42. Board Oversight of AI Strategy

The Board should understand how AI contributes to the organization's strategy.

Board oversight may consider:

* Strategic objectives
* AI investment
* Business transformation
* Competitive positioning
* Operational efficiency
* Innovation
* Strategic dependencies

AI strategy should be aligned with the organization's broader business strategy.

---

### 43. Board Approval of Material AI Strategy

Depending on the organization's governance structure, the Board may approve or provide oversight of significant AI strategies and investments.

Material decisions may involve:

* Enterprise AI transformation
* Major AI platforms
* Significant acquisitions
* Critical AI dependencies
* Major AI-enabled business models

The threshold for Board involvement should be defined by governance policy and delegated authority.

---

### 44. Board Understanding of AI Portfolio Exposure

The Board should have visibility into the organization's overall AI portfolio.

Portfolio reporting may include:

* Number of AI systems
* Critical AI systems
* High-risk AI systems
* Major vendors
* Significant AI investments
* Material incidents
* Major regulatory exposures

Portfolio-level visibility is more useful than receiving disconnected reports about individual systems.

---

### 45. Board Oversight of AI Risk Appetite

The Board should ensure that AI risk appetite is appropriately established.

Risk appetite may address:

* High-risk AI
* Autonomous AI
* Sensitive data
* Critical infrastructure
* Customer-facing AI
* Regulatory exposure
* Third-party dependency

Management should translate Board-approved risk appetite into operational thresholds.

---

### 46. Board Oversight of AI Risk Management

The Board should receive assurance that significant AI risks are identified and managed.

Board reporting should provide visibility into:

* Inherent risk
* Controls
* Residual risk
* Risk trends
* Risk appetite
* Significant exceptions

The Board should challenge management when risk exposure is unclear or appears inconsistent with organizational objectives.

---

### 47. Board Challenge of AI Risk

Effective Board oversight requires meaningful challenge.

Directors may ask:

* What are our most significant AI risks?
* Which risks are increasing?
* What controls address them?
* What risks have been accepted?
* Who accepted them?
* What happens if those controls fail?
* Are we within our risk appetite?

Questions should focus on materiality and business impact.

---

### 48. Board Oversight of AI Cybersecurity

AI-related cybersecurity should be integrated into Board-level cybersecurity oversight.

Areas may include:

* AI attack surfaces
* Data leakage
* Prompt injection
* Model manipulation
* AI-enabled attacks
* Agent security
* Third-party AI dependencies
* Unauthorized AI use

The Board should understand how AI changes the organization's overall cyber risk profile.

---

### 49. Board Oversight of AI Security Governance

The Board should receive assurance that AI security governance is integrated with the enterprise security program.

This may include:

* Security policies
* AI security standards
* Risk assessments
* Security testing
* Monitoring
* Incident response
* Security assurance

AI security should not become an isolated governance discipline disconnected from cybersecurity.

---

### 50. Board Oversight of AI Privacy

Where AI processes personal or sensitive information, privacy risk may require Board visibility.

Relevant areas include:

* Personal data use
* Sensitive data
* Cross-border processing
* Data retention
* Privacy incidents
* Regulatory exposure
* Third-party processing

The Board should understand material privacy risks without becoming involved in routine privacy assessments.

---

### 51. Board Oversight of Responsible AI

The Board should consider whether AI deployment aligns with organizational values and responsible-use principles.

This may include:

* Fairness
* Transparency
* Accountability
* Human oversight
* Explainability
* Potential discrimination
* Social impact

Responsible AI should be treated as part of enterprise governance and risk management.

---

### 52. Board Oversight of AI Safety

For AI systems capable of causing physical or significant operational harm, the Board should understand safety risks.

Examples may include AI used in:

* Industrial environments
* Healthcare
* Transportation
* Critical infrastructure
* Autonomous systems

The level of Board oversight should correspond to potential impact.

---

### 53. Board Oversight of High-Impact AI Decisions

AI may influence decisions involving:

* Employment
* Credit
* Insurance
* Customers
* Security
* Access
* Benefits
* Other significant outcomes

The Board should ensure that management has appropriate governance over high-impact AI decisions.

---

### 54. Board Oversight of Human Oversight

The Board should understand where human oversight is required.

Questions may include:

* Which decisions require human approval?
* Can humans override AI outputs?
* Who is accountable for final decisions?
* What happens when the AI behaves unexpectedly?

Human oversight should be appropriate to the consequences of AI decisions.

---

### 55. Board Oversight of AI Autonomy

Autonomous AI introduces additional governance considerations.

The Board should understand where AI systems can:

* Make decisions
* Execute actions
* Access systems
* Use tools
* Modify data
* Initiate transactions

Greater autonomy may require stronger controls and greater management oversight.

---

### 56. Board Oversight of Agentic AI

Agentic AI can act across multiple systems and perform sequences of actions.

Board-level questions may include:

* What authority do agents have?
* What systems can they access?
* What actions can they perform?
* What prevents unauthorized actions?
* Can their activity be stopped?
* Is activity logged and monitored?

Agentic AI should operate within clearly defined authority boundaries.

---

### 57. Board Oversight of AI Third-Party Risk

Third-party AI can create dependencies that are difficult to control directly.

Board oversight may consider:

* Critical vendors
* Foundation model providers
* Cloud providers
* AI SaaS providers
* Data providers
* Outsourced AI operations

Material dependencies should be visible at the enterprise level.

---

### 58. Board Oversight of AI Concentration Risk

Concentration may arise when multiple AI services depend on the same:

* Model provider
* Cloud provider
* Infrastructure
* Data source
* AI platform

The Board should understand whether a failure involving one provider could affect multiple business functions.

---

### 59. Board Oversight of AI Supply Chain Risk

AI supply chains can include:

* Foundation models
* Datasets
* Open-source components
* APIs
* Cloud services
* Software libraries
* Vendors
* Subprocessors

The Board should receive appropriate visibility into significant supply-chain exposure.

---

### 60. Board Oversight of AI Regulatory Compliance

The Board should understand significant regulatory obligations affecting AI.

Oversight may include:

* Applicable regulations
* Regulatory changes
* Compliance status
* Material findings
* Investigations
* Enforcement exposure

Management should be responsible for implementing the required controls.

---

### 61. Board Oversight of Regulatory Change

AI regulatory requirements can evolve rapidly.

The Board should receive information about material changes that may affect:

* Strategy
* Risk
* Investments
* Products
* Operations
* Compliance

Regulatory change should be incorporated into the organization's broader governance processes.

---

### 62. Board Oversight of AI Incidents

Material AI incidents should be escalated according to defined criteria.

Examples may include:

* Significant security breaches
* Major privacy incidents
* Regulatory violations
* Significant financial losses
* Critical operational failures
* Serious customer harm
* Significant AI safety events

The Board should understand the cause, impact, response, and remediation.

---

### 63. Board Oversight of AI Incident Preparedness

The Board should ensure that management is prepared for significant AI incidents.

Preparedness may include:

* Incident response plans
* Crisis management
* Escalation procedures
* Communications
* Business continuity
* Recovery
* Regulatory notification

Preparedness should be tested rather than assumed.

---

### 64. Board Oversight of AI Resilience

AI-dependent business processes should be incorporated into enterprise resilience planning.

The Board may ask:

* Which AI services are critical?
* What happens if they become unavailable?
* Are alternative providers available?
* Are manual processes available?
* Can the organization recover quickly?

AI resilience should be considered part of operational resilience.

---

### 65. Board Oversight of AI Business Continuity

Where AI supports critical processes, continuity plans should identify:

* AI dependencies
* Recovery priorities
* Manual alternatives
* Recovery time objectives
* Recovery point objectives
* Key vendors

The Board should receive appropriate assurance that critical AI dependencies have been considered.

---

### 66. Board Oversight of AI Disaster Recovery

Disaster recovery arrangements may need to account for:

* AI models
* Data
* Configurations
* Applications
* APIs
* Infrastructure
* Credentials
* External dependencies

Recovery capability should be tested periodically.

---

### 67. Board Oversight of AI Investments

Significant AI investments should be evaluated from both business and risk perspectives.

Board-level considerations may include:

* Expected business value
* Strategic alignment
* Risk exposure
* Total cost
* Vendor dependency
* Regulatory requirements
* Long-term sustainability

Investment decisions should not focus exclusively on technology capability.

---

### 68. Board Oversight of AI Business Value

AI governance should help ensure that AI investments produce legitimate organizational value.

Board reporting may include:

* Expected benefits
* Realized benefits
* Adoption
* Performance
* Cost
* Risk

AI systems that consistently fail to deliver expected value may require reassessment.

---

### 69. Board Oversight of AI Performance

Performance reporting should provide an appropriate balance between technical and business information.

Relevant measures may include:

* Accuracy
* Reliability
* Availability
* User adoption
* Business outcomes
* Cost
* Incident trends

The Board should focus on material performance issues.

---

### 70. Board Oversight of AI Governance Maturity

The Board should understand the maturity of the organization's AI governance capabilities.

Maturity may be assessed across:

* Governance
* Risk
* Security
* Privacy
* Data
* Lifecycle management
* Third-party risk
* Assurance
* Culture

Maturity assessments should lead to actionable improvement plans.

---

### 71. Board Oversight of AI Internal Controls

The Board should receive assurance that important AI controls are appropriately designed and operating.

Sources of assurance may include:

* Management reporting
* Second-line monitoring
* Internal Audit
* External assurance
* Independent assessments

The Board should understand significant control deficiencies.

---

### 72. Board Oversight of Internal Audit AI Assurance

Internal Audit should provide independent assurance over significant AI risks.

The Board or Audit Committee should understand:

* AI audit coverage
* Significant findings
* Control weaknesses
* Remediation status
* Emerging AI risks

Internal Audit should retain its independence.

---

### 73. Board Oversight of AI Audit Findings

Significant AI audit findings should receive appropriate Board or Audit Committee attention.

Examples include:

* Material governance gaps
* Repeated control failures
* Significant unauthorized AI use
* Unresolved high-risk findings
* Weak risk acceptance processes

Management should be accountable for remediation.

---

### 74. Board Oversight of AI Remediation

The Board should monitor significant remediation programs.

Reporting may include:

* Finding
* Risk
* Responsible executive
* Corrective action
* Target date
* Current status
* Residual risk

Persistent delays should trigger appropriate escalation.

---

### 75. Board Oversight of AI Exceptions

Material or recurring exceptions should be visible to leadership.

Reporting may identify:

* Number of exceptions
* Risk level
* Business areas
* Duration
* Compensating controls
* Expired exceptions

A high volume of exceptions may indicate that governance requirements are unrealistic or poorly implemented.

---

### 76. Board Oversight of AI Risk Acceptance

The Board may establish thresholds for which risks require Board-level approval.

For example:

* Material financial risk
* Significant regulatory risk
* Critical operational risk
* Significant customer impact
* Strategic risk

Routine risk acceptance should remain delegated to appropriate management levels.

---

### 77. Board Oversight of AI Reporting

Board reporting should be:

* Concise
* Risk-focused
* Decision-oriented
* Trend-based
* Material
* Understandable

Technical complexity should be translated into business and risk implications.

---

### 78. Board AI Dashboard

A Board-level AI dashboard may include:

| Area         | Example Metric                   |
| ------------ | -------------------------------- |
| AI Portfolio | Number of material AI systems    |
| Risk         | High-risk AI exposure            |
| Security     | Significant AI security findings |
| Privacy      | Material privacy incidents       |

| Third Party  | Critical AI dependencies         |

| Governance   | Assessment completion            |

The dashboard should support oversight rather than become an operational reporting tool.

---

### 79. Board AI Governance Questions

Effective Board oversight may be strengthened by consistently asking:

1. Where are we using AI?
2. What are our most significant AI risks?
3. Who owns those risks?
4. Are we within our risk appetite?
5. What controls protect us?
6. Where have we accepted residual risk?
7. What significant incidents have occurred?
8. What regulatory changes affect us?
9. How dependent are we on AI providers?
10. What independent assurance do we have?

These questions help maintain enterprise-level visibility.

---

### 80. Board Accountability for AI Governance

The Board's role can be summarized as:

**Oversee**

→ Ensure effective AI governance exists.

**Challenge**

→ Question significant AI risks and management decisions.

**Approve**

→ Approve matters within Board authority.

**Monitor**

→ Review material AI risk and performance.

**Assure**

→ Obtain appropriate assurance.

**Escalate**

→ Address matters exceeding delegated management authority.

The Board therefore provides the organization's highest level of **strategic oversight, risk governance, accountability, and challenge for material AI-related matters**.

# 01.12 — Board, Executive and Senior Management Responsibilities

## Part 3 — Executive Management and Senior Management Responsibilities

### 81. Executive Management Accountability for AI

Executive management translates Board direction into an operational AI governance environment.

Executives are responsible for ensuring that:

* AI strategy is implemented
* Governance structures are functioning
* AI risks are managed
* Resources are available
* Responsibilities are assigned
* Significant issues are escalated
* Governance performance is monitored

Executive accountability connects strategic oversight with operational execution.

---

### 82. Chief Executive Officer and AI Governance

The CEO has overall executive accountability for ensuring that AI supports organizational objectives while remaining within acceptable risk boundaries.

The CEO may be responsible for:

* Establishing executive sponsorship
* Aligning AI with corporate strategy
* Resolving major cross-functional conflicts
* Allocating executive attention
* Escalating material AI risks to the Board

The CEO does not need to manage individual AI systems.

---

### 83. Executive AI Strategy

Executive management should establish an enterprise AI strategy that addresses both opportunity and risk.

The strategy may define:

* AI objectives
* Priority use cases
* Investment areas
* Risk boundaries
* Governance expectations
* Operating model
* Capability requirements

AI strategy should be integrated with the organization's broader business and technology strategy.

---

### 84. Executive AI Governance Sponsorship

Executive sponsorship provides authority and organizational support for AI governance.

The executive sponsor may:

* Establish governance authority
* Resolve organizational conflicts
* Secure resources
* Support governance committees
* Escalate significant issues
* Reinforce accountability

Strong sponsorship helps prevent AI governance from becoming fragmented.

---

### 85. Executive Responsibility for AI Risk

Executives should ensure that material AI risks are identified, assessed, treated, and monitored.

Executive risk oversight may include:

* Risk exposure
* Risk appetite
* Significant exceptions
* Control weaknesses
* Material incidents
* Remediation

AI risk should be integrated into enterprise risk management.

---

### 86. Executive AI Risk Appetite Implementation

The Board may establish the organization's overall risk appetite, while executives translate it into operational boundaries.

These boundaries may define:

* Prohibited AI uses
* Restricted AI uses
* High-risk approval requirements
* Autonomous AI limitations
* Sensitive-data restrictions
* Third-party requirements

This creates a connection between Board-level risk appetite and operational decision-making.

---

### 87. Executive AI Governance Committee

An organization may establish an executive AI governance committee to coordinate significant AI decisions.

Membership may include representatives from:

* Business
* Technology
* Cybersecurity
* Risk
* Privacy
* Legal
* Compliance
* Data
* AI leadership

# 01.12 — Board, Executive and Senior Management Responsibilities

## Part 4 — Leadership Coordination, Accountability, Assurance and Governance Effectiveness

### 121. Coordinated Leadership Accountability

Effective AI governance requires coordinated accountability across the Board, executive management, and senior management.

Each level has a distinct role:

**Board**

→ Provides strategic oversight, challenge, and governance direction.

**Executive Management**

→ Translates strategy into enterprise-wide governance and resource decisions.

**Senior Management**

→ Implements governance and manages AI risks within business and operational areas.

Clear separation prevents both accountability gaps and unnecessary duplication.

---

### 122. Board–Executive Information Flow

The Board requires reliable information from executive management to exercise effective oversight.

Information should include:

* Material AI risks
* Significant incidents
* Risk appetite breaches
* Major exceptions
* Regulatory exposure
* Significant investments
* Critical third-party dependencies
* Major audit findings

Information should be presented in a manner that enables decisions rather than simply reporting activity.

---

### 123. Executive–Senior Management Information Flow

Executives depend on senior management for detailed information about AI operations.

Senior management should report:

* AI portfolio changes
* Operational risks
* Control weaknesses
* Incidents
* Exceptions
* Performance
* Remediation
* Emerging risks

This creates a structured flow from operational activity to enterprise-level decision-making.

Senior management should escalate matters that exceed their authority or risk tolerance.

Escalation triggers may include:

* Risk appetite breaches
* Material incidents

* Major regulatory concerns
* Significant customer impact
* Critical vendor failures
* Unresolved high-risk issues

Escalation criteria should be defined in advance.

Examples may include:

* Major strategic AI decisions

* Material reputational impact

### 129. Leadership Accountability for Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance should occur at the level appropriate to the magnitude of the risk.

A simplified structure may be:

**Low Risk**

→ Operational management.

**Moderate Risk**

**Material Enterprise Risk**

The thresholds should be formally defined.
