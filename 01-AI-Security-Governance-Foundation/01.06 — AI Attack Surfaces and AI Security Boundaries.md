# 01.06 — AI Attack Surfaces and AI Security Boundaries

## Part 1 — Foundations of the AI Attack Surface and Security Boundary

### 1. Introduction

AI systems introduce a broader and more dynamic attack surface than many traditional information systems.

A conventional application may primarily expose:

* User interfaces
* APIs
* Network services
* Databases
* Operating systems
* Applications
* Infrastructure

An AI system can expose all of these components while also introducing additional attack surfaces involving:

* Models
* Prompts
* Training data
* Fine-tuning data
* Retrieval systems
* Embeddings
* Vector databases
* AI agents
* Plugins
* External tools
* Model APIs
* AI supply chains
* AI development environments

Understanding these attack surfaces is fundamental to AI Security Governance.

---

### 2. What Is an Attack Surface?

An **attack surface** is the collection of points through which an attacker may attempt to:

* Enter a system
* Influence system behavior
* Access information
* Modify data
* Obtain privileges
* Disrupt operations
* Manipulate decisions
* Exfiltrate information

In AI environments, the attack surface includes both traditional technical components and AI-specific components.

---

### 3. What Is an AI Attack Surface?

An **AI attack surface** is the collection of technical, data, human, organizational, and external interaction points through which an AI system may be attacked, manipulated, compromised, or misused.

This includes the entire AI ecosystem rather than only the model itself.

A useful conceptual representation is:

**Users**

↓

**AI Application**

↓

**AI Model**

↓

**Data**

↓

**Tools and APIs**

↓

**Enterprise Systems**

↓

**External Providers**

Each layer can introduce security exposure.

---

### 4. AI Attack Surface Is Larger Than the Model

One of the most important governance principles is:

> **The AI model is only one component of the AI attack surface.**

Protecting the model while ignoring:

* Prompts
* Data
* APIs
* Identity
* Tools
* Infrastructure
* Users

does not provide complete AI security.

---

### 5. What Is a Security Boundary?

A **security boundary** defines the point at which security controls separate trusted resources from untrusted or differently trusted resources.

Examples in traditional environments include:

* Internet ↔ Corporate Network
* User ↔ Application
* Application ↔ Database
* Public Cloud ↔ Private Network

AI systems introduce additional boundaries.

---

### 6. AI Security Boundaries

AI security boundaries may exist between:

* User and AI application
* AI application and model
* Model and retrieved data
* AI system and external tools
* AI agent and enterprise systems
* Organization and AI provider
* Training environment and production environment

Each boundary requires appropriate controls.

---

### 7. AI Security Boundaries Are Not Always Network Boundaries

Traditional cybersecurity often emphasizes network segmentation.

AI security boundaries may exist at the level of:

* Data
* Instructions
* Identity
* Authorization
* Model behavior
* Tool access
* Trust
* Decision-making

For example, a retrieved document may cross a trust boundary even though it is delivered through an approved network connection.

---

### 8. The AI Trust Boundary

A **trust boundary** represents a point where information or control moves between entities with different levels of trust.

For AI systems, examples include:

**Untrusted User**

→ **AI Application**

or:

**External Web Content**

→ **Retrieval System**

→ **AI Model**

or:

**AI Agent**

→ **Enterprise Database**

Each transition should be evaluated for potential abuse.

---

### 9. The Expanding AI Security Boundary

A simple AI application may involve:

**User**

→ **Application**

→ **Model**

As capabilities increase, the architecture may become:

**User**

→ **Application**

→ **Model**

→ **Retrieval**

→ **Enterprise Data**

→ **Tools**

→ **External APIs**

→ **External Services**

The security boundary expands with every additional capability.

---

### 10. AI Attack Surface Categories

AI attack surfaces can be organized into several major categories:

1. User attack surface
2. Application attack surface
3. Model attack surface
4. Prompt attack surface
5. Data attack surface
6. Training attack surface
7. Retrieval attack surface
8. Agent attack surface
9. Tool and API attack surface
10. Infrastructure attack surface
11. Supply-chain attack surface
12. Third-party attack surface
13. Governance attack surface

This classification helps organizations develop a comprehensive security model.

---

### 11. User Attack Surface

Users are often the first interaction point with an AI system.

Potential risks include:

* Malicious prompts
* Accidental disclosure
* Credential misuse
* Social engineering
* Unauthorized AI usage
* Abuse of AI capabilities

Users therefore remain an important component of AI security.

---

### 12. Application Attack Surface

The AI application provides the interface between users and AI capabilities.

It may contain:

* Web interfaces
* Mobile applications
* APIs
* Authentication mechanisms
* Session management
* Business logic
* Integration components

Traditional application-security vulnerabilities remain relevant.

---

### 13. Model Attack Surface

The model itself can become a security target.

Potential risks include:

* Model theft
* Model manipulation
* Model poisoning
* Model extraction
* Adversarial manipulation
* Unsafe behavior
* Information leakage

The security requirements depend on the model's role and sensitivity.

---

### 14. Prompt Attack Surface

Prompts are a unique AI security boundary.

They may contain:

* User instructions
* System instructions
* Developer instructions
* Retrieved content
* Tool outputs
* Conversation history

Attackers may attempt to manipulate this instruction environment.

---

### 15. System Prompt Exposure

System instructions may contain information about:

* AI behavior
* Security restrictions
* Application architecture
* Tool capabilities
* Internal processes

Organizations should carefully consider whether revealing system instructions could facilitate attacks.

---

### 16. Prompt Injection Boundary

Prompt injection occurs when untrusted content influences AI instructions.

The boundary may be:

**Trusted Instructions**

↓

**Untrusted Content**

↓

**AI Model**

The security challenge is ensuring that untrusted content does not override or improperly influence trusted instructions.

---

### 17. Training Data Attack Surface

Training data can influence model behavior.

Potential threats include:

* Data poisoning
* Malicious records
* Manipulated datasets
* Unauthorized data
* Sensitive information
* Poor-quality data

Training-data governance is therefore part of AI security governance.

---

### 18. Fine-Tuning Data Attack Surface

Fine-tuning introduces another data boundary.

Organizations may use internal datasets to modify a foundation model.

Risks include:

* Unauthorized data
* Poisoned data
* Sensitive information
* Poor-quality data
* Improperly controlled datasets

Fine-tuning data should be governed according to its sensitivity and intended purpose.

---

### 19. Data Ingestion Attack Surface

AI systems often ingest information from multiple sources.

Examples include:

* Databases
* Documents
* Websites
* Emails
* APIs
* File systems
* Knowledge bases

Each ingestion point creates an opportunity for malicious or uncontrolled content to enter the AI environment.

---

### 20. Retrieval-Augmented Generation Attack Surface

Retrieval-Augmented Generation, commonly known as **RAG**, allows an AI system to retrieve information from external repositories before generating a response.

A typical flow is:

**User**

↓

**AI Application**

↓

**Retriever**

↓

**Knowledge Repository**

↓

**Retrieved Content**

↓

**AI Model**

RAG improves contextual accuracy but introduces additional attack surfaces.

---

### 21. Retrieval Data Poisoning

Attackers may attempt to insert malicious information into a knowledge repository.

If the AI retrieves that information, the malicious content may influence its response.

Potential sources include:

* Compromised documents
* Malicious web pages
* Unauthorized database records
* Manipulated knowledge articles

---

### 22. Vector Database Attack Surface

RAG systems frequently use vector databases or vector stores.

These systems may contain:

* Embeddings
* Document references
* Metadata
* Sensitive business information

Security concerns include:

* Unauthorized access
* Data leakage
* Manipulation
* Excessive permissions
* Insecure APIs

---

### 23. Embedding Attack Surface

Embeddings convert information into numerical representations that can be used for similarity searches.

Security risks may involve:

* Unauthorized access
* Manipulation
* Data leakage
* Poisoned content
* Incorrect retrieval

Embedding systems should therefore be treated as security-sensitive components.

---

### 24. AI Agent Attack Surface

AI agents introduce additional attack surfaces because they may:

* Plan actions
* Call tools
* Access data
* Execute commands
* Interact with applications

The attack surface therefore expands from **information processing** to **action execution**.

---

### 25. Agent Planning Attack Surface

An AI agent may generate a plan based on:

* User instructions
* Retrieved information
* Model reasoning
* Tool results

An attacker may attempt to manipulate the information used by the agent so that it generates an unsafe plan.

---

### 26. Agent Execution Attack Surface

The most significant risk may occur when an AI agent executes an action.

Examples include:

* Sending an email
* Modifying a database
* Creating a user
* Executing code
* Changing cloud configuration

Execution privileges should therefore be tightly controlled.

---

### 27. Tool Attack Surface

AI agents may use tools such as:

* Search engines
* Databases
* APIs
* File systems
* Code interpreters
* Cloud services

Each tool introduces a new security boundary.

---

### 28. API Attack Surface

AI systems frequently communicate through APIs.

Examples include:

* Model APIs
* Retrieval APIs
* Enterprise APIs
* External service APIs

Traditional API security controls remain essential, including:

* Authentication
* Authorization
* Rate limiting
* Input validation
* Logging
* Monitoring

---

### 29. Plugin Attack Surface

Plugins can extend AI capabilities.

A plugin may provide access to:

* External services
* Enterprise applications
* Databases
* Files
* Search systems

Plugins should therefore be evaluated as privileged integrations rather than ordinary extensions.

---

### 30. External Content Attack Surface

AI systems that browse or retrieve external content can encounter untrusted information.

Potential sources include:

* Websites
* Emails
* Public documents
* Social media
* External knowledge bases

External content should not automatically be considered trustworthy merely because the AI system successfully retrieved it.

## Part 2 — AI Attack Surface Components and Threat Exposure

### 31. Data Access Attack Surface

AI systems frequently require access to organizational data.

Potential data sources include:

* Databases
* File repositories
* Cloud storage
* Document management systems
* Email
* Customer systems
* Enterprise applications
* Security platforms

Every data connection expands the AI attack surface.

---

### 32. Excessive Data Access

An AI system may receive access to more information than necessary.

For example, an internal AI assistant may have access to an entire document repository when it only needs access to a specific department's information.

Excessive access increases the potential impact of:

* Prompt injection
* Compromised accounts
* Misconfigured applications
* Malicious users
* AI-generated data disclosure

---

### 33. Data Authorization Boundary

AI systems should enforce authorization before retrieving information.

A simplified model is:

**User Identity**

↓

**Authorization Policy**

↓

**Permitted Data**

↓

**AI System**

The AI model should not become a mechanism for bypassing existing data-access controls.

---

### 34. Retrieval Authorization

RAG systems create a specific authorization challenge.

A user may ask:

> "Show me all documents related to this customer."

The retrieval system must determine whether the user is authorized to access those documents before returning them to the model.

Authorization must occur at the appropriate data-access layer.

---

### 35. Cross-User Data Exposure

Multi-user AI systems may create risks if context or retrieved information is improperly isolated.

Potential consequences include:

* One user's information appearing in another user's response
* Cross-tenant data exposure
* Session contamination
* Unauthorized retrieval

Strong tenant and session isolation is therefore important.

---

### 36. Conversation History Attack Surface

AI assistants may retain conversation history.

History can contain:

* Personal information
* Confidential business information
* Credentials
* Sensitive instructions
* Proprietary information

If conversation history is improperly protected, it can become an information-disclosure channel.

---

### 37. Session Context Attack Surface

The context supplied to a model may include information from previous interactions.

Potential risks include:

* Context leakage
* Incorrect context association
* Unauthorized reuse
* Cross-session contamination

Organizations should establish clear rules for context storage and reuse.

---

### 38. Memory Attack Surface

Some AI applications maintain persistent memory.

Memory may store:

* User preferences
* Previous interactions
* Business information
* Tasks
* Personal information

Persistent memory creates a long-term data asset that requires appropriate security and privacy controls.

---

### 39. AI Memory Poisoning

An attacker may attempt to insert malicious or misleading information into an AI system's persistent memory.

If the system trusts that information in future interactions, the attacker may influence later behavior.

Memory integrity should therefore be considered where persistent AI memory is implemented.

---

### 40. Context Window Attack Surface

AI systems process a defined amount of contextual information.

An attacker may attempt to manipulate context through:

* Excessive content
* Malicious instructions
* Repeated instructions
* Irrelevant information
* Large volumes of retrieved content

Context management is therefore part of AI security architecture.

---

### 41. Context Manipulation

AI behavior may change when the context supplied to the model changes.

Attackers may attempt to:

* Introduce misleading information
* Override expected priorities
* Inject instructions
* Manipulate retrieved content

The organization must understand what information is allowed into the model context.

---

### 42. Output Attack Surface

AI-generated outputs may be consumed by:

* Humans
* Applications
* Databases
* APIs
* Agents
* Automated workflows

The more automated the downstream process, the more important output validation becomes.

---

### 43. Unvalidated AI Output

An AI-generated response should not automatically be treated as trusted.

For example:

**AI Output**

→ **Application**

→ **Database Command**

If the application executes the output without validation, an attacker may exploit the AI system as an indirect command-generation mechanism.

---

### 44. Output Injection

Output injection occurs when AI-generated content is inserted into another system without appropriate validation or encoding.

Potential targets include:

* HTML
* SQL
* Shell commands
* Scripts
* API requests
* Documents

Traditional secure-output-handling principles therefore remain relevant.

---

### 45. AI-to-Application Boundary

The boundary between an AI model and an application must be clearly controlled.

The application should determine:

* What outputs are accepted
* What outputs are rejected
* What actions are allowed
* What validation is required
* What permissions apply

The model should not independently determine enterprise authorization.

---

### 46. AI-to-Database Boundary

Direct AI access to databases creates additional risk.

Potential concerns include:

* Unauthorized queries
* Excessive data access
* Data modification
* Data deletion
* SQL injection through generated queries
* Sensitive data disclosure

Database access should therefore remain governed by established security controls.

---

### 47. AI-to-Cloud Boundary

AI systems may interact with cloud resources such as:

* Storage
* Compute
* Databases
* Secrets
* Kubernetes clusters
* Cloud APIs

Excessive cloud permissions can significantly increase the impact of AI compromise.

---

### 48. AI-to-Email Boundary

AI agents may be granted access to email systems.

Potential risks include:

* Unauthorized message generation
* Phishing
* Data disclosure
* Malicious forwarding
* Unauthorized deletion
* Impersonation

Email actions should therefore have appropriate authorization and monitoring.

---

### 49. AI-to-Code Execution Boundary

Some AI systems can generate and execute code.

This creates a particularly sensitive boundary.

Potential risks include:

* Malicious code generation
* Unauthorized file access
* System modification
* Credential exposure
* Network access
* Resource exhaustion

Code execution environments should be strongly isolated.

---

### 50. AI Sandbox Boundary

A sandbox can restrict what AI-generated code can access.

Controls may include:

* Network restrictions
* File-system restrictions
* Resource limits
* Process isolation
* Temporary credentials
* Execution time limits

Sandboxing can reduce the blast radius of unsafe AI-generated code.

---

### 51. AI Infrastructure Attack Surface

AI infrastructure may include:

* Servers
* GPUs
* Containers
* Kubernetes
* Storage
* Networking
* Operating systems
* Cloud services

Traditional infrastructure-security controls remain essential.

---

### 52. GPU Infrastructure Risk

AI workloads may depend heavily on GPU infrastructure.

Potential risks include:

* Unauthorized access
* Resource exhaustion
* Insecure drivers
* Vulnerable libraries
* Shared-resource isolation failures

GPU environments should therefore be included in infrastructure-security assessments.

---

### 53. Container Attack Surface

AI workloads frequently operate in containers.

Container-related risks include:

* Vulnerable images
* Excessive privileges
* Insecure configurations
* Exposed interfaces
* Compromised dependencies

Container security should be integrated into AI security architecture.

---

### 54. Kubernetes and AI

Large AI environments may use Kubernetes to orchestrate workloads.

Security concerns may include:

* Misconfigured RBAC
* Exposed services
* Insecure secrets
* Container escape
* Excessive permissions
* Compromised images

AI security must therefore extend into orchestration platforms.

---

### 55. Model Hosting Environment

The environment hosting a model may contain sensitive assets.

Examples include:

* Model weights
* Configuration
* Credentials
* Logs
* Training artifacts
* User data

Compromise of the hosting environment can affect both model security and data security.

---

### 56. Model Artifact Attack Surface

Model artifacts may include:

* Model weights
* Configuration files
* Tokenizers
* Metadata
* Fine-tuning artifacts

These artifacts should be protected against:

* Unauthorized modification
* Replacement
* Theft
* Tampering

Integrity verification can help identify unauthorized changes.

---

### 57. Model Repository Attack Surface

Organizations may obtain models from:

* Internal repositories
* Cloud repositories
* Vendor marketplaces
* Open-source repositories

Model repositories should be evaluated for:

* Authenticity
* Integrity
* Provenance
* Licensing
* Security
* Known vulnerabilities

---

### 58. Open-Source AI Attack Surface

Open-source AI ecosystems provide significant benefits but also create risks.

Potential concerns include:

* Malicious packages
* Vulnerable dependencies
* Compromised repositories
* Untrusted model files
* Poorly maintained projects

Open-source AI components should therefore undergo appropriate security review.

---

### 59. AI Development Environment

The AI development environment may contain:

* Source code
* Training data
* Credentials
* Models
* Experiment results
* Evaluation datasets

Compromise of the development environment may allow attackers to influence future production systems.

---

### 60. AI Development Pipeline

A typical AI development pipeline may include:

**Data Collection**

→ **Data Preparation**

→ **Training**

→ **Evaluation**

→ **Model Registry**

→ **Deployment**

Every stage represents a potential attack surface.

---

### 61. Machine Learning Pipeline Security

Machine learning pipelines should protect:

* Data
* Code
* Models
* Configuration
* Credentials
* Build processes
* Deployment mechanisms

Security should be embedded throughout the pipeline rather than added only at deployment.

---

### 62. MLOps Attack Surface

MLOps environments integrate:

* Data engineering
* Machine learning
* Software engineering
* Infrastructure
* Deployment

This creates a complex security environment where compromise of one component may affect multiple stages of the AI lifecycle.

---

### 63. CI/CD Attack Surface for AI

AI applications may use CI/CD pipelines.

Attackers may attempt to compromise:

* Source code
* Build agents
* Dependencies
* Deployment credentials
* Model artifacts

Secure CI/CD practices therefore remain essential for AI systems.

---

### 64. Model Deployment Attack Surface

Deployment introduces additional exposure through:

* APIs
* Endpoints
* Load balancers
* Applications
* Authentication
* Network access

A secure model in an insecure deployment environment can still become compromised.

---

### 65. Model API Attack Surface

Model APIs may be exposed internally or externally.

Potential risks include:

* Unauthorized access
* Excessive queries
* Model extraction
* Denial of service
* Abuse
* Data leakage

API gateways and access controls can help manage these risks.

---

### 66. Rate-Limiting and Resource Abuse

Attackers may send excessive AI requests to:

* Exhaust compute resources
* Increase costs
* Reduce availability
* Consume API quotas

Rate limiting and usage controls can help prevent AI resource abuse.

---

### 67. AI Cost Attack Surface

AI systems can introduce a financial attack surface.

Attackers may intentionally generate expensive workloads through:

* Excessive requests
* Large prompts
* Repeated tool calls
* Recursive agent activity
* Resource-intensive processing

This can create **AI cost abuse** or **economic denial-of-service** conditions.

---

### 68. Token Consumption Risk

Generative AI services may charge based on:

* Input tokens
* Output tokens
* Model calls
* Tool calls
* Compute consumption

An attacker who manipulates an application into generating excessive requests can create unexpected costs.

---

### 69. Denial-of-Service Against AI

AI services may be targeted to reduce availability.

Potential methods include:

* Request flooding
* Resource exhaustion
* Large inputs
* Recursive agent operations
* Dependency attacks

Availability controls should therefore be incorporated into AI security architecture.

---

### 70. AI Resource Exhaustion

Resource exhaustion can involve:

* CPU
* GPU
* Memory
* Storage
* API quotas
* Network capacity

AI environments may require specialized capacity monitoring because AI workloads can be computationally intensive.

---

### 71. AI Supply-Chain Attack Surface

The AI supply chain can include:

* Training data
* Models
* Libraries
* Frameworks
* APIs
* Cloud providers
* Hardware
* Development tools
* SaaS platforms

Each dependency introduces potential security exposure.

---

### 72. Model Supply-Chain Integrity

Organizations should establish confidence that a model is:

* Authentic
* Untampered
* From an approved source
* Appropriate for the intended use
* Free from known unacceptable risks

Model provenance becomes an important governance control.

---

### 73. Dataset Supply-Chain Risk

Datasets may originate from:

* Vendors
* Public repositories
* Web scraping
* Partners
* Internal systems

The organization should understand:

* Data origin
* Collection methods
* Ownership
* Licensing
* Integrity
* Security

---

### 74. AI Hardware Supply Chain

AI infrastructure may depend on specialized:

* GPUs
* Accelerators
* Servers
* Networking components
* Firmware

Hardware supply-chain compromise can introduce security risks before the AI system is even deployed.

---

### 75. Third-Party Model Providers

External foundation-model providers may become critical components of the AI architecture.

Organizations should understand:

* Where processing occurs
* How data is handled
* What security controls exist
* How models are updated
* What dependencies exist
* What happens during service disruption

---

### 76. Multi-Provider AI Architecture

Organizations may use several AI providers.

For example:

**Provider A**

→ General-purpose AI

**Provider B**

→ Specialized AI

**Provider C**

→ AI Search

This can reduce concentration risk but increases governance complexity.

---

### 77. AI Vendor Integration Boundary

The boundary between the organization and an AI vendor may involve:

* APIs
* Data transfers
* Authentication
* Logs
* Model outputs
* Support channels

This boundary should be explicitly documented and governed.

---

### 78. AI SaaS Attack Surface

AI-enabled SaaS applications can introduce AI capabilities without the organization deploying the model directly.

For example:

* CRM with AI
* HR platform with AI
* Collaboration platform with AI
* Security platform with AI

AI governance must therefore identify embedded AI capabilities throughout the enterprise technology environment.

---

### 79. Embedded AI

An organization may unknowingly use AI because a vendor has incorporated AI into an existing product.

This creates a discovery challenge.

The organization should determine:

* Which products contain AI
* What data the AI processes
* What model or provider is involved
* Whether AI can be disabled
* What controls are available

---

### 80. AI Attack Surface Is Continuously Expanding

The AI attack surface expands whenever organizations introduce:

* New models
* New datasets
* New users
* New integrations
* New agents
* New tools
* New vendors
* New autonomous capabilities

AI security therefore requires continuous attack-surface discovery and reassessment.

## Part 3 — AI Attack Vectors, Trust Boundaries and Security Control Areas

### 81. Prompt Injection Attack Surface

Prompt injection represents one of the most distinctive AI attack surfaces.

An attacker may attempt to influence the model by introducing instructions that conflict with the intended behavior of the AI application.

Potential sources include:

* Direct user prompts
* Retrieved documents
* Web content
* Emails
* Database records
* Tool outputs
* Uploaded files

The security concern is the movement of untrusted instructions across a trust boundary.

---

### 82. Direct Prompt Injection

Direct prompt injection occurs when the attacker directly interacts with the AI system.

The attacker may attempt to:

* Override instructions
* Circumvent restrictions
* Obtain confidential information
* Manipulate decisions
* Trigger unauthorized actions

The application must therefore treat user input as potentially untrusted.

---

### 83. Indirect Prompt Injection

Indirect prompt injection originates from information consumed by the AI system.

For example:

**Attacker**

→ Malicious Web Page

→ AI Retrieval

→ AI Context

→ AI Model

The attacker may never interact directly with the AI application.

This makes indirect prompt injection particularly important for AI systems with browsing or retrieval capabilities.

---

### 84. Instruction Hierarchy Attack Surface

AI applications may have multiple instruction sources.

These can include:

* System instructions
* Developer instructions
* User instructions
* Retrieved content
* Tool responses

Security architecture should define the intended trust relationship among these sources.

---

### 85. System Instruction Protection

System instructions may contain security-sensitive information.

They may define:

* Model behavior
* Security restrictions
* Tool permissions
* Business rules
* Application logic

Unauthorized disclosure may help attackers identify ways to manipulate the system.

---

### 86. Jailbreak Attack Surface

Jailbreaking attempts to bypass restrictions imposed on an AI model or application.

Attackers may use:

* Role-play
* Instruction transformation
* Encoded requests
* Multi-step conversations
* Adversarial prompts

AI security testing should consider multiple forms of jailbreak attempts.

---

### 87. Context Manipulation Attack

An attacker may attempt to manipulate the context supplied to an AI system.

Potential techniques include:

* Injecting misleading information
* Flooding the context
* Adding conflicting instructions
* Altering retrieved information

Context integrity is therefore an important AI security consideration.

---

### 88. Retrieval Manipulation

Attackers may attempt to influence which information an AI system retrieves.

Potential objectives include:

* Promoting malicious documents
* Suppressing legitimate information
* Injecting false information
* Manipulating search rankings

Retrieval security therefore becomes part of AI security.

---

### 89. Knowledge Base Poisoning

A knowledge base used by an AI system may contain malicious or inaccurate information.

If the AI treats the knowledge base as authoritative, poisoned content may influence responses or decisions.

Organizations should establish governance over:

* Knowledge-source approval
* Data validation
* Content ownership
* Modification rights
* Integrity monitoring

---

### 90. Data Poisoning Attack Surface

Data poisoning can target:

* Training datasets
* Fine-tuning datasets
* Feedback datasets
* Knowledge bases
* Retrieval repositories

The attacker attempts to influence future model behavior by manipulating data.

---

### 91. Training Pipeline Manipulation

An attacker who compromises the training pipeline may attempt to alter:

* Dataset selection
* Data preprocessing
* Training parameters
* Model artifacts
* Evaluation results

Pipeline integrity is therefore critical to model trustworthiness.

---

### 92. Backdoor Attack Surface

A compromised model may contain hidden behavior that activates under particular conditions.

A backdoor could potentially cause:

* Specific outputs
* Unsafe behavior
* Unauthorized actions
* Targeted manipulation

Model provenance and testing are important defenses against such risks.

---

### 93. Model Theft Attack Surface

Models may represent valuable intellectual property.

Attackers may attempt to obtain:

* Model weights
* Architecture
* Configuration
* Fine-tuning data
* Training information

Strong access control and infrastructure security are required to protect these assets.

---

### 94. Model Extraction

Model extraction attempts to reproduce the behavior of a target model through repeated queries.

Potential impacts include:

* Intellectual-property loss
* Competitive disadvantage
* Security analysis by attackers
* Replication of proprietary capabilities

Organizations exposing models through public APIs should consider query-abuse protections.

---

### 95. Model Inversion

Model inversion attempts to infer information about the data or characteristics used to create a model.

This becomes particularly concerning when models have been trained using:

* Personal information
* Confidential information
* Sensitive business data

Data minimization and appropriate model-development controls can reduce exposure.

---

### 96. Membership Inference

Membership inference attempts to determine whether a particular record or individual was included in training data.

Potential consequences include:

* Privacy violations
* Confidentiality exposure
* Sensitive information disclosure

The risk depends heavily on the training data and model characteristics.

---

### 97. Adversarial Input Attack Surface

Attackers may construct inputs designed to cause AI systems to produce incorrect or unexpected results.

Such inputs may be:

* Slightly modified
* Carefully crafted
* Visually altered
* Contextually manipulated

Security testing should consider adversarial inputs where appropriate.

---

### 98. Evasion Attacks

Evasion attacks attempt to cause a model to misclassify or incorrectly interpret input.

Examples may occur in:

* Image recognition
* Fraud detection
* Malware detection
* Intrusion detection
* Identity verification

The security implications depend on the AI system's purpose.

---

### 99. Data Exfiltration Through AI

AI systems can become data-exfiltration channels.

An attacker may attempt to extract:

* Confidential documents
* Personal data
* Source code
* System information
* Internal instructions

Data-loss prevention and access controls should therefore encompass AI interfaces.

---

### 100. Cross-Context Data Leakage

An AI system may unintentionally combine information from different contexts.

For example:

**User A's Data**

*

**User B's Data**

→

**AI Response**

Improper context isolation can therefore create serious confidentiality issues.

---

### 101. Cross-Tenant Data Exposure

Multi-tenant AI platforms introduce additional isolation requirements.

Organizations should ensure that:

* Tenant data is separated
* Retrieval is tenant-aware
* Memory is appropriately isolated
* Access policies are enforced

A failure in tenant isolation can have broad consequences.

---

### 102. Sensitive Data in Prompts

Users may submit sensitive information into AI systems.

Examples include:

* Personal information
* Passwords
* Customer records
* Source code
* Contracts
* Security findings

Organizations should establish clear rules for handling sensitive data in prompts.

---

### 103. Sensitive Data in AI Logs

AI platforms may log:

* Prompts
* Responses
* User identifiers
* Tool calls
* Retrieved information

Logs can therefore become a secondary repository of sensitive information.

Logging systems require appropriate access control, retention, and protection.

---

### 104. AI Credential Exposure

AI systems may interact with external services using:

* API keys
* OAuth tokens
* Service accounts
* Cloud credentials
* Database credentials

Credentials must not be unnecessarily exposed to:

* Models
* Prompts
* Logs
* Users
* Generated outputs

---

### 105. Secret Leakage Through AI

Secrets may accidentally appear in:

* Prompts
* Code
* Documents
* Retrieved content
* AI responses
* Logs

Organizations should implement appropriate secret-management and detection controls.

---

### 106. AI Agent Privilege Escalation

An AI agent may begin with limited permissions but potentially obtain additional capabilities through:

* Tool chaining
* Credential access
* Application integration
* Configuration manipulation

Authorization should therefore remain enforced independently of the AI's reasoning.

---

### 107. Agent Goal Manipulation

An attacker may attempt to alter an agent's understanding of its objective.

For example, an attacker could introduce information that causes an agent to interpret:

> "Protect customer information"

as:

> "Provide customer information to complete the task."

The security architecture must distinguish trusted objectives from untrusted content.

---

### 108. Agent Tool Chaining Risk

An AI agent may combine multiple individually legitimate tools.

For example:

**Search Tool**

*

**File Tool**

*

**Email Tool**

=

**Potential Data Exfiltration Path**

Each tool may be safe independently while the combination creates a dangerous capability.

---

### 109. Privilege Aggregation

AI agents may accumulate privileges across multiple systems.

An agent with:

* CRM access
* Email access
* File access
* Database access

may effectively possess more authority than any single application appears to provide.

Organizations should therefore evaluate **combined privileges**, not only individual permissions.

---

### 110. AI Agent Identity

Each autonomous AI agent should have an identifiable security identity where appropriate.

This allows organizations to determine:

* Which agent acted
* What permissions it used
* Which user authorized the action
* What systems it accessed

Identity becomes essential for accountability.

---

### 111. AI Agent Authentication

Agents accessing enterprise services should use controlled authentication mechanisms.

Requirements may include:

* Strong authentication
* Credential protection
* Short-lived credentials
* Scoped permissions
* Credential rotation
* Revocation

Shared credentials should be avoided where practical.

---

### 112. AI Agent Authorization

Authorization should determine:

* Which data an agent can access
* Which tools it can use
* Which actions it can perform
* Which users it can act for
* Which transactions require approval

AI reasoning should never substitute for authorization enforcement.

---

### 113. Human Approval Boundary

Some AI actions should require explicit human approval.

Examples may include:

* Financial transactions
* Privileged access changes
* Destructive actions
* Regulatory decisions
* High-impact customer decisions

The approval boundary should reflect the potential impact of the action.

---

### 114. Autonomous Action Boundary

Organizations should explicitly define what AI systems may do autonomously.

Possible categories include:

**Allowed**

Low-impact, reversible actions.

**Conditional**

Actions requiring additional checks.

**Human Approval Required**

High-impact or irreversible actions.

**Prohibited**

Actions that the organization does not permit AI to perform.

---

### 115. Reversibility as a Security Control

The risk of an AI action depends partly on whether it can be reversed.

For example:

**Create Draft Email**

Low impact and reversible.

**Send Email**

Higher impact.

**Delete Customer Database**

Extremely high impact and potentially irreversible.

AI authorization should therefore consider action reversibility.

---

### 116. Blast Radius

The **blast radius** represents the potential scope of damage if an AI system is compromised or misused.

Blast radius increases with:

* More data access
* More tools
* More privileges
* More connected systems
* More autonomous authority

Security architecture should aim to limit unnecessary blast radius.

---

### 117. AI Network Attack Surface

AI systems may communicate with:

* Internal networks
* Internet services
* Cloud platforms
* APIs
* Databases
* External providers

Network controls should restrict communication to legitimate requirements.

---

### 118. AI Egress Control

Outbound connections from AI environments should be controlled.

Unrestricted egress can allow compromised AI applications or agents to:

* Exfiltrate data
* Contact malicious infrastructure
* Access unauthorized services

Network egress controls can reduce these risks.

---

### 119. AI Ingress Control

Inbound access should also be restricted.

Organizations should control:

* Public endpoints
* APIs
* Administrative interfaces
* Model-management interfaces
* Development environments

Only required connections should be exposed.

---

### 120. AI Security Segmentation

AI environments may benefit from segmentation between:

* Development
* Testing
* Staging
* Production
* Training
* Model repositories
* Data repositories

Segmentation can reduce lateral movement and limit the consequences of compromise.

---

### 121. Development-to-Production Boundary

Development environments often have greater experimentation and fewer restrictions.

Production environments require stronger controls.

The organization should prevent uncontrolled movement of:

* Models
* Data
* Credentials
* Configuration
* Code

between environments.

---

### 122. Training-to-Production Boundary

Training environments may contain large datasets and experimental models.

Production environments may contain:

* Critical applications
* Customer information
* Operational systems

The two environments should have clearly defined trust and access boundaries.

---

### 123. Model Promotion Boundary

A model should not automatically move from development into production.

Promotion may require:

* Security testing
* Performance validation
* Risk assessment
* Approval
* Integrity verification

This establishes a formal model-release boundary.

---

### 124. AI Configuration Boundary

AI configuration may determine:

* Model selection
* System instructions
* Tool permissions
* Data sources
* Safety controls

Unauthorized configuration changes can materially alter AI behavior.

Configuration management should therefore be part of the security boundary.

---

### 125. Model Update Boundary

Model updates may change:

* Accuracy
* Behavior
* Security characteristics
* Performance
* Data handling

Organizations should determine whether updates require:

* Testing
* Revalidation
* Approval
* Documentation
* Risk reassessment

---

### 126. AI Change-Control Boundary

Not every change to an AI system carries the same risk.

Changes should be categorized according to impact.

Examples include:

* Minor configuration changes
* Prompt changes
* Data-source changes
* Tool changes
* Model changes
* Architecture changes

Higher-impact changes should require stronger review.

---

### 127. AI Monitoring Boundary

Monitoring should cover important interactions across the AI environment.

Potential monitoring areas include:

* Authentication
* Data access
* Model requests
* Prompt patterns
* Tool invocation
* Administrative changes
* Model changes
* Security events

---

### 128. AI Detection Boundary

Security teams should be able to detect suspicious AI activity.

Examples include:

* Unusual request volumes
* Unexpected tool calls
* Abnormal data retrieval
* Repeated jailbreak attempts
* Unauthorized model access
* Unusual agent behavior

AI telemetry should be integrated with broader security monitoring.

---

### 129. AI Incident Response Boundary

When an AI security event occurs, organizations need to determine:

* Which system is affected
* Which data is exposed
* Which identities were involved
* Which tools were accessed
* Whether autonomous actions occurred
* Whether external parties were affected

AI-specific incident information should be incorporated into incident response processes.

---

### 130. AI Security Boundary as a Governance Concept

The AI security boundary should ultimately be understood as more than a technical perimeter.

It includes:

**People**

*

**Processes**

*

**Data**

*

**Models**

*

**Applications**

*

**Infrastructure**

*

**Tools**

*

**External Providers**

*

**Decisions**

The boundary therefore represents the complete environment within which AI security risks can emerge.

## Part 4 — AI Attack Surface Management and Governance

### 131. AI Attack Surface Discovery

An organization cannot protect an AI attack surface that it does not know exists.

AI attack-surface discovery should identify:

* AI applications
* AI models
* AI agents
* AI APIs
* AI-enabled SaaS
* Data sources
* Vector databases
* Plugins
* External tools
* AI vendors
* Development environments
* Production environments

Discovery should cover both officially approved AI systems and unauthorized or unmanaged AI usage.

---

### 132. AI Asset Inventory

An AI asset inventory provides a structured view of the organization's AI environment.

Depending on the organization, the inventory may include:

| AI Asset       | Examples                               |

The inventory becomes a foundational governance capability.

### 133. AI Asset Ownership

Every material AI asset should have an identifiable owner.

Ownership may include:

* Business owner
* System owner
* Model owner
* Data owner
* Security owner
* Vendor owner

Ownership establishes accountability for decisions concerning the asset.

---

### 134. AI Asset Classification

AI assets should be classified according to their characteristics.

Possible classification factors include:

* Business criticality
* Data sensitivity
* Regulatory exposure
* Security risk
* Degree of autonomy
* External exposure
* User population
* Potential impact

Classification allows security controls to be proportionate to risk.

---

### 135. AI Risk-Based Attack Surface Management

Not every AI attack surface represents the same level of risk.

For example:

**Internal FAQ Assistant**

may have limited impact.

A:

**Financial Transaction Agent**

may have significantly greater impact.

Attack-surface management should therefore prioritize assets according to their potential consequences.

---

### 136. Critical AI Systems

Organizations should identify AI systems that support critical processes.

Examples may include AI used for:

* Financial operations
* Security operations
* Healthcare
* Industrial control
* Fraud detection
* Customer decisions
* Critical infrastructure

Critical AI systems should generally receive enhanced governance and security controls.

---

### 137. AI Exposure Assessment

Organizations should evaluate how exposed each AI system is.

Relevant factors include:

* Internet exposure
* Number of users
* Data sensitivity
* Number of integrations
* Autonomous capabilities
* External dependencies
* Privilege level

Greater exposure generally requires stronger controls.

---

### 138. AI Attack Surface Mapping

Attack-surface mapping should identify relationships between AI components.

For example:

**Model**

↓

**RAG**

↓

**Vector Database**

↓

**Enterprise Data**

↓

**Agent**

↓

**External API**

This mapping helps identify where security boundaries exist.

---

### 139. AI Data-Flow Mapping

Data-flow mapping identifies how information moves through an AI system.

A typical flow might be:

**User Data**

→ **Application**

→ **Model**

→ **Retrieval**

→ **Enterprise Data**

→ **AI Output**

→ **Downstream Application**

Each movement represents a potential confidentiality, integrity, or privacy consideration.

### 140. AI Trust-Boundary Mapping

Organizations should explicitly document trust boundaries.

For example:

**Trusted Enterprise Data**

↓

**Retrieval Layer**

↓

**AI Context**

↓

**Model**

↓

**External User**

Different security controls may apply at each boundary.

### 141. AI Privilege Mapping

AI systems should be mapped according to the privileges they possess.

This includes:

* Data access
* API access
* Administrative access
* Transaction authority
* File access
* Cloud permissions
* Tool permissions

Privilege mapping helps identify excessive authority.

---

### 142. Least Privilege for AI

The principle of least privilege applies to AI systems just as it does to other technologies.

An AI application should receive only the permissions necessary to perform its intended function.

This principle should apply to:

* Users
* Agents
* APIs
* Service accounts
* Tools
* Data repositories

---

### 143. AI Capability Minimization

Organizations should avoid granting unnecessary capabilities.

For example, an AI assistant that only needs to read customer records may not need:

* Database write access
* Email access
* Cloud administration
* File deletion
* Code execution

Reducing unnecessary capabilities reduces the potential attack surface.

---

### 144. AI Tool Governance

Tools connected to AI systems should be governed individually.

For each tool, organizations should determine:

* Purpose
* Owner
* Required permissions
* Permitted users
* Permitted agents
* Data accessed
* Actions permitted
* Logging requirements

Tools should not be treated as automatically trusted simply because they are internally approved.

---

### 145. AI Agent Permission Boundaries

Agent permissions should be explicitly defined.

An agent may have:

**Read**

* Customer records

**Create**

* Draft documents

**Execute**

* Approved workflow

**Prohibited**

* Delete records
* Change privileged access

Clear permission boundaries reduce unintended autonomous actions.

---

### 146. Human-in-the-Loop Controls

Human oversight can provide an additional control boundary.

Human approval may be required before:

* Financial transactions
* Destructive actions
* High-impact decisions
* External communications
* Privileged changes

The human approval process should itself be meaningful rather than a simple automatic confirmation.

---

### 147. Human-on-the-Loop Controls

In some environments, continuous human approval may not be practical.

Instead, humans may supervise AI activity through:

* Monitoring
* Alerts
* Exception handling
* Periodic review

This model may be appropriate for lower-risk autonomous operations.

---

### 148. Human-out-of-the-Loop Risks

Fully autonomous AI actions can create greater governance challenges.

Where no human intervention exists, organizations should place greater emphasis on:

* Strong authorization
* Guardrails
* Transaction limits
* Monitoring
* Reversibility
* Fail-safe mechanisms

Autonomy should be proportionate to risk.

---

### 149. AI Guardrails

Guardrails are mechanisms designed to constrain AI behavior.

They may include:

* Input filtering
* Output filtering
* Policy enforcement
* Access restrictions
* Tool restrictions
* Transaction limits
* Human approval

Guardrails should complement, not replace, fundamental security controls.

---

### 150. Input Guardrails

Input guardrails may identify or restrict:

* Malicious prompts
* Sensitive information
* Dangerous requests
* Unauthorized operations
* Suspicious patterns

They can reduce exposure before information reaches the model.

---

### 151. Output Guardrails

Output controls may detect:

* Sensitive information
* Unsafe content
* Unauthorized disclosures
* Malicious code
* Policy violations

Outputs should be evaluated according to the risk of the intended use.

---

### 152. Tool Guardrails

Tool guardrails can limit what an AI system is permitted to do.

For example:

**AI Agent**

→ Search: Allowed

→ Read database: Allowed

→ Create transaction: Approval required

→ Delete database: Prohibited

This creates explicit capability boundaries.

---

### 153. Transaction Guardrails

High-impact actions may require controls such as:

* Amount limits
* Frequency limits
* Approval thresholds
* Segregation of duties
* Dual authorization

These controls can prevent AI systems from executing unlimited transactions.

---

### 154. AI Fail-Safe Controls

AI systems should have mechanisms for safely handling abnormal conditions.

Examples include:

* Stop execution
* Disable tools
* Revoke credentials
* Isolate the agent
* Require human approval
* Fall back to manual processing

Fail-safe controls reduce potential impact when AI behavior becomes unreliable.

---

### 155. AI Kill Switch

For highly autonomous or critical systems, organizations may need an emergency mechanism to disable:

* Model access
* Agent execution
* Tool access
* Automated workflows

The mechanism should be tested periodically rather than existing only as a theoretical capability.

---

### 156. AI Containment

Containment limits the impact of a compromised AI system.

Possible mechanisms include:

* Network isolation
* Restricted credentials
* Sandbox execution
* Tool disabling
* Data-access restrictions
* Transaction limits

Containment should be considered during architecture design rather than after an incident.

---

### 157. AI Security Testing

AI attack surfaces should be tested before and after deployment.

Testing may include:

* Vulnerability assessment
* Penetration testing
* Adversarial testing
* Prompt-injection testing
* Data-leakage testing
* Agent testing
* Access-control testing

Testing should reflect the actual architecture and intended use.

---

### 158. AI Red Teaming

AI red teaming attempts to identify weaknesses by simulating realistic attacks.

Activities may include testing:

* Prompt injection
* Jailbreaking
* Data exfiltration
* Agent manipulation
* Tool abuse
* Model extraction
* Unsafe autonomous actions

Red teaming can provide evidence of how AI behaves under adversarial conditions.

---

### 159. AI Security Validation

Security validation should determine whether controls actually work.

Examples include verifying that:

* Unauthorized users cannot retrieve protected information.
* Agents cannot execute prohibited actions.
* Untrusted content cannot override security controls.
* Credentials are not exposed to the model.
* Production models cannot be modified without authorization.

Validation should produce evidence suitable for governance and assurance.

---

### 160. AI Continuous Monitoring

AI attack surfaces change over time.

Monitoring should identify changes involving:

* New models
* New data sources
* New tools
* New users
* New integrations
* New vendors
* New privileges

Continuous monitoring helps maintain an accurate security posture.

### 161. AI Configuration Monitoring

Configuration changes can materially alter AI behavior.

Monitoring may include:

* System prompts

### 162. AI Model Monitoring

* Performance

The objective is to identify meaningful changes that could affect risk.

### 163. AI Drift and Security

Model or data drift may change how an AI system behaves.

### 180. AI Security Boundary and Risk Appetite

### 181. AI Attack Surface and Business Context

The same technical capability can present different levels of risk depending on its business context.

For example, AI-generated text used for brainstorming may have limited consequences.

Attack-surface analysis must therefore consider **business impact**, not merely technical exposure.

### 182. AI Attack Surface and Data Sensitivity

**Public Information**

AI processing:

**Confidential Business Information**

AI processing:

**Highly Sensitive or Regulated Information**

### 183. AI Attack Surface and Autonomy

Autonomy is another major risk factor.

A useful conceptual progression is:

**Informational AI**

→ Provides information.

**Assistive AI**

→ Helps a human perform an action.

**Decision-Support AI**

→ Influences decisions.

**Action-Oriented AI**

→ Performs approved actions.

**Autonomous AI Agent**

→ Performs multi-step actions with limited human intervention.

As autonomy increases, the required security boundaries and governance controls generally become more significant.

---

### 184. AI Attack Surface and Reversibility

Security architecture should consider whether an AI action can be reversed.

A reversible action generally presents less potential impact than an irreversible action.

Therefore:

**Risk ≈ Impact × Exposure × Privilege × Autonomy × Irreversibility**

This is a conceptual model rather than a universal quantitative formula, but it illustrates why AI attack-surface analysis should extend beyond the number of exposed interfaces.

### 185. AI Attack Surface and Blast-Radius Reduction

* Data isolation
* Transaction limits
* Human approval
* Sandboxing

These controls reduce the potential blast radius.
