# 01.06 — AI Governance Roles, Responsibilities, and Accountability

## Part 1 — Introduction to AI Governance Roles, Responsibilities, and Accountability

### 1. Introduction

AI Governance cannot operate effectively without clearly defined **roles, responsibilities, authorities, and accountability**.

An organization may have excellent AI policies, sophisticated security controls, and strong risk-management processes, but governance can still fail if nobody clearly understands:

* Who owns an AI system
* Who owns the associated risk
* Who approves AI deployment
* Who operates the controls
* Who provides oversight
* Who manages incidents
* Who can accept residual risk
* Who provides independent assurance

AI Governance therefore requires a clear organizational accountability structure.

The fundamental principle is:

> **Every material AI system, AI risk, governance requirement, and control should have clearly defined ownership and accountability.**

---

### 2. Why Roles and Responsibilities Matter

AI systems frequently cross organizational boundaries.

A single AI application may involve:

* Business users
* Product management
* Data scientists
* Software engineers
* Cybersecurity
* Privacy
* Legal
* Procurement
* Enterprise Risk
* Compliance
* AI Governance
* Internal Audit
* External suppliers

Without clearly defined responsibilities, organizations can experience:

* Unclear ownership
* Duplicated activities
* Gaps in controls
* Delayed decisions
* Unmanaged risks
* Weak incident response
* Poor auditability

AI Governance therefore requires more than an organizational chart.

It requires clearly defined **decision rights and accountability relationships**.

---

### 3. Responsibility Versus Accountability

These terms should be carefully distinguished.

**Responsibility**

Means being assigned to perform an activity.

**Accountability**

Means being answerable for the outcome.

For example:

> An AI engineer may be responsible for implementing model security controls.

The:

> AI system owner may be accountable for ensuring the system meets organizational requirements.

This distinction becomes especially important when multiple teams participate in the AI lifecycle.

---

### 4. Authority

A third concept is **authority**.

Authority defines who has the power to make or approve a decision.

For example:

* AI engineer → responsible for implementation
* Security architect → responsible for security review
* AI Governance → responsible for governance oversight
* Business owner → accountable for business use
* AI Governance Committee → authority to approve certain high-risk use cases

A person cannot realistically be accountable for an outcome if they lack the authority required to influence it.

---

### 5. Ownership

AI Governance should establish several forms of ownership.

### Business Ownership

Who owns the business purpose and outcome?

### Technical Ownership

Who owns the technical implementation?

### Data Ownership

Who owns the relevant data?

### Risk Ownership

Who owns the identified risk?

### Control Ownership

Who owns the control?

### Governance Ownership

Who owns the governance requirement?

These ownership types may belong to different people.

---

### 6. AI System Owner

Every material AI system should have an identified **AI System Owner**.

The AI System Owner is typically accountable for ensuring that the system:

* Has a defined purpose
* Has appropriate governance
* Has an appropriate risk classification
* Meets applicable requirements
* Has required approvals
* Is monitored
* Has appropriate documentation
* Is changed appropriately
* Is retired when no longer required

The AI System Owner is one of the most important accountability roles in AI Governance.

---

### 7. Business Owner

The Business Owner is accountable for the business purpose and value of the AI system.

Responsibilities may include:

* Defining the business objective
* Approving intended use
* Determining business criticality
* Accepting business-level residual risk within authority
* Ensuring appropriate resources
* Confirming continued business need

The Business Owner should understand what the AI system does and how its outputs affect business decisions.

---

### 8. Technical Owner

The Technical Owner is responsible for the technical operation of the AI system.

Responsibilities may include:

* Architecture
* Infrastructure
* Configuration
* Integration
* Availability
* Technical security
* Performance
* Technical change management

The Technical Owner should coordinate with cybersecurity and AI Governance where appropriate.

---

### 9. AI Product Owner

For AI products, an AI Product Owner may coordinate:

* Product objectives
* AI functionality
* User requirements
* Product lifecycle
* Model integration
* Product risks
* Stakeholder needs

The Product Owner may work closely with the Business Owner and Technical Owner.

---

### 10. Model Owner

Where model governance is important, the organization may assign a **Model Owner**.

Responsibilities may include:

* Model documentation
* Model validation
* Performance monitoring
* Model changes
* Version control
* Model limitations
* Model retirement

A Model Owner may exist separately from the overall AI System Owner.

---

### 11. Data Owner

The Data Owner is accountable for appropriate governance of the data used by AI.

Responsibilities may include:

* Data classification
* Data access
* Data quality
* Data usage
* Retention
* Data ownership
* Data authorization

AI teams should not assume that because they can technically access data, they are authorized to use it for AI purposes.

---

### 12. Data Steward

The Data Steward typically supports operational data governance.

Responsibilities may include:

* Data quality
* Metadata
* Data definitions
* Data lineage
* Data classification
* Data issue management

The Data Steward may work under the authority of the Data Owner.

---

### 13. AI Governance Function

The AI Governance function provides the organizational framework for managing AI.

Its responsibilities may include:

* AI Governance policy
* Standards
* Governance requirements
* AI inventory
* Risk classification
* Governance workflows
* Metrics
* Reporting
* Oversight
* Exception management
* Coordination across functions

The AI Governance function should not necessarily own every AI risk.

Instead, it should establish the framework through which those risks are governed.

---

### 14. AI Governance Manager

The AI Governance Manager may coordinate the day-to-day governance program.

Responsibilities can include:

* Maintaining governance processes
* Coordinating assessments
* Managing AI inventory
* Tracking approvals
* Monitoring compliance
* Coordinating committees
* Reporting metrics
* Managing exceptions
* Supporting audits

The role can serve as the central coordination point for AI Governance.

### 15. AI Governance Committee

An AI Governance Committee provides cross-functional oversight.

Membership may include:

* Executive leadership
* AI Governance
* Cybersecurity
* Privacy
* Legal
* Risk
* Data Governance
* Technology
* Business representatives
* Compliance

The committee should have clearly defined authority.

---

### 16. Responsibilities of the AI Governance Committee

The committee may:

* Approve AI governance policies
* Review high-risk AI systems
* Review significant AI risks
* Review material exceptions
* Review major AI incidents
* Review governance metrics
* Escalate material risks
* Recommend improvements

The committee should not become a purely informational meeting.

It should have meaningful decision rights.

---

### 17. Executive Management

Executive management provides strategic direction and organizational sponsorship.

Responsibilities may include:

* Establishing AI strategy
* Approving risk appetite
* Allocating resources
* Supporting governance
* Reviewing material AI risks
* Making strategic decisions

Executives should understand AI risk at a level appropriate to their responsibilities without needing to become AI engineers.

### 18. Board of Directors

The Board's role depends on organizational structure and applicable governance requirements.

At the strategic level, the Board may oversee:

* AI-related enterprise risk
* Significant regulatory exposure
* Major ethical or reputational risks
* Critical AI dependencies
* Material incidents
* Management's AI Governance capability

The Board should receive information that enables meaningful oversight.

### 26. Internal Audit

Internal Audit should not normally own AI controls because doing so could impair independence.

Instead, Internal Audit evaluates:

* Governance design
* Risk management
* Control effectiveness
* Compliance
* Evidence
* Governance maturity

This preserves the third-line role.

AI Governance must therefore establish both:

**Policy controls**

**technical or monitoring controls**

### 40. Decision Rights

Roles alone are insufficient.

Organizations should define **who has authority to make specific AI decisions**.

Examples:

* Who approves low-risk AI?
* Who approves high-risk AI?
* Who accepts residual risk?
* Who can authorize exceptions?
* Who can suspend an AI system?
* Who approves material model changes?
* Who can retire an AI system?

These decision rights should be documented.

---

### 41. Delegated Authority

Not every AI decision needs executive approval.

Organizations can establish delegated authority.

For example:

**Low Risk**

→ Business Owner.

**Moderate Risk**

→ Business + AI Governance.

**High Risk**

→ AI Governance Committee.

**Critical Risk**

→ Executive Management.

The thresholds should be formally defined.

---

### 42. Escalation Authority

Some roles should have authority to escalate or suspend AI activity when serious risk is detected.

For example:

> Security may recommend immediate suspension of an AI system experiencing a critical security compromise.

> Privacy may escalate unauthorized personal-data processing.

> AI Governance may escalate a material policy violation.

Clear escalation authority reduces response delays.

---

### 43. Accountability for AI Outcomes

A critical governance principle is:

> **The organization cannot delegate accountability to the AI system itself.**

Statements such as:

> "The AI made the decision."

do not establish organizational accountability.

There should always be an identifiable human or organizational owner responsible for the use of the AI system.

---

### 44. Accountability Across the AI Lifecycle

Accountability should exist throughout:

**Planning**

→ Business Owner

**Design**

→ Product / Technical Owner

**Data**

→ Data Owner

**Development**

→ Engineering / Model Owner

**Security**

→ Security Owner

**Deployment**

→ Authorized Approver

**Operation**

→ System Owner

**Monitoring**

→ System / Governance Owners

**Incident**

→ Incident Owner

**Retirement**

→ System Owner

Different people may perform these roles, but the accountability chain should remain clear.

---

### 45. Key Takeaways

AI Governance requires a clearly defined organizational accountability model.

The most important concepts are:

1. Responsibility is not the same as accountability.
2. Authority must accompany accountability.
3. Every material AI system should have an owner.
4. Business ownership and technical ownership may be separate.
5. Data ownership should be clearly defined.
6. Risk ownership should be explicit.
7. Control ownership should be assigned.
8. AI Governance provides the framework and oversight.
9. Cybersecurity owns relevant security responsibilities.
10. Privacy owns relevant privacy responsibilities.
11. Data Governance owns relevant data governance responsibilities.
12. Legal and Compliance support regulatory and legal governance.
13. Procurement and Third-Party Risk govern external AI providers.
14. Internal Audit provides independent assurance.
15. AI developers and data scientists operate within governance requirements.
16. Employees are part of the AI control environment.
17. Decision rights must be explicitly defined.
18. Risk-based approval thresholds should be established.
19. Escalation and suspension authority should be defined.
20. Organizational accountability cannot be delegated to an AI system.

---

### 46. Closing Perspective

A mature AI Governance program should make it possible to answer a simple question for every material AI system:

> **"Who is accountable?"**

The answer should not be:

> "The AI team."

It should identify the specific organizational roles responsible for:

**Business Purpose**

→ **Risk**

→ **Data**

→ **Technology**

→ **Security**

→ **Privacy**

→ **Compliance**

→ **Operation**

→ **Monitoring**

→ **Incident Response**

→ **Assurance**

The ultimate objective is to establish an accountability chain in which every significant AI decision has:

**An owner**

**An authority**

**A responsibility**

**A control**

**An evidence trail**

**An escalation path**

and

**An assurance mechanism**.

At the strategy stage, leadership determines:

* Why the organization will use AI
* What business objectives AI should support
* Which AI capabilities are strategic
* What risks the organization is willing to accept
* What governance capabilities are required

Key participants may include:

* Board
* Executive Management
* CIO
* CISO
* Chief Data Officer
* Chief Risk Officer
* AI Governance
* Business Leadership

The key governance question is:

> **"Why should the organization use AI, and under what boundaries?"**

* Accurate
* Relevant

* Properly sourced
* Appropriately retained

## 62. Independent Validation

For higher-risk systems, validation should have an appropriate degree of independence.

For example:

> The person who developed the model may not be the only person responsible for validating it.

Independent challenge helps identify issues that developers may overlook.

---

## 63. AI Governance Approval

Approval should occur before production deployment where required.

Approval may require evidence of:

* Risk assessment
* Security assessment
* Privacy assessment
* Testing
* Business justification
* Required controls
* Residual risk decision

The approval authority should match the risk level.

## 64. AI Go-Live Decision

The go-live decision should answer:

> **"Are we comfortable deploying this AI system under the identified risk conditions?"**

Possible outcomes are:

**Approved**

Deployment may proceed.

**Conditionally Approved**

Deployment may proceed subject to defined conditions.

**Rejected**

Deployment may not proceed.

**Deferred**

Additional evidence or remediation is required.

## 72. AI Incident Escalation

Severity should determine escalation.

For example:

**Low**

→ Operational team.

**Moderate**

→ AI System Owner + Governance.

**High**

→ Security / Privacy / Risk leadership.

**Critical**

→ Executive Management / AI Governance Committee.

## 73. Authority to Suspend AI

The organization should define who can suspend an AI system.

Potential triggers include:

* Critical security incident
* Significant privacy violation
* Unacceptable model behavior
* Regulatory concern
* Safety concern
* Material control failure

A governance framework without emergency suspension authority can create unnecessary risk.

---

## 74. AI Exception Management

AI systems may occasionally require exceptions.

The exception owner should document:

* Requirement
* Reason for exception
* Risk
* Compensating controls
* Expiration date
* Approval authority

Exceptions should never become indefinite.

---

## 75. AI Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance should be performed by an authorized role.

The risk owner should understand:

* Risk description
* Inherent risk
* Existing controls
* Residual risk
* Business benefit
* Risk appetite

Risk acceptance should be documented.

---

## 76. AI Risk Escalation

Risk should be escalated when:

* Risk exceeds appetite
* Controls fail
* New information increases impact
* Material incidents occur
* Regulatory expectations change

Escalation ensures that risks are addressed at the appropriate organizational level.

* Decision rights should be explicitly documented.
* Risk acceptance must follow delegated authority.
* High-risk AI requires stronger governance.
* AI Governance should coordinate rather than unnecessarily duplicate existing GRC functions.
* Third-party providers do not eliminate organizational accountability.
* AI systems should have defined suspension and escalation mechanisms.
* Human oversight must have real authority.
* Accountability must continue throughout the AI lifecycle.
* Governance should extend through AI retirement.
* Internal Audit should remain independent from management's AI controls.
* AI governance maturity should include measurable accountability indicators.

The central principle is:

> **A mature AI Governance framework does not merely identify who participates; it defines who is responsible, who is accountable, who has authority, who can challenge decisions, who can stop the system, and who provides independent assurance.**

## 166. AI Governance RACI

A RACI model can define responsibilities.

**R — Responsible**

Performs the activity.

**A — Accountable**

A well-designed RACI should avoid multiple "A" assignments unless there is a clear reason.

## 168. Avoiding RACI Problems

RACI matrices can become ineffective when:

* Nobody is Accountable
* Too many people are Consulted
* Informed parties are excessive
* Roles do not match actual authority

A RACI should simplify accountability rather than create administrative complexity.

## 201. AI Governance Scenario — Failed Accountability

Consider an AI system that generates incorrect customer decisions.

The business says:

This is an accountability failure.

A mature operating model should make ownership clear **before deployment**.

Decision-makers have documented authority.

Governance intensity matches risk.

### Principle 4 — Separation

Operational responsibility is separated from independent assurance.

### Principle 5 — Traceability

Important decisions are documented.

### Principle 6 — Challenge

Governance functions can challenge business decisions.

### Principle 7 — Escalation

Material risks are escalated appropriately.

### Principle 8 — Continuity

Accountability exists throughout the AI lifecycle.

### Principle 9 — Evidence

Governance decisions can be demonstrated.

### Principle 10 — Continuous Improvement

Governance evolves as AI risks and technologies change.

## 206. Final Perspective

AI Governance is ultimately an **accountability architecture**.

Policies, standards, frameworks, committees, assessments, controls, and technology are mechanisms that support this architecture.

The fundamental governance chain is:

> **Who owns it?**

↓

> **Who is responsible for it?**

↓

> **Who has authority over it?**

↓

> **Who monitors it?**

↓

> **Who can challenge it?**

↓

> **Who can stop it?**

↓

> **Who accepts the remaining risk?**

↓

> **Who provides independent assurance?**

A mature organization should be able to answer every one of these questions for every material AI system.

The ultimate objective is not merely to establish an **AI Governance Department**.

It is to establish an organizational system in which **AI accountability is clear, decisions are authorized, risks are owned, controls are operated, evidence is maintained, and independent assurance is possible throughout the entire AI lifecycle**.
