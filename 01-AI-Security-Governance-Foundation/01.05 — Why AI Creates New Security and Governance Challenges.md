# 01.05 — Why AI Creates New Security and Governance Challenges

## Part 1 — Introduction to the New AI Security and Governance Landscape

### 1. Introduction

Artificial Intelligence introduces security and governance challenges that differ fundamentally from those associated with traditional information systems.

Traditional cybersecurity governance generally assumes that:

* Systems behave according to explicitly defined rules.
* Software functionality is primarily determined by code.
* Inputs and outputs can be relatively predictable.
* System behavior can usually be reproduced consistently.
* Access boundaries can be clearly defined.
* Security testing can identify known technical weaknesses.
* Human users remain the primary actors interacting with systems.

AI systems challenge many of these assumptions.

AI behavior may depend on:

* Training data
* Model architecture
* Statistical relationships
* Prompts
* Context
* Retrieval data
* External tools
* User behavior
* Model updates
* Environmental changes

As a result, an AI system may produce different outputs for similar inputs and may behave in ways that are difficult to anticipate completely.

This creates a governance problem in addition to a technical security problem.

---

### 2. Why Traditional Governance Is Not Enough

Traditional IT governance was largely designed around applications, infrastructure, databases, networks, and clearly defined business processes.

AI introduces additional governance questions.

Traditional application governance may ask:

> Who developed the application?

> What data does the application process?

> Who can access it?

> What controls protect it?

AI Governance must additionally ask:

> What model is being used?

> Where did the model and its data originate?

> How was the model trained?

> How does the model behave under unexpected conditions?

> Can the model generate harmful or misleading outputs?

> Can users manipulate its behavior?

> Can the AI system access external tools?

> Can the system make decisions autonomously?

> What happens when the model changes?

These questions demonstrate why AI Governance cannot simply be treated as traditional application governance with an additional AI label.

---

### 3. AI Changes the Nature of Technology Risk

Traditional software generally follows explicitly programmed logic.

For example:

**Input**

→ **Defined Rules**

→ **Processing**

→ **Output**

AI systems may instead operate through:

**Input**

→ **Model**

→ **Probabilistic Inference**

→ **Output**

The result may depend on statistical patterns learned from data rather than only on deterministic rules.

This difference has major consequences for:

* Predictability
* Testing
* Explainability
* Security
* Safety
* Accountability
* Monitoring
* Risk management

---

### 4. Probabilistic Behavior

One of the most important characteristics of many AI systems is probabilistic behavior.

A traditional software application may produce the same result whenever identical inputs and system conditions are provided.

An AI system may produce different outputs because of:

* Model characteristics
* Sampling
* Context
* Prompt structure
* Conversation history
* Retrieval results
* Model updates
* External tools

This creates additional governance requirements for testing and monitoring.

---

### 5. Non-Determinism and Governance

Non-deterministic behavior creates challenges for traditional assurance methods.

A security team may ask:

> "Can we prove exactly what this system will do?"

For some AI systems, the answer may be:

> "We can establish expected behavior and acceptable boundaries, but we cannot guarantee every possible output."

Governance therefore needs to shift from attempting to guarantee every output toward establishing:

* Acceptable behavior
* Prohibited behavior
* Risk thresholds
* Monitoring requirements
* Intervention mechanisms
* Escalation procedures

---

### 6. AI Systems Learn From Data

Traditional applications are primarily created through software development.

AI systems additionally depend heavily on data.

The quality and characteristics of the data can influence:

* Model performance
* Bias
* Accuracy
* Security
* Privacy
* Fairness
* Reliability

This means that AI Governance must extend beyond software governance into **data governance**.

---

### 7. Data Becomes Part of the Security Boundary

In traditional applications, security boundaries often focus on:

* Servers
* Networks
* Applications
* Databases
* Endpoints

For AI systems, the security boundary may also include:

* Training datasets
* Fine-tuning datasets
* Embeddings
* Vector databases
* Retrieval repositories
* Prompts
* System instructions
* Model weights
* Model artifacts

Compromising any of these components may affect the security or behavior of the AI system.

---

### 8. AI Creates New Data Exposure Paths

Generative AI introduces new ways information can enter and leave an organization.

For example:

**Employee**

→ **Prompt**

→ **AI Service**

→ **Model Processing**

→ **Generated Response**

Sensitive information could potentially be exposed during this process if governance controls are inadequate.

Organizations therefore need to understand the complete information flow.

---

### 9. AI Can Process Sensitive Information

AI systems may process:

* Personal data
* Financial information
* Customer information
* Intellectual property
* Source code
* Security information
* Strategic information
* Confidential business information

This creates additional privacy, confidentiality, and data-governance challenges.

The organization must understand:

> What information is entering the AI system?

> Where is it processed?

> Who can access it?

> How long is it retained?

> Is it used for model improvement?

> Can it be retrieved later?

---

### 10. AI Creates New Trust Boundaries

AI systems increasingly interact with multiple components.

A modern AI application may look like:

**User**

↓

**AI Application**

↓

**Foundation Model**

↓

**Retrieval System**

↓

**Enterprise Data**

↓

**External APIs**

↓

**Cloud Infrastructure**

Each connection creates a potential trust boundary.

Security governance must therefore understand the entire AI ecosystem rather than treating the model as an isolated component.

---

### 11. AI Expands the Attack Surface

Traditional applications already have significant attack surfaces.

AI adds additional attack vectors involving:

* Prompts
* Models
* Training data
* Retrieval systems
* Plugins
* Agents
* APIs
* Model repositories
* AI development environments
* AI supply chains

The AI attack surface will be examined in greater detail in **01.06 — AI Attack Surfaces and AI Security Boundaries**.

---

### 12. Prompt-Based Interaction

Traditional applications typically communicate through structured interfaces.

Generative AI often accepts natural-language instructions.

This creates a unique security characteristic:

> **Language itself can become an attack interface.**

An attacker may attempt to manipulate the AI system through specially constructed instructions.

This creates security challenges that are different from conventional application input validation.

---

### 13. Prompt Injection

Prompt injection occurs when an attacker attempts to manipulate an AI system by inserting instructions that influence the model's behavior.

For example, an attacker may attempt to cause an AI application to:

* Ignore intended instructions
* Reveal sensitive information
* Execute unauthorized actions
* Change its behavior
* Misuse connected tools

Prompt injection demonstrates that AI security cannot rely solely on traditional authentication and authorization controls.

---

### 14. Indirect Prompt Injection

The attack does not necessarily need to come directly from the user.

Malicious instructions may be embedded in:

* Web pages
* Documents
* Emails
* PDFs
* Databases
* Knowledge repositories

An AI system retrieving that content may unintentionally process the embedded instructions.

This creates a new security challenge:

> **Untrusted data can become an instruction channel.**

---

### 15. AI Agents Increase Governance Complexity

An AI model that only generates text has one level of risk.

An AI agent that can:

* Read files
* Send emails
* Execute code
* Query databases
* Modify records
* Call APIs
* Purchase services

creates a significantly different risk profile.

The more autonomy and authority an AI system possesses, the greater the governance requirements.

---

### 16. From Information Processing to Action

Traditional AI applications may primarily provide information.

Agentic AI can potentially move from:

**Observe**

→ **Reason**

→ **Plan**

→ **Act**

This introduces a critical governance question:

> **Should an AI system be allowed to take an action without human approval?**

The answer should depend on:

* Risk
* Impact
* Authorization
* Business context
* Reversibility
* Regulatory requirements

---

### 17. AI Autonomy Creates New Accountability Questions

When an AI system performs an action, organizations must determine who is accountable.

For example:

**AI Agent**

→ Deletes a file

Who is responsible?

* The AI developer?
* The system owner?
* The business owner?
* The employee who authorized the agent?
* The vendor?
* The organization?

AI Governance must establish accountability before autonomous capabilities are deployed.

---

### 18. AI Can Generate Incorrect Information

AI systems can produce:

* Incorrect answers
* Fabricated references
* Misleading explanations
* Incomplete information
* Contradictory outputs

These failures may be referred to as **hallucinations** in generative AI contexts.

The security and governance problem is not simply that an answer is wrong.

The more important question is:

> **What happens when someone trusts the incorrect answer?**

---

### 19. AI Hallucination as a Governance Risk

An incorrect AI output may create:

* Business risk
* Legal risk
* Financial risk
* Safety risk
* Reputational risk
* Customer harm

The severity depends on how the output is used.

A hallucinated answer in a brainstorming session may be relatively low risk.

The same behavior in a medical, financial, legal, or safety-critical context could have serious consequences.

---

### 20. AI Creates an Automation Trust Problem

Organizations may begin to trust AI outputs because the system appears:

* Fast
* Confident
* Consistent
* Intelligent
* Authoritative

However, apparent confidence does not guarantee correctness.

This creates a governance requirement for:

* Human oversight
* Output validation
* Appropriate user training
* Defined decision rights
* Clear accountability

---

### 21. AI Can Amplify Existing Risks

AI does not always create an entirely new risk.

It can amplify an existing one.

For example:

**Existing Fraud Capability**

*

**AI Automation**

→

**Higher-Volume Fraud**

Similarly:

**Existing Phishing Capability**

*

**Generative AI**

→

**More Scalable and Convincing Phishing**

AI therefore changes the **scale, speed, and sophistication** of some existing threats.

---

### 22. AI Can Create New Threat Capabilities

AI can also introduce capabilities that were previously difficult or expensive to achieve.

Examples include:

* Large-scale content generation
* Automated social engineering
* Automated code generation
* Automated reconnaissance
* Synthetic identities
* Deepfakes
* Automated decision manipulation

This creates a changing threat environment for cybersecurity governance.

---

### 23. AI Lowers the Barrier to Entry

Some AI capabilities can make sophisticated activities accessible to less-skilled users.

This may affect:

* Cybercrime
* Fraud
* Disinformation
* Social engineering
* Malware development
* Identity manipulation

From a governance perspective, the organization must consider not only advanced attackers but also a larger population of potential attackers.

---

### 24. AI Accelerates the Threat Lifecycle

Traditional attacks may require significant manual effort.

AI can potentially accelerate:

**Reconnaissance**

→ **Content Generation**

→ **Social Engineering**

→ **Exploitation**

→ **Automation**

This reduces the time available for defenders to detect and respond.

---

### 25. AI Creates a Governance Speed Problem

AI technologies evolve rapidly.

Organizations may face:

* New models
* New AI platforms
* New providers
* New capabilities
* New vulnerabilities
* New regulations
* New attack techniques

Traditional governance processes may take months to approve technologies.

AI innovation may occur within days or weeks.

This creates tension between:

> **Governance Control**

and

> **Technology Speed**

A mature AI Governance program must address both.

---

### 26. Governance Must Enable Responsible Innovation

AI Governance should not simply prevent employees from using AI.

The objective should be to enable:

> **Safe, secure, responsible, and valuable AI adoption.**

This requires governance mechanisms that are:

* Risk-based
* Proportionate
* Fast enough for business needs
* Clear
* Repeatable
* Practical

Effective governance should enable innovation rather than unintentionally drive it underground.

---

### 27. The Shadow AI Problem

When official AI processes are too restrictive, employees may use unauthorized AI services.

This creates **Shadow AI**.

Examples include:

* Personal generative AI accounts
* Unapproved AI browser extensions
* Unauthorized AI coding assistants
* Personal AI APIs
* AI-enabled applications not reviewed by security

Shadow AI can create significant governance blind spots.

---

### 28. AI Governance Visibility Challenge

Organizations cannot effectively govern what they cannot see.

Therefore, AI Governance needs mechanisms to identify:

* What AI systems exist
* Who owns them
* What data they process
* What models they use
* What vendors support them
* What decisions they influence
* What permissions they have

This is one reason why an **AI inventory** becomes a fundamental governance capability.

---

### 29. AI Creates Lifecycle Complexity

AI systems may change through:

* Retraining
* Fine-tuning
* Model replacement
* Prompt changes
* Retrieval changes
* Data changes
* Configuration changes
* Tool integration

A system that was approved six months ago may no longer be materially the same system.

Governance must therefore account for **continuous change**.

---

### 30. The Central Governance Challenge

The fundamental challenge created by AI can be summarized as:

> **Organizations must govern systems whose behavior may be probabilistic, whose inputs and outputs can be dynamic, whose capabilities evolve rapidly, and whose decisions may increasingly influence or perform real-world actions.**

This is why AI Security Governance requires more than simply applying traditional cybersecurity controls to AI.

It requires an integrated approach involving:

* Governance
* Risk Management
* Cybersecurity
* Privacy
* Safety
* Data Governance
* Model Governance
* Legal
* Compliance
* Human Oversight
* Business Leadership

The next parts will examine these challenges in greater depth and establish the foundations for understanding the expanded AI attack surface and security boundaries.

## Part 2 — AI-Specific Security Challenges

### 31. AI Introduces New Security Assumptions

Traditional cybersecurity is built around assumptions about how systems behave, how users interact with applications, and how security boundaries can be enforced.

AI challenges these assumptions because:

* AI behavior can be probabilistic.
* Natural language can function as an input mechanism.
* Data can influence model behavior.
* Models can be difficult to inspect directly.
* AI systems may dynamically retrieve information.
* AI agents may invoke external tools.
* AI capabilities can change without traditional software changes.

Security governance must therefore account for characteristics that are not normally present in conventional applications.

---

### 32. The AI Security Boundary Is Dynamic

A traditional application may have a relatively stable security boundary.

An AI system can have a dynamic boundary involving:

**User**

→ **Application**

→ **Prompt**

→ **Model**

→ **Retrieval System**

→ **Enterprise Data**

→ **External Tool**

→ **External Service**

The boundary can change when:

* A new model is introduced.
* A new data source is connected.
* A new tool is enabled.
* A new plugin is installed.
* A new API is integrated.
* User permissions change.

AI Security Governance must therefore continuously evaluate the security boundary.

---

### 33. AI Makes the Input Boundary More Complex

Traditional applications generally distinguish between:

* User input
* Application logic
* System instructions
* Data

AI systems can blur these distinctions.

Natural-language content may contain both:

* Data
* Instructions

An AI system may not always reliably distinguish between them.

This creates a fundamental AI security challenge.

---

### 34. Instructions and Data Can Become Intermingled

Consider an AI system that retrieves a document.

The document may contain:

> Customer information...

But it could also contain malicious instructions designed to influence the AI.

The AI system may interpret both as part of its context.

This creates an important governance principle:

> **Untrusted content must not automatically become trusted instructions.**

---

### 35. Prompt Injection as a New Security Problem

Prompt injection exploits the way AI systems process natural-language instructions.

An attacker may attempt to manipulate:

* System instructions
* User prompts
* Retrieved content
* Tool instructions
* Agent objectives

The objective may be to alter the AI system's intended behavior.

This is fundamentally different from many traditional attacks that exploit software vulnerabilities through structured inputs.

---

### 36. Indirect Prompt Injection

Indirect prompt injection occurs when malicious instructions originate from external content rather than directly from the user.

Potential sources include:

* Websites
* Emails
* Documents
* Knowledge bases
* Search results
* Customer-submitted content

This creates a significant challenge for AI applications using retrieval or browsing capabilities.

---

### 37. Prompt Injection and Trust

AI applications may contain multiple instruction sources.

For example:

**System Instructions**

↓

**Developer Instructions**

↓

**User Instructions**

↓

**Retrieved Content**

↓

**Tool Results**

The security architecture must define how these sources are trusted and prioritized.

Without appropriate controls, lower-trust content may influence higher-trust behavior.

---

### 38. Jailbreaking

Jailbreaking involves attempts to bypass restrictions imposed on an AI model.

An attacker may attempt to cause the system to:

* Ignore safety restrictions
* Generate prohibited content
* Reveal protected information
* Circumvent intended limitations

Jailbreaking is therefore an important consideration for AI security testing.

---

### 39. AI Output Manipulation

AI security is not limited to protecting inputs.

Attackers may also attempt to influence outputs.

Potential objectives include:

* Misleading users
* Manipulating decisions
* Generating malicious content
* Causing incorrect actions
* Triggering downstream systems

Output integrity therefore becomes an important security consideration.

---

### 40. AI Data Poisoning

Data poisoning involves deliberately introducing malicious or misleading information into datasets used by AI systems.

Potential targets include:

* Training datasets
* Fine-tuning datasets
* Retrieval repositories
* Knowledge bases
* Feedback datasets

The objective may be to influence future AI behavior.

---

### 41. Training Data Integrity

Organizations need confidence that training and fine-tuning data has not been improperly modified.

Controls may include:

* Data provenance
* Access controls
* Dataset versioning
* Integrity monitoring
* Validation
* Approval processes

Training data should therefore be treated as a security-sensitive asset.

---

### 42. Model Poisoning

Model poisoning involves compromising model artifacts or influencing the model so that it behaves maliciously or unexpectedly.

Potential attack targets include:

* Model weights
* Fine-tuned models
* Model repositories
* Model files
* Model distribution mechanisms

Organizations using externally sourced models should consider model integrity as part of supply-chain security.

---

### 43. Model Supply-Chain Risk

AI systems often depend on multiple external components.

For example:

**AI Application**

↓

**Third-Party Framework**

↓

**Open-Source Library**

↓

**External Model**

↓

**Cloud Infrastructure**

Each dependency can introduce security risk.

The AI supply chain therefore becomes part of the organization's security boundary.

---

### 44. AI Dependency Risk

An AI system may depend on:

* Foundation models
* Model APIs
* Cloud providers
* Data providers
* Vector databases
* Open-source libraries
* AI frameworks
* External tools

A failure or compromise in one dependency can affect the entire AI service.

---

### 45. Model Theft and Extraction

AI models can represent significant intellectual property.

Attackers may attempt to:

* Steal model artifacts
* Replicate model behavior
* Extract sensitive information
* Reverse engineer proprietary capabilities

Model protection should therefore consider both cybersecurity and intellectual-property requirements.

---

### 46. Model Extraction Through Queries

An attacker may attempt to learn about a model by repeatedly querying it.

Potential objectives include:

* Reconstructing model behavior
* Identifying weaknesses
* Approximating proprietary functionality
* Extracting information

Organizations should consider whether model interfaces expose excessive information.

---

### 47. Membership Inference Risk

Membership inference attacks attempt to determine whether particular information was included in training data.

This creates potential privacy concerns where sensitive or personal information may have been used during model development.

Appropriate data governance and privacy controls are therefore important.

---

### 48. Model Inversion Risk

Model inversion attempts to infer information about the underlying training data from model behavior or outputs.

This can create risks when models have been trained using sensitive information.

Organizations should consider:

* Data minimization
* Training-data controls
* Access restrictions
* Privacy-preserving techniques
* Output monitoring

---

### 49. Sensitive Information Disclosure

AI systems can unintentionally expose:

* Personal information
* Credentials
* Confidential documents
* Source code
* Internal procedures
* Security configurations
* Proprietary information

Disclosure may occur through:

* Prompts
* Responses
* Logs
* Retrieval systems
* Training
* Fine-tuning
* Tool integrations

---

### 50. Insecure AI Output Handling

AI output should not automatically be treated as trusted data.

For example:

**AI Output**

→ **Application**

→ **Database**

→ **Command**

If the downstream application interprets the AI output as executable or trusted input, an attacker may be able to manipulate the AI into producing dangerous content.

This creates an important principle:

> **AI-generated output should be validated before being trusted by downstream systems.**

---

### 51. Excessive Agency

An AI system may have more authority than necessary.

For example, an AI agent might have access to:

* Email
* Databases
* Cloud resources
* File systems
* Financial systems

If the agent is compromised or manipulated, excessive permissions can increase the impact.

This is why **least privilege** is particularly important for agentic AI.

---

### 52. Excessive Autonomy

Autonomy becomes a security concern when an AI system can take actions without meaningful human intervention.

Risk increases when actions are:

* Irreversible
* High impact
* Financially significant
* Security-sensitive
* Privacy-sensitive
* Safety-critical

Organizations should establish explicit boundaries around autonomous actions.

---

### 53. Tool Abuse

AI agents may use external tools through:

* APIs
* Plugins
* Function calls
* Scripts
* Databases
* Enterprise applications

A compromised or manipulated agent could potentially misuse these capabilities.

Tool access should therefore be:

* Authorized
* Limited
* Monitored
* Logged
* Revocable

---

### 54. Unauthorized Tool Invocation

AI systems should not automatically invoke every available capability.

Each tool should have:

* A defined purpose
* An owner
* Authorized users
* Permission boundaries
* Security requirements
* Monitoring
* Revocation mechanisms

Tool authorization should be treated similarly to privileged access management.

---

### 55. Agent-to-Agent Risk

Future enterprise environments may contain multiple AI agents interacting with each other.

For example:

**Customer Service Agent**

↓

**Billing Agent**

↓

**Fraud Detection Agent**

↓

**Payment Agent**

This creates new trust relationships and communication paths.

A compromised agent could potentially influence another agent.

---

### 56. AI-to-AI Trust

Organizations may need to establish:

* Agent identity
* Agent authorization
* Trust levels
* Message integrity
* Action permissions
* Logging
* Accountability

AI-to-AI communication should not automatically be trusted merely because the participating systems belong to the same organization.

---

### 57. AI Identity Risk

AI systems increasingly require identities of their own.

Examples include:

* Service accounts
* Agent identities
* API identities
* Model identities
* Machine identities

Organizations should be able to determine:

> Which AI system performed this action?

> Under whose authority?

> With what permissions?

This supports accountability and forensic investigation.

---

### 58. AI Authentication Challenges

AI agents may interact with multiple services.

Authentication must ensure that:

* The AI system is properly identified.
* Credentials are protected.
* Credentials are appropriately scoped.
* Credentials can be revoked.
* Authentication events are logged.

AI agents should not rely on shared or unmanaged credentials.

---

### 59. AI Authorization Challenges

Authentication answers:

> **Who or what are you?**

Authorization answers:

> **What are you allowed to do?**

For AI systems, authorization may need to consider:

* User identity
* Agent identity
* Task context
* Data sensitivity
* Action type
* Risk level

This creates the foundation for risk-based AI access control.

---

### 60. AI Logging and Auditability

AI systems should generate appropriate records of significant activity.

Depending on the system, this may include:

* User identity
* AI identity
* Prompt
* Model version
* Retrieved sources
* Tool calls
* Actions
* Output
* Approval
* Errors
* Security events

Logging requirements should consider privacy and data-minimization requirements.

---

### 61. AI Observability

Traditional monitoring may focus on:

* CPU
* Memory
* Network
* Application availability

AI observability may additionally require monitoring:

* Model performance
* Output quality
* Drift
* Bias indicators
* Tool usage
* Prompt patterns
* Retrieval behavior
* Safety events

This expands the scope of security operations.

---

### 62. AI Model Drift

AI systems may become less reliable when the environment changes.

Examples include:

* Changing customer behavior
* Changing market conditions
* Changing language patterns
* New business processes
* New data distributions

This is known as **model drift**.

Drift can create security, business, safety, and compliance risks.

---

### 63. Data Drift

Data drift occurs when the statistical characteristics of incoming data change.

For example:

**Historical Data**

→ Customer behavior A

**Current Data**

→ Customer behavior B

The model may no longer perform as expected.

Monitoring data distributions can therefore become part of AI governance.

---

### 64. Concept Drift

Concept drift occurs when the relationship between inputs and desired outcomes changes.

For example, a model trained on historical fraud patterns may become less effective when attacker behavior changes.

This means that AI security and risk monitoring must consider changing threat conditions.

---

### 65. AI Vulnerability Management

AI systems require vulnerability management just as traditional applications do.

However, the scope may include:

* AI frameworks
* Libraries
* Models
* APIs
* Plugins
* Infrastructure
* Containers
* Retrieval systems
* Agent tools

AI vulnerability management should be integrated with enterprise vulnerability management.

---

### 66. AI Patch Management

AI environments may involve multiple update types:

* Application patches
* Library updates
* Model updates
* Prompt updates
* Configuration changes
* Security-rule changes

Each type of change can affect system behavior differently.

Governance should therefore define change-control requirements for AI environments.

---

### 67. AI Configuration Drift

An AI system may gradually diverge from its approved configuration.

Changes could involve:

* System prompts
* Model parameters
* Access permissions
* Data sources
* Tools
* API connections
* Safety controls

Configuration monitoring can help identify unauthorized or unexpected changes.

---

### 68. AI Resilience Challenges

AI services can depend heavily on external infrastructure and providers.

Potential failures include:

* Model provider outage
* Cloud outage
* API failure
* Network failure
* Capacity exhaustion
* Data-store failure

Critical AI services should have appropriate resilience and recovery strategies.

---

### 69. AI Availability and Dependency

If a business process depends on an external AI API, an outage may affect the organization's operations.

Therefore, organizations should identify:

* Critical AI dependencies
* Service-level requirements
* Alternative providers
* Manual fallback processes
* Recovery objectives

AI dependency should be incorporated into business continuity planning.

---

### 70. AI Security Requires Continuous Adaptation

AI security cannot be treated as a one-time certification activity.

The threat environment changes because:

* New attack techniques emerge.
* Models change.
* AI applications change.
* New integrations are introduced.
* New vulnerabilities are discovered.
* New regulations become applicable.

AI Security Governance must therefore be **continuous rather than static**.

---

### 71. AI Security Is Multidisciplinary

AI security cannot be owned entirely by cybersecurity.

Effective governance may require collaboration among:

* Cybersecurity
* AI Governance
* Data Governance
* Privacy
* Legal
* Compliance
* Risk Management
* Engineering
* IT
* Procurement
* Internal Audit
* Business Leadership

Each function addresses a different dimension of AI risk.

---

### 72. AI Security and Traditional Cybersecurity

AI Security should not replace traditional cybersecurity.

Instead:

**Traditional Cybersecurity**

*

**AI-Specific Security Controls**

=

**AI Security Governance**

Traditional controls remain necessary:

* Identity and access management
* Network security
* Endpoint security
* Vulnerability management
* Encryption
* Security monitoring
* Incident response

AI-specific controls supplement these capabilities.

---

### 73. AI Security and Data Security

Data security becomes especially important because AI systems depend heavily on data.

Controls should address:

* Data classification
* Access
* Encryption
* Data loss prevention
* Retention
* Provenance
* Integrity
* Authorized use

AI Security Governance therefore depends on strong data-security foundations.

---

### 74. AI Security and Application Security

AI applications should remain subject to secure software development practices.

These include:

* Secure architecture
* Secure coding
* Testing
* Dependency management
* Vulnerability remediation
* Secure deployment
* Change management

AI introduces additional requirements but does not eliminate traditional application security.

---

### 75. AI Security and Cloud Security

Many AI systems rely on cloud infrastructure.

Cloud-related AI risks may include:

* Misconfigured storage
* Excessive permissions
* Insecure APIs
* Exposed endpoints
* Weak identity controls
* Insecure containers
* Provider dependency

AI Governance should therefore integrate with existing cloud-security governance.

---

### 76. AI Security and Supply-Chain Security

AI supply-chain governance should consider:

* Model providers
* Dataset providers
* Open-source components
* AI frameworks
* Cloud services
* APIs
* Plugins
* Development tools

Security requirements should extend across the AI supply chain.

---

### 77. AI Security and Zero Trust

Zero Trust principles can be applied to AI environments.

The organization should avoid automatically trusting:

* Users
* Applications
* Agents
* Tools
* Data sources
* Models
* External providers

Instead, access should be continuously evaluated based on:

* Identity
* Context
* Authorization
* Risk
* Resource sensitivity

---

### 78. AI Security and Least Privilege

AI systems should receive only the minimum permissions required.

For example:

An AI customer-support agent may need to:

* Read customer records
* Create support tickets

It may not need permission to:

**Data Controls**

**Output Validation**

## Part 3 — AI Governance, Risk and Organizational Challenges

### 81. AI Changes the Governance Model

AI introduces governance challenges that extend beyond traditional technology governance.

Organizations must coordinate:

* Business objectives
* AI strategy
* Cybersecurity
* Data governance
* Privacy
* Risk management
* Legal requirements
* Regulatory obligations
* Ethical considerations
* Operational resilience

AI Governance therefore requires a cross-functional operating model.

---

### 82. AI Governance Requires Clear Accountability

One of the most important governance challenges is determining who is responsible for an AI system.

Potential stakeholders include:

* Business Owner
* AI System Owner
* Product Owner
* Data Owner
* Model Owner
* Security Owner
* Privacy Officer
* Risk Owner
* Vendor Owner
* Executive Sponsor

Without clearly defined accountability, AI risks can become organizational gaps.

---

### 83. AI System Ownership

Every material AI system should have an identifiable owner.

The AI System Owner should understand:

* Purpose of the system
* Business processes supported
* Data processed
* Models used
* Risks identified
* Controls implemented
* Third-party dependencies
* Performance requirements
* Lifecycle status

Ownership should remain clear even when the AI capability is provided by a third party.

---

### 84. AI Creates Shared Accountability

AI governance rarely belongs to a single department.

For example:

**Business**

→ Defines the purpose.

**Engineering**

→ Builds or integrates the system.

**Data Governance**

→ Governs data.

**Cybersecurity**

→ Manages security risks.

**Privacy**

→ Manages privacy requirements.

**Risk**

→ Evaluates enterprise exposure.

**Legal**

→ Addresses legal obligations.

**Executive Management**

→ Makes significant risk decisions.

Shared responsibility requires clearly documented decision rights.

---

### 85. AI Governance and the Three Lines Model

Organizations may apply the Three Lines model to AI Governance.

**First Line**

Business and technology teams own and manage AI risks.

**Second Line**

Risk, compliance, security, privacy, and AI governance functions provide oversight.

**Third Line**

Internal Audit provides independent assurance.

This separation helps prevent governance responsibilities from becoming concentrated within one function.

---

### 86. AI Governance and the Board

Boards may need visibility into significant AI risks.

Relevant issues may include:

* Strategic AI exposure
* Major AI incidents
* Regulatory risk
* Critical AI dependencies
* Significant data exposure
* High-impact AI systems
* Risk acceptance decisions

The Board does not need to manage technical AI controls.

It needs sufficient information to exercise effective oversight.

---

### 87. AI Governance and Executive Management

Executive Management must translate AI strategy into controlled execution.

Responsibilities may include:

* Approving AI strategy
* Establishing risk appetite
* Allocating resources
* Approving significant risks
* Establishing governance structures
* Monitoring AI risk

Executive accountability becomes particularly important for high-impact AI use cases.

---

### 88. AI Governance and the CISO

The CISO may be responsible for AI-related cybersecurity governance.

Responsibilities can include:

* AI security strategy
* Security architecture
* AI threat management
* Security assessments
* AI incident response
* Security monitoring
* Third-party AI security
* Security awareness

However, the CISO should not automatically become the sole owner of all AI risks.

---

### 89. AI Governance and the CIO

The CIO may oversee:

* Enterprise AI adoption
* Technology architecture
* AI platforms
* Infrastructure
* Integration
* Technology standards

The CIO's role should be coordinated with cybersecurity, privacy, risk, and AI governance.

### 90. AI Governance and the CTO

The CTO may focus on:

* AI engineering
* Model architecture
* Technical innovation
* AI platforms
* Research and development

* Risk appetite
* Risk aggregation
* Risk reporting
* Risk acceptance
* Enterprise risk dashboards

### 92. AI Governance and the Data Protection Officer

The DPO may address:

* Lawful processing

* Privacy impact assessments
* Data subject rights
* Data minimization
* Data retention
* Third-party processing

AI Governance must therefore integrate with privacy governance.

**Preventive controls**

### 105. AI Governance and AI Literacy

Employees need to understand:

AI literacy therefore becomes a governance control.

Human oversight should reflect AI risk.

Possible levels include:

**Human-in-the-Loop**

A human approves important actions.

**Human-on-the-Loop**

A human supervises the AI and can intervene.

**Human-in-Command**

A human retains ultimate authority over the AI system.

Higher-risk systems generally require stronger human oversight.

### 107. AI Governance and Decision Rights

AI governance must define who can:

* Approve AI use
* Approve high-risk applications
* Accept residual risk
* Approve model changes
* Approve new data sources
* Authorize autonomous actions
* Suspend an AI system

Undefined decision rights create governance ambiguity.

### 108. AI Governance and Risk Appetite

Organizations should establish how much AI risk they are willing to accept.

For example:

> High-risk AI may require formal approval.

> Certain AI applications may be prohibited entirely.

Risk appetite provides the basis for consistent decisions.

---

### 109. AI Governance and Risk Acceptance

Residual AI risk should be formally accepted by an authorized individual or committee.

Risk acceptance should document:

* Risk description
* Potential impact
* Existing controls
* Residual risk
* Business justification
* Acceptance authority
* Expiration or review date

Informal acceptance creates accountability problems.

Organizations may occasionally need exceptions to AI policies.

Exceptions should be:

* Documented
* Justified
* Time-limited
* Risk-assessed
* Approved
* Monitored

Permanent exceptions can become uncontrolled weaknesses.

AI organizations need policies addressing areas such as:

* Acceptable AI use
* AI development
* AI security
* Data use
* Privacy
* Third-party AI
* AI risk assessment

* AI-generated content

Policies establish organizational expectations.

Standards translate policy requirements into measurable expectations.

* AI registration procedure
* AI risk assessment procedure
* AI security review
* AI privacy assessment
* AI approval workflow
* AI incident response
* AI retirement procedure

Procedures make governance operational.

* Risks

* Standards
* Regulations
* Frameworks
* Control objectives

This enables traceability from risk to control.

Having an AI control does not necessarily mean the risk is adequately managed.

* Designed appropriately

Assurance activities can include:

* Internal Audit
* Independent testing
* Red teaming

### 117. AI Governance and Internal Audit

Internal Audit may assess whether:

* Governance structures exist
* Responsibilities are clear
* AI risks are identified
* Controls are operating
* Exceptions are managed
* Risk acceptance is appropriate
* Monitoring is effective

Internal Audit should remain independent from operational AI ownership.

---

### 118. AI Governance and Continuous Monitoring

AI governance cannot stop at deployment.

Organizations should monitor:

* Security
* Performance
* Privacy
* Compliance
* Data changes
* Model changes
* User behavior
* Third-party dependencies

Continuous monitoring helps detect emerging risks.

### 119. AI Governance and Continuous Improvement

AI Governance should evolve based on:

* Incidents
* Near misses
* Audit findings

* New threats
* Technology changes
* Lessons learned

A mature governance program continuously improves rather than remaining static.

### 120. The Organizational Challenge

The central organizational challenge can be summarized as:

It is simultaneously:

* A business capability
* A technology capability
* A data capability
* A cybersecurity concern
* A privacy concern
* A risk-management concern
* A regulatory concern
* A strategic capability

This means that no single department can effectively govern AI in isolation.

A mature organization therefore establishes a coordinated governance structure where **business ownership, technical responsibility, risk oversight, security, privacy, compliance, and independent assurance work together**.

* Customer experience
* Workforce
* Products and services

* Reputation

Poorly governed AI can therefore create strategic risk even when the underlying technology is technically secure.

AI may enable organizations to:

* Automate processes
* Create new services
* Reduce operating costs
* Personalize customer experiences
* Accelerate decision-making
* Develop new products

Governance must therefore balance:

**AI Opportunity**

with

**AI Risk**

For example:

**Customer Service**

→ AI

**Fraud Detection**

→ AI

**Software Development**

→ AI

**Financial Analysis**

→ AI

**Security Operations**

→ AI

* Cloud providers
* AI platforms
* Data providers
* AI infrastructure vendors

A major provider outage, security incident, commercial change, or regulatory restriction could affect multiple business processes simultaneously.

AI may become embedded into daily operations.

* Availability
* Resilience

* Service continuity
* Alternative solutions
* Manual fallback

AI should therefore be incorporated into operational resilience planning.

Critical AI-enabled processes should have defined continuity arrangements.

Organizations should determine:

* What happens if the AI service becomes unavailable?
* Can employees perform the process manually?
* Is an alternative AI provider available?
* Can another model be used?

Business continuity requirements should reflect the criticality of the AI-enabled process.

* System prompts
* Data
* Vector databases
* APIs
* Credentials
* Infrastructure
* Integration components

The organization should understand all dependencies required to restore the AI capability.

### 128. AI and Operational Resilience

AI resilience should therefore consider:

* Provider outages
* Model failures
* Cyberattacks
* Data corruption
* Infrastructure failures
* Regulatory restrictions
* Supply-chain disruption

The objective is not merely system recovery but **continued business capability**.

### 129. AI Creates Workforce Challenges

* New responsibilities
* New skills

Organizations need governance for the human impact of AI adoption.

Employees may:

The greater the potential impact, the stronger the expected oversight.

* Accountability

### 171. The Core AI Governance Challenge

The fundamental challenge is not simply:

> **"How do we secure AI?"**

The broader question is:

> **"How do we govern an evolving technology whose behavior, data, decisions, dependencies, and impacts may extend across the entire organization?"**

This requires organizations to move beyond traditional technology governance.

### 174. Key Lessons From AI Security and Governance Challenges

The major lessons are:

1. AI changes traditional assumptions about technology behavior.
2. AI introduces probabilistic and dynamic behavior.
3. Data becomes a critical component of the AI security boundary.
4. Natural language can become an attack interface.
5. AI agents can introduce autonomous-action risks.
6. AI systems create new identity and authorization challenges.
7. AI expands third-party and supply-chain dependencies.
8. AI creates new privacy and confidentiality risks.
9. AI can amplify existing cyber threats.
10. AI can create entirely new threat capabilities.
11. AI changes organizational accountability.
12. AI requires multidisciplinary governance.
13. AI governance must be proportional to risk.
14. AI systems require lifecycle-based governance.
15. AI governance must continue after deployment.
16. AI governance requires measurable evidence and assurance.
17. AI governance must balance innovation with control.
18. AI security must integrate with traditional cybersecurity.
19. AI risk must be integrated with enterprise risk management.
20. AI Security Governance must be an enterprise capability.
