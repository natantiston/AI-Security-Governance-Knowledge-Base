# 01.05 — AI Governance Principles and Core Governance Requirements

## Part 1 — Introduction to AI Governance Principles and Core Governance Requirements

### 1. Introduction

AI Governance requires more than policies, committees, and technical controls.

An effective governance program must be based on a set of **principles** that establish how an organization expects AI to be designed, acquired, deployed, used, monitored, and retired.

These principles provide the foundation for translating organizational values and external requirements into:

* Governance requirements
* Policies
* Standards
* Risk criteria
* Control objectives
* Controls
* Processes
* Metrics
* Assurance activities

The relationship can be represented as:

**AI Governance Principles**

↓

**Governance Requirements**

↓

**Policies and Standards**

↓

**Risk and Control Framework**

↓

**Operational Processes**

↓

**Evidence and Assurance**

Principles therefore sit near the top of the AI Governance hierarchy.

---

### 2. What Is an AI Governance Principle?

An AI Governance principle is a high-level rule or expectation that guides organizational decisions concerning AI.

For example:

> **AI systems should have appropriate human oversight proportional to their potential impact.**

This is a principle.

The organization can then translate that principle into more specific requirements.

For example:

**Principle**

→ Human oversight should be appropriate.

**Requirement**

→ High-impact AI decisions require documented human review.

**Control**

→ The system must prevent final approval until an authorized human reviewer completes the required review.

**Evidence**

→ Human approval record.

This progression is essential for GRC professionals.

---

### 3. Principles Versus Requirements

These concepts should not be confused.

### Principle

Provides high-level direction.

Example:

> AI should be secure.

### Requirement

Defines what must be done.

Example:

> AI systems must undergo security assessment before production deployment.

### Control

Defines the mechanism used to satisfy the requirement.

Example:

> Security approval must be recorded in the AI deployment workflow.

### Evidence

Demonstrates implementation.

Example:

> Approved security assessment record.

The complete chain is:

**Principle → Requirement → Control → Evidence**

### 6. Principle 1 — Accountability

AI systems should have identifiable accountability.

The organization should know:

* Who owns the AI system
* Who owns the business process
* Who manages the model
* Who manages the data
* Who accepts residual risk
* Who approves deployment
* Who responds to incidents

Accountability prevents situations where AI decisions are effectively made by a system with no responsible human or organizational owner.

---

### 7. Accountability Is Not the Same as Responsibility

These terms are closely related but can be distinguished.

**Responsibility**

Refers to the obligation to perform a task.

**Accountability**

Refers to being answerable for the outcome.

For example:

An AI engineer may be **responsible** for implementing a model.

The business owner may remain **accountable** for the business use of that model.

This distinction is important when creating AI RACI matrices.

---

### 8. Principle 2 — Transparency

AI use should be sufficiently transparent for relevant stakeholders.

Transparency may involve communicating:

* That AI is being used
* Why AI is being used
* What the AI does
* Important limitations
* Relevant risks
* Who is accountable

Transparency requirements should be proportional to:

* Risk
* Impact
* Audience
* Regulatory requirements

Transparency does not necessarily mean disclosing proprietary algorithms or confidential technical information.

---

### 9. Principle 3 — Explainability

Explainability concerns the ability to understand relevant AI outputs or decisions.

For example:

If AI recommends rejecting a financial application, stakeholders may need to understand the significant factors behind the recommendation.

Explainability requirements depend on:

* AI type
* Decision impact
* Risk
* Stakeholder needs
* Legal requirements

Explainability and transparency are therefore related but not identical.

---

### 10. Transparency Versus Explainability

A useful distinction is:

**Transparency**

> "Tell me that AI is being used and explain what it is intended to do."

**Explainability**

> "Help me understand why the AI produced this particular result."

A system can be transparent without being sufficiently explainable.

For example:

> "This decision was assisted by AI."

is transparent.

But it does not necessarily explain:

> "Why did the AI reach this decision?"

---

### 11. Principle 4 — Human Oversight

AI systems should have appropriate human oversight.

Human oversight may include:

* Review
* Approval
* Intervention
* Override
* Escalation
* Appeal

The required level should be proportional to risk.

Low-impact AI may require limited human intervention.

High-impact AI may require mandatory human review before a consequential decision is finalized.

---

### 12. Meaningful Human Oversight

Human oversight should not be merely symbolic.

A human reviewer should have:

* Sufficient information
* Appropriate authority
* Relevant competence
* Adequate time
* Ability to challenge the AI
* Ability to override the AI where appropriate

A process where humans automatically approve every AI recommendation without review may technically contain a human but lack meaningful oversight.

---

### 13. Principle 5 — Fairness

AI systems should be designed and operated to reduce inappropriate or unjustified unfair outcomes.

Fairness may involve evaluating:

* Different demographic groups
* Different user groups
* Different geographic populations
* Different business segments

Fairness is context-dependent.

There is no single universal mathematical definition that applies to every AI system.

---

### 14. Principle 6 — Non-Discrimination

AI should not produce unlawful or unjustified discriminatory outcomes.

Potential discrimination can arise from:

* Training data
* Historical decisions
* Model design
* Proxy variables
* Deployment context
* Human interpretation

Organizations should therefore consider discrimination risks throughout the AI lifecycle.

---

### 15. Principle 7 — Privacy

AI systems should protect personal information and respect applicable privacy requirements.

Privacy principles may include:

* Purpose limitation
* Data minimization
* Appropriate processing
* Access control
* Retention
* Security
* Data subject rights

Privacy should be considered during AI design rather than added only after deployment.

---

### 16. Principle 8 — Security

AI systems should be protected against relevant threats.

Security should cover:

* Models
* Data
* Applications
* APIs
* Infrastructure
* Identity
* Access
* Agents
* Integrations
* Supply chain

AI Security is therefore both a technical and governance concern.

---

### 17. Principle 9 — Safety

AI systems should not create unacceptable safety risks.

Safety is particularly important for AI used in:

* Healthcare
* Industrial environments
* Transportation
* Critical infrastructure
* Autonomous systems

Safety requirements should reflect the potential severity of harm.

---

### 18. Safety Versus Security

These concepts should be distinguished.

**Security**

Protects systems and information from threats such as unauthorized access, manipulation, or attacks.

**Safety**

Focuses on preventing unacceptable harm resulting from system behavior.

An AI system can therefore be:

**Secure but unsafe**

or

**Safe under normal conditions but insecure against attackers**.

Both dimensions must be considered.

---

### 19. Principle 10 — Reliability

AI systems should perform consistently enough for their intended purpose.

Reliability may involve:

* Consistent performance
* Availability
* Accuracy
* Stable operation
* Predictable behavior

Reliability requirements should be defined according to the business context.

---

### 20. Principle 11 — Robustness

Robustness concerns how well AI systems behave under unexpected or changing conditions.

A robust AI system should be designed to handle relevant:

* Input variations
* Environmental changes
* Data anomalies
* Unexpected requests
* Operational conditions

Robustness is especially important where AI operates in dynamic environments.

---

### 21. Principle 12 — Resilience

AI resilience concerns the ability to continue operating or recover when failures occur.

Resilience may require:

* Backup
* Recovery
* Failover
* Redundancy
* Alternative processes
* Model rollback
* Manual fallback

AI should not create a single point of failure for critical business operations without appropriate contingency arrangements.

---

### 22. Principle 13 — Traceability

AI activities should be sufficiently traceable to support governance and investigation.

Traceability may include:

* Model version
* Data source
* Configuration
* User
* Timestamp
* Input
* Output
* Decision
* Approval
* Change

The exact level of traceability should be proportional to risk.

---

### 23. Principle 14 — Auditability

AI systems and governance processes should generate sufficient evidence to support appropriate assurance.

Auditability may require:

* Logs
* Records
* Assessments
* Approvals
* Model documentation
* Monitoring results
* Incident records
* Change records

Auditability converts governance requirements into demonstrable evidence.

---

### 24. Principle 15 — Data Governance

AI should use data that is appropriately governed.

Data Governance should address:

* Quality
* Ownership
* Classification
* Provenance
* Lineage
* Integrity
* Access
* Retention

The principle can be summarized as:

> **AI governance cannot be stronger than the data governance supporting the AI system.**

---

### 25. Principle 16 — Proportionality

AI Governance should be proportional to the potential risk and impact of an AI system.

For example:

**Low-risk AI**

→ Basic controls.

**Moderate-risk AI**

→ Formal assessment and approval.

**High-risk AI**

→ Enhanced testing, oversight, monitoring, and assurance.

Proportionality prevents governance from becoming unnecessarily burdensome.

---

### 26. Principle 17 — Risk-Based Governance

AI Governance should prioritize resources according to risk.

The organization should consider:

* Likelihood
* Impact
* Exposure
* Business criticality
* Regulatory requirements
* Human impact
* Security risk

The highest-risk systems should receive the strongest governance attention.

---

### 27. Principle 18 — Purpose Limitation

AI should be used for clearly defined and legitimate purposes.

An organization should understand:

* Why the AI exists
* What it is authorized to do
* What data it may use
* What decisions it may influence
* What actions it may perform

Purpose expansion should trigger appropriate governance review.

---

### 28. Principle 19 — Data Minimization

AI systems should use only the data reasonably necessary for the intended purpose, subject to applicable requirements and legitimate business needs.

For example, an internal AI summarization tool may not need access to:

* Payroll records
* Customer databases
* Health information
* Executive correspondence

Unnecessary data access increases:

* Privacy risk
* Security risk
* Compliance risk

---

### 29. Principle 20 — Continuous Improvement

AI Governance should evolve as:

* Technology changes
* Threats change
* Regulations change
* Business use changes
* AI models change
* Incidents occur
* Audit findings emerge

A mature governance program therefore operates as a continuous improvement cycle.

### 30. AI Governance Principles and GRC

These principles become particularly valuable when translated into GRC structures.

For example:

**Principle**

AI systems should be secure.

↓

**Risk**

AI system may be compromised.

↓

**Control Objective**

↓

**Control**

↓

**Evidence**

Approved security assessment.

↓

**Testing**

Review whether assessment was completed.

↓

**Assurance**

Determine whether the control operates effectively.

This is the fundamental transformation from **AI principles to GRC controls**.

### 32. Principles Are Not Controls

One of the most important GRC distinctions is:

> **A principle describes what the organization believes or expects; a control demonstrates how that expectation is enforced or achieved.**

For example:

**Principle**

> AI should be accountable.

**Control**

> Every production AI system must have a designated business owner recorded in the AI inventory.

The second statement can be tested.

That makes it suitable for a control framework.

---

### 33. From Principle to Control

A practical mapping sequence is:

**Principle**

↓

What does the organization expect?

↓

**Requirement**

What must be done?

↓

**Risk**

What happens if it is not done?

↓

**Control Objective**

What must the control achieve?

↓

**Control**

What mechanism ensures it happens?

↓

**Evidence**

What proves it happened?

↓

**Test**

How do we verify it?

This sequence is central to AI GRC implementation.

### 34. Example — Accountability

**Principle**

AI systems must have accountability.

**Requirement**

Every material AI system must have an accountable owner.

**Risk**

No individual is accountable for AI-related failures.

**Control**

AI systems cannot enter production without a designated owner.

**Evidence**

Approved AI inventory record.

**Test**

Sample production AI systems and verify ownership.

---

### 35. Example — Human Oversight

**Principle**

High-impact AI requires meaningful human oversight.

**Requirement**

High-impact AI decisions must receive human review.

**Risk**

AI produces an inappropriate decision without meaningful human intervention.

**Control**

System requires documented human approval before final execution.

**Evidence**

Approval record.

**Test**

Review sampled decisions for evidence of human review.

---

### 36. Example — Security

**Principle**

AI systems should be secure.

**Requirement**

AI systems must undergo security assessment before deployment.

**Risk**

AI system contains exploitable security weaknesses.

**Control**

Security approval is mandatory before production deployment.

**Evidence**

Security assessment and approval.

**Test**

Verify assessment and approval for sampled systems.

---

### 37. Example — Transparency

**Principle**

Relevant stakeholders should understand when AI is being used.

**Requirement**

Users must be informed when interacting directly with an AI system where appropriate.

**Risk**

Users may incorrectly assume they are interacting with a human.

**Control**

AI interfaces display an appropriate disclosure.

**Evidence**

Interface configuration and test result.

**Test**

Verify disclosure in production.

---

### 38. Example — Fairness

AI should avoid inappropriate discriminatory outcomes.

**Requirement**

Relevant high-risk AI systems must undergo documented fairness assessment.

**Risk**

The AI system produces unjustified disparate outcomes.

**Control**

Fairness testing is required before deployment and periodically thereafter.

**Evidence**

Fairness assessment report.

**Test**

Verify testing was performed and findings addressed.

### 42. AI Governance Principles and Decision Rights

Principles should influence who can make decisions.

For example:

**Low-risk AI**

→ Business owner approval.

**Moderate-risk AI**

→ Business + GRC approval.

**High-risk AI**

→ Cross-functional governance approval.

**Critical AI**

→ Executive or governance committee approval.

### 43. AI Governance Principles and Exceptions

Principles should not automatically eliminate the possibility of exceptions.

However, exceptions should be:

* Documented
* Risk-assessed
* Approved
* Time-limited
* Monitored
* Reassessed

An exception should not silently become the normal operating model.

### 44. AI Governance Principles and Assurance

Assurance should evaluate whether principles have been translated effectively.

An auditor may ask:

> Is accountability defined?

> Is accountability implemented?

> Is evidence available?

> Is the control effective?

This creates a chain from:

**Principle**

to

**Assurance**.

---

### 45. Core Principle for GRC Professionals

A useful mental model is:

> **Principles define expectations. Governance assigns authority. Risk identifies uncertainty. Controls manage risk. Evidence demonstrates implementation. Assurance evaluates effectiveness.**

This is the foundation for converting AI Governance from a conceptual discipline into an auditable enterprise capability.

The most important concepts are:

1. AI Governance principles provide high-level direction.
2. Principles should be translated into requirements.
3. Requirements should be translated into controls.
4. Controls require evidence.
5. Evidence enables testing and assurance.
6. Accountability must be clearly assigned.
7. Transparency and explainability are related but distinct.
8. Human oversight must be meaningful.
9. Fairness and non-discrimination require ongoing evaluation.
10. Privacy and security should be integrated into AI governance.
11. Safety should be considered independently from security.
12. Reliability, robustness, and resilience address different dimensions of AI trust.
13. Traceability and auditability support governance and assurance.
14. Data Governance is foundational to AI Governance.
15. Governance should be proportional to risk.
16. AI Governance should be risk-based rather than universally restrictive.
17. Purpose limitation and data minimization reduce unnecessary exposure.
18. Continuous improvement is essential because AI environments change.
19. Principles should ultimately become measurable control requirements.
20. A mature GRC professional should be able to trace an AI principle all the way to evidence and assurance.

### 47. Closing Perspective

AI Governance principles provide the **philosophical and organizational foundation** of AI governance.

But principles alone are insufficient.

The mature GRC approach is:

**Principle**

→ **Requirement**

→ **Risk**

→ **Control Objective**

→ **Control**

→ **Evidence**

→ **Testing**

→ **Assurance**

→ **Continuous Improvement**

This transformation is what allows an organization to move from statements such as:

> **"We use AI responsibly."**

to a demonstrable governance position:

> **"We have defined what responsible AI means, identified the associated risks, implemented controls, collected evidence, tested those controls, monitored performance, and independently assured the effectiveness of the governance framework."**

AI Governance principles become useful only when they are translated into concrete governance requirements that can be assigned, implemented, measured, and tested. This section examines how the core principles become operational requirements and how they connect to enterprise GRC.

### 48. Principle-to-Requirement Translation

The first step in operationalizing an AI Governance principle is to translate the principle into a clear requirement.

For example:

**Principle**

AI systems must be accountable.

↓

**Requirement**

Every material AI system must have an identified business owner and technical owner.

↓

**Control**

The AI inventory must require owner assignment before the system can be approved for production.

↓

**Evidence**

Approved AI inventory record.

The requirement should be sufficiently specific that an organization can determine whether it has been satisfied.

### 49. Characteristics of a Good AI Governance Requirement

An effective governance requirement should be:

* Clear
* Specific
* Relevant
* Risk-based
* Testable
* Assignable
* Measurable
* Documentable
* Enforceable

A requirement such as:

> "AI must be trustworthy."

is difficult to test.

A stronger requirement would be:

> "High-impact AI systems must undergo documented validation before production deployment."

The second requirement can be assigned, measured, and audited.

---

### 50. Mandatory Versus Advisory Requirements

AI Governance requirements can be divided into different categories.

### Mandatory

Must be followed.

Example:

> High-risk AI systems require formal approval before production deployment.

### Conditional

Apply when specific conditions exist.

Example:

> AI systems processing personal data must undergo an appropriate privacy assessment.

### Advisory

Provide recommended practices.

Example:

> AI owners should periodically review model documentation.

This distinction prevents governance frameworks from becoming unnecessarily rigid.

---

### 51. Risk-Based Governance Requirements

Not every AI system should be subject to identical requirements.

A simple model could be:

| AI Risk  | Governance Intensity |
| -------- | -------------------- |
| Low      | Basic                |
| Moderate | Enhanced             |
| High     | Strong               |
| Critical | Maximum              |

For example, a low-risk AI tool used to summarize internal meeting notes may require limited governance.

An AI system supporting medical diagnosis would require significantly stronger controls.

### 92. Control Design Effectiveness

A control is design-effective when it is appropriately designed to address the intended risk.

Example:

**Risk**

Unauthorized AI system deployment.

**Control**

AI systems require governance approval before production.

The auditor should ask:

> Would this control, if properly operated, reasonably address the identified risk?

If the answer is no, the control has a design deficiency.

---

### 93. Operating Effectiveness

A control is operating effectively when it actually functions as designed.

For example:

**Control**

High-risk AI requires approval before production.

**Test**

Sample high-risk AI deployments and verify approval evidence.

A control may therefore be:

* Well designed but poorly operated
* Poorly designed but consistently operated
* Well designed and well operated
* Neither well designed nor well operated

This distinction is fundamental to GRC assurance.

### 106. Inherent and Residual AI Risk

AI risk should distinguish between:

**Inherent Risk**

Risk before controls.

**Residual Risk**

Risk remaining after controls.

For example:

> An AI system processes sensitive information.

Before controls:

**High inherent privacy risk**

After implementing:

* Access control
* Encryption
* Data minimization
* Retention controls
* Monitoring

the organization may determine:

**Moderate residual risk**

The residual risk must then be compared with organizational risk appetite.

### 155. Key Takeaways

AI Governance should operate as an integrated component of Enterprise GRC.

The major relationships are:

* **Enterprise Risk Management** → identifies and manages AI risk.
* **Cybersecurity** → protects AI systems and data.
* **Privacy** → governs personal information.
* **Data Governance** → governs AI data.
* **Third-Party Risk** → governs external AI providers.
* **Business Continuity** → addresses AI dependency.
* **Change Management** → governs model and system changes.
* **IAM** → controls AI access and privileges.
* **SOC** → detects AI security events.
* **Internal Audit** → provides independent assurance.
* **GRC Platforms** → integrate workflows, controls, evidence, and reporting.

The central principle is:

> **AI Governance should connect AI-specific risks to the organization's existing governance, risk, compliance, security, privacy, and assurance infrastructure.**

This allows AI Governance to scale as AI adoption increases without creating unnecessary parallel processes.

### 156. AI Governance Principles — Implementation, Measurement, Assurance, and Continuous Improvement

The final stage of operationalizing AI Governance principles is ensuring that the principles are **implemented, measured, monitored, tested, reported, and continuously improved**.

A governance principle has limited value if it exists only in a policy document.

A mature organization must be able to demonstrate:

> **What principle exists?**

> **What requirement implements it?**

> **What risk does it address?**

> **What control supports it?**

> **What evidence demonstrates implementation?**

> **How is effectiveness measured?**

> **Who provides assurance?**

This creates the complete AI Governance accountability chain:

**Principle → Requirement → Risk → Control → Evidence → Measurement → Assurance → Improvement**

### 158. AI Governance Control Design

Each control should answer five fundamental questions:

1. **What risk is being addressed?**
2. **What must the control accomplish?**
3. **Who owns the control?**
4. **How does the control operate?**
5. **What evidence demonstrates operation?**

For example:

**Risk**

Unauthorized deployment of AI.

**Control Objective**

Ensure AI systems are approved before production.

**Control**

All material AI systems must receive documented governance approval before production deployment.

**Owner**

AI Governance Manager.

**Evidence**

Approved governance record.

This makes the control suitable for GRC management and assurance.

### 159. Preventive, Detective, and Corrective AI Controls

Designed to respond to an identified problem.

A mature control framework normally requires a combination of all three.
