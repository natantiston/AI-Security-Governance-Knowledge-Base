# 01.16 — AI Decision Rights and Risk Acceptance

## Part 1 — Decision Rights, Authority and AI Governance Decision-Making

### 1. Introduction to AI Decision Rights

AI Security Governance requires clear decision rights.

AI systems can involve decisions across:

* Business strategy
* Risk
* Security
* Privacy
* Technology
* Data
* Compliance
* Legal
* Operations

Without clearly defined decision rights, AI governance can result in:

* Conflicting decisions
* Delayed approvals
* Unclear accountability
* Unauthorized risk acceptance
* Governance gaps
* Excessive escalation

Decision rights establish **who has the authority to make a particular AI-related decision**.

---

### 2. Meaning of AI Decision Rights

AI decision rights define the authority assigned to individuals, committees, or organizational functions to make specific AI governance decisions.

Examples include authority to:

* Approve an AI use case
* Approve an AI system for production
* Classify AI risk
* Accept residual risk
* Approve exceptions
* Authorize high-risk AI
* Suspend an AI system
* Approve material changes
* Retire an AI system

Decision rights should be explicitly documented.

---

### 3. Decision Rights Versus Responsibility

Responsibility and decision authority are related but different.

**Responsibility**

→ The obligation to perform or manage an activity.

**Decision authority**

→ The authority to make the final decision.

For example, a cybersecurity team may be responsible for performing a security assessment, while an AI Governance Committee may have authority to approve the deployment.

---

### 4. Decision Rights Versus Accountability

Accountability identifies who is answerable for the outcome.

Decision authority identifies who can make the decision.

A business executive may be accountable for an AI system while a specialized committee has authority to approve specific high-risk controls.

Both relationships should be clearly defined.

---

### 5. Principle of Clear Decision Authority

Every material AI governance decision should have a clearly identified decision authority.

The organization should be able to answer:

* Who decides?
* What can they decide?
* Under what conditions?
* What information must they consider?
* What authority limits apply?
* When must the matter be escalated?

Ambiguous decision authority creates governance risk.

---

### 6. Decision Rights Across the AI Lifecycle

Decision rights should exist throughout the AI lifecycle.

They may apply during:

1. Strategy
2. Ideation
3. Intake
4. Design
5. Development
6. Testing
7. Approval
8. Deployment
9. Operation
10. Change
11. Incident response
12. Retirement

Different decision authorities may apply at different stages.

---

### 7. AI Use-Case Approval

Organizations should define who can approve an AI use case.

Approval may consider:

* Business purpose
* Expected benefits
* Data involved
* Risk classification
* Security requirements
* Privacy implications
* Regulatory obligations

Low-risk use cases may follow streamlined approval, while high-risk use cases may require enhanced governance.

---

### 8. AI Risk Classification Authority

The organization should define who can classify AI systems according to risk.

Possible authorities include:

* AI risk function
* Enterprise Risk Management
* AI Governance Committee
* Business risk owner
* Specialized risk assessor

Risk classification should be based on defined criteria rather than individual preference.

---

### 9. Risk Classification Challenge

Governance should provide a mechanism to challenge an AI risk classification.

For example:

**Business owner**

→ Classifies system as moderate risk.

**Security or Risk function**

→ Determines that the system has characteristics requiring high-risk treatment.

The organization should have a formal mechanism for resolving the disagreement.

---

### 10. AI Security Approval Authority

The organization should establish who can approve security readiness for AI systems.

Security approval may depend on:

* Risk level
* System criticality
* Data sensitivity
* External exposure
* Autonomous capability
* Regulatory significance

Security approval should not be granted solely because a business owner wants to deploy the system.

---

### 11. AI Privacy Approval Authority

Where AI processing involves personal data, organizations should establish appropriate privacy decision rights.

The responsible privacy function may have authority concerning:

* Privacy assessments
* Data-processing requirements
* Privacy risks
* High-risk processing
* Data protection measures

The exact authority should reflect the organization's legal and governance structure.

---

### 12. AI Production Deployment Authority

Organizations should define who can authorize production deployment.

Deployment authority may require confirmation that:

* Required assessments are complete
* Security controls are implemented
* Privacy requirements are addressed
* Risks are within tolerance
* Required approvals are obtained
* Monitoring is operational

Production deployment should be a controlled governance decision.

---

### 13. AI Change Approval Authority

Material AI changes should have defined approval authority.

Changes may include:

* New model
* Significant retraining
* New dataset
* New integration
* New user population
* Increased autonomy
* New geographic deployment

The approval level should reflect the potential impact of the change.

---

### 14. AI Suspension Authority

Organizations should define who can suspend or restrict an AI system.

Suspension may be required when:

* A serious security incident occurs
* Unsafe behavior is detected
* Material controls fail
* Regulatory requirements are breached
* Significant unauthorized activity occurs

Emergency suspension authority should be clear before an incident occurs.

---

### 15. Emergency Decision Rights

Emergency situations may require decisions outside normal governance workflows.

Emergency decision rights should establish:

* Who can act immediately
* What actions can be taken
* What conditions trigger emergency authority
* Who must be notified
* How the decision is documented
* How normal governance is restored

Emergency authority should be clearly bounded.

---

### 16. Delegated AI Decision Authority

Decision authority may be delegated where appropriate.

Delegation should specify:

* Authority granted
* Scope
* Risk limits
* Duration
* Conditions
* Escalation requirements

Delegation should not create uncontrolled authority.

---

### 17. Limits of Delegated Authority

Delegated authority should have defined boundaries.

For example, an individual may be authorized to approve:

* Low-risk AI systems

but may not be authorized to approve:

* High-risk AI
* Material security exceptions
* Significant regulatory risks

Authority limits should correspond to risk.

---

### 18. AI Governance Approval Thresholds

Organizations should establish thresholds for different levels of governance decisions.

Thresholds may be based on:

* Risk score
* Financial impact
* Data sensitivity
* Business criticality
* Regulatory significance
* Human impact
* Autonomy

Higher thresholds should generally require greater authority.

---

### 19. Risk-Based Decision Authority

Decision authority should increase as AI risk increases.

A simplified model may be:

**Low Risk**

→ Operational approval.

**Moderate Risk**

→ Business and specialist review.

**High Risk**

→ Senior management or governance committee approval.

**Critical Risk**

→ Executive or board-level oversight where appropriate.

The exact structure should reflect the organization's governance model.

---

### 20. AI Governance Decision Matrix

Organizations should maintain a decision-rights matrix.

Example:

| Decision              | Business Owner | Security | Risk | Privacy | AI Governance Committee | Executive |
| --------------------- | -------------- | -------- | ---- | ------- | ----------------------- | --------- |
| AI use case           | A              | C        | C    | C       | I                       | I         |
| Risk classification   | C              | C        | A    | C       | I                       | I         |
| Security approval     | C              | A        | C    | I       | I                       | I         |
| Privacy approval      | C              | I        | C    | A       | I                       | I         |
| High-risk AI approval | C              | C        | C    | C       | A                       | I         |
| Risk acceptance       | C              | C        | A*   | C       | A*                      | A*        |
| Emergency suspension  | C              | A        | C    | I       | A                       | I         |

`A*` depends on the organization's risk threshold and delegated authority.

The matrix should be formally approved and maintained.

---

### 21. RACI for AI Decisions

A RACI model can complement the decision-rights matrix.

For each decision, organizations can identify:

* **Responsible**
* **Accountable**
* **Consulted**
* **Informed**

RACI clarifies participation, while decision rights clarify authority.

---

### 22. AI Governance Committees

An AI Governance Committee may serve as a cross-functional decision-making body.

Its responsibilities may include:

* Reviewing high-risk AI
* Resolving governance conflicts
* Approving exceptions
* Reviewing material AI risks
* Establishing governance requirements
* Escalating significant risks

The committee's authority should be formally documented.

---

### 23. AI Governance Committee Membership

Membership should reflect the organization's AI risk profile.

Potential participants include:

* AI leadership
* CISO
* CIO
* CTO
* CRO
* DPO
* Legal
* Compliance
* Data governance
* Business leadership
* Internal Audit in an appropriate advisory or assurance capacity

Membership should avoid unnecessary complexity while providing the expertise required for significant decisions.

---

### 24. Separation of Decision Roles

Organizations should consider separating certain roles to reduce conflicts of interest.

For example, the individual responsible for delivering an AI system may not be the sole authority for accepting significant risks created by that system.

Separation should be proportionate to risk.

---

### 25. Segregation of Duties

Segregation of duties may be appropriate where AI activities involve significant risk.

Potential separation may exist between:

* Development
* Security assessment
* Approval
* Deployment
* Risk acceptance
* Monitoring

No single individual should have excessive control over a material AI process where separation is necessary.

---

### 26. Independent Challenge

AI governance should allow qualified functions to challenge proposed decisions.

Challenge may come from:

* Risk
* Security
* Privacy
* Compliance
* Legal
* Internal assurance

Challenge should be based on evidence, defined requirements, and risk considerations.

---

### 27. Management Override

Senior management may sometimes override a recommendation or control decision.

Where this occurs, governance should require:

* Documented rationale
* Identification of the risk
* Appropriate authority
* Risk acceptance where required
* Compensating controls where possible

Management override should remain visible and auditable.

---

### 28. Escalation Rights

Governance functions should have the ability to escalate material AI risks.

Escalation may be required when:

* Risk exceeds appetite
* Controls are insufficient
* Disagreement cannot be resolved
* Regulatory exposure exists
* Safety concerns exist
* Security incidents are material

Escalation should lead to an identified decision authority.

---

### 29. AI Decision Documentation

Material AI decisions should be documented.

Decision records may include:

* Decision
* Date
* Decision maker
* Participants
* Evidence
* Risk assessment
* Conditions
* Exceptions
* Follow-up actions

Documentation supports accountability and future review.

---

### 30. AI Decision Traceability

Organizations should be able to trace significant decisions through their supporting governance records.

For example:

**AI Use Case**

→ Risk Classification

→ Security Assessment

→ Privacy Assessment

→ Governance Review

→ Risk Acceptance

→ Production Approval

→ Monitoring

This traceability demonstrates how governance decisions were reached and implemented.

## Part 2 — AI Risk Acceptance, Risk Appetite and Residual Risk

### 31. Introduction to AI Risk Acceptance

AI governance cannot eliminate every risk associated with AI systems.

Organizations must therefore establish a formal mechanism for deciding which risks are:

* Accepted
* Mitigated
* Transferred
* Avoided
* Escalated

**Risk acceptance** is the formal decision to tolerate a known level of residual risk within established authority and risk appetite.

---

### 32. Inherent AI Risk

Inherent risk is the level of risk that exists before considering the effectiveness of controls.

AI inherent risk may arise from:

* AI autonomy
* Data sensitivity
* Model behavior
* Business criticality
* External exposure
* Human impact
* Regulatory exposure
* Third-party dependency

Inherent risk provides the starting point for risk assessment.

---

### 33. Control Risk Reduction

Security, privacy, governance, and operational controls can reduce AI risk.

For example:

**Inherent Risk**

→ Unauthorized access to an AI system.

**Controls**

→ Strong authentication, least privilege, monitoring, and access reviews.

**Residual Risk**

→ Remaining risk after controls are applied.

The effectiveness of the controls must be evaluated rather than assumed.

---

### 34. Residual AI Risk

Residual risk is the risk remaining after controls have been implemented.

Residual risk may remain because:

* Controls cannot eliminate the threat
* Some risks are inherent to AI behavior
* Controls have limitations
* New threats emerge
* Data or models remain uncertain
* Business requirements require continued exposure

Residual risk is the primary subject of formal risk acceptance.

---

### 35. Risk Acceptance Versus Risk Elimination

Risk acceptance does not mean that an organization has eliminated the risk.

It means that an authorized decision maker has determined that the remaining exposure is acceptable under defined conditions.

For example:

> The organization acknowledges that a defined residual risk remains and accepts it within the approved risk tolerance.

The risk should continue to be monitored after acceptance.

---

### 36. AI Risk Appetite

Risk appetite describes the amount and type of risk an organization is generally willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives.

AI risk appetite may establish boundaries for:

* Security
* Privacy
* Safety
* Financial impact
* Regulatory exposure
* Operational disruption
* Reputational impact

Risk appetite provides the strategic context for individual risk decisions.

---

### 37. AI Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance defines acceptable variation around established risk objectives or thresholds.

For example, an organization may have:

**Risk Appetite**

→ Low tolerance for unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information.

**Risk Tolerance**

→ No unapproved disclosure of specified categories of sensitive data.

Risk tolerance can therefore provide more operationally meaningful boundaries.

---

### 38. AI Risk Capacity

Risk capacity represents the maximum level of risk the organization can withstand before its viability or ability to achieve objectives is materially threatened.

Risk capacity may be influenced by:

* Financial resources
* Regulatory obligations
* Operational resilience
* Legal exposure
* Safety requirements
* Reputation
* Customer impact

Risk capacity generally represents a boundary that should not be exceeded.

---

### 39. Relationship Between Appetite, Tolerance and Capacity

These concepts can be viewed as:

**Risk Capacity**

→ Maximum risk the organization can withstand.

**Risk Appetite**

→ Amount of risk the organization is willing to pursue or accept.

**Risk Tolerance**

→ Permitted variation around defined risk objectives.

Individual AI risk decisions should remain within these boundaries unless appropriately escalated.

---

### 40. AI Risk Acceptance Criteria

Organizations should establish criteria for determining whether AI risk may be accepted.

Criteria may consider:

* Risk severity
* Likelihood
* Business benefit
* Control effectiveness
* Regulatory requirements
* Data sensitivity
* Human impact
* Safety implications
* Risk appetite

Acceptance should be based on documented evidence.

---

### 41. Risk Acceptance Authority

Not every employee should have authority to accept every level of AI risk.

Authority should be aligned with risk.

For example:

| Risk Level | Potential Acceptance Authority                         |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Low        | Operational owner                                      |
| Moderate   | Business/risk management                               |
| High       | Senior management or risk committee                    |
| Critical   | Executive authority or other designated governing body |

The exact thresholds should be defined by the organization.

---

### 42. Risk Acceptance Thresholds

Organizations should establish quantitative or qualitative thresholds where practical.

Thresholds may be based on:

* Risk scores
* Financial exposure
* Data classification
* Regulatory significance
* System criticality
* Human impact

Thresholds help prevent inconsistent risk acceptance decisions.

---

### 43. Risk Acceptance Documentation

Every material risk acceptance decision should be documented.

A risk acceptance record may include:

* Risk description
* Affected AI system
* Risk assessment
* Existing controls
* Residual risk
* Business justification
* Acceptance authority
* Acceptance date
* Expiration date
* Conditions
* Monitoring requirements

Documentation establishes accountability.

---

### 44. Business Justification for Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance should normally include a business justification.

The justification may explain:

* Why the AI capability is needed
* Why the risk cannot currently be eliminated
* What benefits the system provides
* What alternatives were considered
* Why the remaining exposure is considered tolerable

Business benefit should not automatically justify unacceptable risk.

---

### 45. Risk Acceptance Conditions

Risk acceptance may be conditional.

Conditions may require:

* Additional monitoring
* Compensating controls
* Remediation
* Limited deployment
* Restricted users
* Reduced permissions
* Additional testing

Acceptance conditions should be measurable where possible.

---

### 46. Temporary Risk Acceptance

Some risk acceptance decisions should be temporary.

Temporary acceptance may be appropriate when:

* Remediation is underway
* A control is being implemented
* A technology limitation exists
* A replacement system is planned
* A transition period is required

Temporary acceptance should have a defined expiration or review date.

---

### 47. Permanent Risk Acceptance

Some residual risks may be inherently associated with an AI system and may remain for its operational lifetime.

Permanent acceptance should still be:

* Explicit
* Authorized
* Documented
* Periodically reviewed

"Permanent" should not mean that the risk is ignored.

---

### 48. Expiration of Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance should not automatically remain valid indefinitely.

An acceptance may expire because:

* The system changes
* The risk changes
* Controls change
* Regulations change
* The business purpose changes
* The acceptance period ends

Expired acceptance should trigger reassessment or remediation.

---

### 49. Risk Acceptance Review

Accepted risks should be periodically reviewed.

The review should determine whether:

* The original assumptions remain valid
* Risk has increased
* Controls remain effective
* New mitigation options exist
* The business justification remains valid

Risk acceptance should therefore be treated as an active governance decision.

---

### 50. Risk Acceptance Register

Organizations should maintain a centralized or appropriately controlled register of material accepted AI risks.

The register may include:

* Risk ID
* AI system
* Risk description
* Risk owner
* Acceptance authority
* Risk rating
* Controls
* Residual risk
* Acceptance date
* Expiration/review date
* Status

The register provides visibility into accumulated risk exposure.

---

### 51. Aggregated AI Risk

Individual accepted risks may appear manageable while the combined exposure becomes significant.

For example:

* Multiple AI systems
* Multiple third-party providers
* Multiple sensitive datasets
* Multiple autonomous agents

may collectively create substantial enterprise exposure.

AI governance should therefore consider both individual and aggregated risks.

---

### 52. Risk Concentration

Organizations should identify concentration of AI risk.

Concentration may occur when multiple systems depend on:

* One AI provider
* One model
* One cloud platform
* One dataset
* One infrastructure component

A common dependency may create systemic exposure.

---

### 53. Risk Transfer

Risk may sometimes be transferred partially through:

* Insurance
* Contracts
* Service agreements
* Vendor obligations
* Indemnification

Risk transfer does not necessarily eliminate the organization's underlying accountability.

Organizations should understand exactly which risks are transferred and which remain.

---

### 54. Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance involves deciding not to undertake an AI activity because the associated exposure is unacceptable.

Examples may include:

* Rejecting a high-risk use case
* Prohibiting certain data from being processed
* Preventing autonomous execution
* Refusing an unsuitable third-party service

Avoidance may be appropriate where risk cannot reasonably be reduced to an acceptable level.

---

### 55. Risk Mitigation

Risk mitigation involves implementing controls that reduce likelihood, impact, or both.

Examples include:

* Access controls
* Human oversight
* Security testing
* Data protection
* Monitoring
* Model validation
* Rate limiting
* Segmentation

Mitigation should be selected based on the specific risk.

---

### 56. Risk Acceptance After Mitigation

Organizations should generally evaluate residual risk after mitigation has been implemented.

The process can be represented as:

**Identify Risk**

→ **Assess Inherent Risk**

→ **Select Controls**

→ **Implement Controls**

→ **Evaluate Control Effectiveness**

→ **Assess Residual Risk**

→ **Accept, Escalate, Further Mitigate, Transfer, or Avoid**

This prevents organizations from accepting risk based solely on the existence of controls.

---

### 57. Risk Acceptance and Control Exceptions

Risk acceptance and control exceptions are related but different.

**Control Exception**

→ Permission to deviate from a defined control requirement.

**Risk Acceptance**

→ Formal acceptance of the resulting residual risk.

An exception may require risk acceptance, but not every risk acceptance necessarily represents a policy or control exception.

---

### 58. Risk Acceptance and Risk Ownership

Every material AI risk should have an identified risk owner.

The risk owner is responsible for:

* Understanding the risk
* Ensuring appropriate treatment
* Monitoring the risk
* Escalating changes
* Maintaining the risk record

The risk owner may not necessarily be the person authorized to formally accept the risk.

---

### 59. Risk Owner Versus Risk Acceptance Authority

These roles should be distinguished.

For example:

**AI Business Owner**

→ Owns the business risk.

**Risk Function**

→ Provides independent challenge and assessment.

**Senior Executive**

→ Has authority to accept risk above the business owner's delegated threshold.

This separation strengthens governance.

---

### 60. Risk Acceptance and Segregation of Duties

High-risk AI decisions may require separation between:

* Person proposing the AI system
* Person assessing the risk
* Person approving deployment
* Person accepting residual risk
* Person providing independent assurance

The appropriate degree of separation depends on risk.

---

### 61. Risk Acceptance and Independent Challenge

Risk functions, cybersecurity, privacy, compliance, and other relevant functions should be able to challenge risk acceptance decisions.

Challenge may identify:

* Incorrect risk ratings
* Missing controls
* Weak business justification
* Inadequate evidence
* Unrecognized regulatory requirements

Challenge does not necessarily mean veto authority unless formally granted.

---

### 62. Risk Acceptance Disagreement

Organizations should define what happens when stakeholders disagree about risk acceptance.

For example:

**Business Owner**

→ Considers risk acceptable.

**CISO**

→ Considers security exposure excessive.

**Risk Function**

→ Determines escalation is required.

The governance framework should identify the next decision authority.

---

### 63. Risk Escalation

Risk should be escalated when it exceeds delegated authority or defined tolerance.

Escalation triggers may include:

* High residual risk
* Regulatory exposure
* Safety implications
* Significant financial exposure
* Major customer impact
* Material security weakness
* Insufficient compensating controls

Escalation ensures that significant decisions reach the appropriate authority.

---

### 64. Risk Acceptance and AI System Criticality

Risk acceptance should consider the criticality of the AI system.

A moderate technical risk affecting a non-critical application may be treated differently from the same technical risk affecting:

* Critical infrastructure
* Financial operations
* Healthcare processes
* Safety-sensitive systems
* Essential business services

Risk is contextual.

---

### 65. Risk Acceptance and AI Autonomy

The degree of AI autonomy should influence risk acceptance.

Higher autonomy may increase the need for:

* Stronger controls
* Human oversight
* Monitoring
* Approval gates
* Emergency intervention
* Lower risk tolerance

An AI system capable of independently taking consequential actions generally requires stronger governance than a system providing informational assistance.

---

### 66. Risk Acceptance and Data Sensitivity

Risk acceptance should consider the sensitivity of data processed by AI.

Systems processing:

* Public information
* Internal information
* Confidential information
* Sensitive personal information
* Highly restricted information

may require progressively stronger controls and decision authority.

---

### 67. Risk Acceptance and Third-Party AI

Third-party AI services can introduce risks that the organization cannot directly control.

Risk acceptance should therefore consider:

* Provider security
* Data handling
* Contractual protections
* Service dependency
* Subprocessors
* Provider resilience
* Transparency
* Exit options

Residual third-party risk should have an accountable owner.

---

### 68. Risk Acceptance and Model Uncertainty

AI models may produce uncertain or variable outputs.

Risk acceptance should consider:

* Accuracy
* Reliability
* Model limitations
* Known failure modes
* Testing results
* Monitoring capability

Uncertainty should be treated as a governance consideration rather than simply a technical characteristic.

---

### 69. Risk Acceptance and Emerging AI Risks

AI risks can change rapidly.

New risks may emerge from:

* New attack techniques
* New models
* New capabilities
* New integrations
* Regulatory developments
* Changes in usage patterns

Previously accepted risk may therefore become unacceptable.

---

### 70. Dynamic Risk Acceptance

AI risk acceptance should be capable of changing as the AI environment changes.

The governance cycle should therefore be:

**Assess**

→ **Treat**

→ **Accept**

→ **Monitor**

→ **Reassess**

→ **Reaccept, Modify, Escalate, or Reject**

This ensures that risk acceptance remains an active governance mechanism rather than a one-time administrative decision.

## Part 3 — Risk Acceptance Governance, Exceptions, Escalation and Assurance

### 71. Risk Acceptance as a Formal Governance Process

Risk acceptance should be treated as a formal governance process rather than an informal agreement between individuals.

A mature process establishes:

* Defined risk criteria
* Defined decision authorities
* Documented evidence
* Approval requirements
* Review periods
* Escalation mechanisms
* Monitoring requirements

This creates consistency in how AI risks are accepted across the organization.

---

### 72. AI Risk Acceptance Workflow

A formal risk acceptance workflow may include:

1. Identify the risk.
2. Assess inherent risk.
3. Identify applicable controls.
4. Implement mitigation.
5. Assess residual risk.
6. Compare residual risk with risk appetite.
7. Determine decision authority.
8. Document the decision.
9. Obtain approval.
10. Establish monitoring.
11. Review periodically.

The workflow should be integrated with enterprise risk management.

---

### 73. Risk Acceptance Request

A risk acceptance request should contain enough information for an informed decision.

It may include:

* AI system name
* Business owner
* Risk owner
* Risk description
* Threat or cause
* Potential impact
* Likelihood
* Inherent risk
* Existing controls
* Residual risk
* Proposed treatment
* Business justification
* Requested acceptance period

Incomplete requests should normally be returned for additional information.

---

### 74. Risk Acceptance Evidence

Decision makers should receive evidence appropriate to the risk.

Evidence may include:

* Security assessment
* Privacy assessment
* Threat model
* Testing results
* Architecture review
* Control assessment
* Incident history
* Vendor assessment
* Business impact analysis

Evidence allows decision makers to evaluate the actual exposure rather than relying on assumptions.

---

### 75. Risk Acceptance Decision Criteria

Decision makers should evaluate whether:

* The risk is accurately characterized.
* The risk rating is reasonable.
* Controls are implemented.
* Controls are effective.
* Alternatives were considered.
* Business benefits justify the remaining exposure.
* The risk is within delegated authority.
* Monitoring is sufficient.

Risk acceptance should be evidence-based.

---

### 76. Risk Acceptance Decision Outcomes

A risk acceptance request may result in several outcomes.

**Accept**

→ Residual risk is formally accepted.

**Reject**

→ The proposed exposure is not acceptable.

**Mitigate Further**

→ Additional controls are required.

**Transfer**

→ Appropriate contractual or insurance mechanisms are considered.

**Avoid**

→ The activity is discontinued or prohibited.

**Escalate**

→ A higher authority must make the decision.

These outcomes provide alternatives to automatic acceptance.

---

### 77. Conditional Risk Acceptance

A decision maker may accept risk subject to defined conditions.

For example:

> Deployment is approved provided that additional monitoring is implemented within 30 days.

Conditional acceptance should identify:

* Condition
* Responsible owner
* Deadline
* Verification method
* Consequence of non-compliance

Failure to satisfy the condition should trigger reassessment.

---

### 78. Compensating Controls

Where a standard control cannot be implemented, compensating controls may reduce the resulting risk.

Examples include:

* Increased monitoring
* Restricted access
* Reduced functionality
* Manual approval
* Network isolation
* Additional testing
* Shorter review intervals

Compensating controls should provide a reasonable alternative level of risk reduction.

---

### 79. Evaluating Compensating Controls

Compensating controls should be evaluated for:

* Effectiveness
* Reliability
* Coverage
* Sustainability
* Residual risk

Simply labeling an alternative measure as a compensating control does not demonstrate that it provides adequate protection.

---

### 80. Temporary Control Deficiency

Temporary control deficiencies may require temporary risk acceptance.

For example:

* A required monitoring capability is being implemented.
* A security integration is delayed.
* A replacement platform is under development.

The risk acceptance should identify the remediation plan and expected completion date.

---

### 81. Risk Acceptance Expiration

Risk acceptance should include an expiration or review date when appropriate.

Expiration ensures that:

* Old assumptions are revisited.
* Risk does not remain accepted indefinitely.
* Remediation is tracked.
* Changes are evaluated.

Expired risk acceptance should not automatically be treated as valid.

---

### 82. Automatic Renewal of Risk Acceptance

Automatic renewal should generally be avoided for material AI risks.

Renewal should require confirmation that:

* The system has not materially changed.
* Risk remains within tolerance.
* Controls remain effective.
* Business justification remains valid.

Material risks should require active reassessment.

---

### 83. Risk Acceptance Register Governance

The AI risk acceptance register should be maintained as an authoritative governance record.

It should support:

* Ownership
* Status tracking
* Expiration monitoring
* Escalation
* Reporting
* Audit

Access to the register should be appropriately controlled.

---

### 84. Risk Acceptance Status

Accepted risks may have statuses such as:

* Draft
* Under Review
* Approved
* Conditionally Approved
* Expired
* Rejected
* Closed
* Escalated

Clear status definitions improve governance visibility.

---

### 85. Risk Acceptance Closure

A risk acceptance may be closed when:

* The risk is eliminated
* Controls are implemented
* The system is retired
* The risk is transferred
* The business activity stops

Closure should be documented.

---

### 86. Risk Acceptance Escalation

Escalation should occur when risk exceeds the authority of the current decision maker.

Triggers may include:

* Risk score above threshold
* Risk appetite breach
* Regulatory concern
* Safety concern
* Significant customer impact
* High financial exposure
* Material control deficiency

Escalation should be timely and documented.

---

### 87. Escalation Hierarchy

A governance hierarchy may resemble:

**AI System Owner**

→ **Business Management**

→ **Risk / Security / Privacy Leadership**

→ **AI Governance Committee**

→ **Executive Management**

→ **Board or Board Committee**

Not every risk needs to reach the highest level. Escalation should be proportional to risk.

---

### 88. AI Risk Veto Authority

Certain governance functions may have formal authority to prevent deployment where mandatory requirements are not satisfied.

Potential veto areas may include:

* Security
* Privacy
* Legal
* Safety
* Compliance

Whether a function has veto authority should be explicitly defined rather than assumed.

---

### 89. Veto Versus Challenge

Challenge and veto should not be confused.

**Challenge**

→ Questions or disputes a proposed decision.

**Veto**

→ Has formal authority to prevent the decision.

An organization should clearly define which governance functions have each type of authority.

---

### 90. Executive Override

Executives may sometimes authorize actions despite a specialist recommendation.

Where executive override exists, it should require:

* Documented rationale
* Identification of the affected risk
* Appropriate authority
* Formal risk acceptance
* Notification of relevant stakeholders

Override should not erase the underlying risk record.

---

### 91. Board-Level Risk Acceptance

Some AI risks may be significant enough to require board-level awareness or approval.

Potential triggers include:

* Enterprise-critical AI
* Significant regulatory exposure
* Major financial exposure
* Material safety risk
* Significant customer impact
* Strategic dependence on AI

The exact threshold should be established by the organization's governance framework.

---

### 92. AI Risk Acceptance and Enterprise Risk Management

AI risk acceptance should integrate with Enterprise Risk Management.

AI risks should be capable of being represented within the organization's:

* Enterprise risk register
* Risk taxonomy
* Risk appetite framework
* Risk reporting
* Risk committees

This prevents AI risk from becoming an isolated governance activity.

---

### 93. AI Risk Taxonomy

A standardized AI risk taxonomy can improve decision consistency.

Categories may include:

* Cybersecurity risk
* Privacy risk
* Operational risk
* Model risk
* Data risk
* Third-party risk
* Regulatory risk
* Legal risk
* Safety risk
* Reputational risk
* Strategic risk

The taxonomy should align with the organization's enterprise risk structure.

---

### 94. AI Risk Scoring

Risk scoring may use:

* Likelihood
* Impact
* Exposure
* Control effectiveness

A simplified model may be:

**Risk = Likelihood × Impact**

More sophisticated models may incorporate additional factors.

The scoring methodology should be documented and consistently applied.

---

### 95. Risk Rating Calibration

Risk ratings should be calibrated to improve consistency.

Calibration may involve:

* Defined scoring criteria
* Historical examples
* Cross-functional review
* Risk workshops
* Periodic validation

Without calibration, different teams may assign very different ratings to similar AI risks.

---

### 96. AI Risk Acceptance Consistency

Similar risks should generally receive similar treatment unless there is a documented reason for different decisions.

Consistency can be supported through:

* Standard methodologies
* Decision matrices
* Approval thresholds
* Governance templates
* Central registers
* Periodic review

Consistency improves fairness and governance credibility.

---

### 97. Risk Acceptance Portfolio View

Organizations should evaluate accepted AI risks collectively.

Portfolio analysis may identify:

* Total accepted risk
* High-risk systems
* Concentrated risks
* Repeated exceptions
* Common control weaknesses
* Provider dependencies

Portfolio reporting gives management a broader view than individual risk records.

---

### 98. Risk Aggregation

Risk aggregation should consider whether multiple AI risks contribute to the same enterprise exposure.

For example:

Several AI systems may independently create moderate risks associated with:

* Sensitive data
* Third-party providers
* Model dependency

Together, these may represent a significant enterprise-level risk.

---

### 99. AI Risk Interdependencies

AI risks can interact with other enterprise risks.

For example:

**AI Model Risk**

→ Can contribute to

**Operational Risk**

→ Which can contribute to

**Financial Risk**

→ Which can contribute to

**Reputational Risk**.

Governance should consider these relationships when evaluating material AI exposure.

---

### 100. Risk Acceptance and Business Benefits

Risk acceptance decisions should consider both risk and business value.

Decision makers may evaluate:

* Strategic importance
* Revenue impact
* Efficiency
* Customer value
* Competitive advantage
* Operational necessity

Business benefit should inform the decision but should not override mandatory legal, safety, or security requirements.

---

### 101. Risk Acceptance and Risk-Adjusted Value

For significant AI investments, organizations may consider risk-adjusted business value.

This involves comparing:

**Expected Business Benefit**

against

**Cost + Residual Risk + Governance Requirements**.

This supports more balanced executive decision-making.

---

### 102. AI Risk Acceptance and Security Architecture

Risk acceptance should not be used as a substitute for appropriate security architecture.

If a system can reasonably be secured through:

* Segmentation
* Access control
* Encryption
* Monitoring
* Secure design

those controls should normally be considered before accepting significant residual risk.

---

### 103. AI Risk Acceptance and Privacy by Design

Risk acceptance should not be used to bypass privacy requirements.

Where privacy risks can be reduced through:

* Data minimization
* Purpose limitation
* Access controls
* Anonymization or pseudonymization
* Retention controls

those measures should be considered before accepting residual privacy risk.

---

### 104. AI Risk Acceptance and Secure-by-Design

AI systems should be designed to reduce foreseeable risks from the beginning.

Risk acceptance should therefore not become a routine mechanism for approving poorly designed systems.

The preferred sequence is:

**Secure Design**

→ **Risk Reduction**

→ **Control Implementation**

→ **Residual Risk Assessment**

→ **Risk Acceptance Where Appropriate**

---

### 105. AI Risk Acceptance and Human Oversight

Human oversight may serve as a compensating control for certain AI risks.

Examples include:

* Human approval before consequential action
* Human review of AI outputs
* Manual verification
* Escalation to specialists

However, human oversight must be sufficiently meaningful and capable of detecting and correcting problematic AI behavior.

---

### 106. Risk Acceptance and AI Monitoring

Accepted risks should have appropriate monitoring.

Monitoring may identify:

* Risk increases
* Control failures
* New attack patterns
* Unexpected model behavior
* Changes in usage
* New regulatory requirements

Monitoring enables organizations to reconsider risk acceptance when conditions change.

---

### 107. Risk Acceptance Triggers for Reassessment

Previously accepted AI risks should be reassessed when material changes occur.

Triggers may include:

* Model change
* Dataset change
* New integration
* New threat
* Security incident
* Privacy incident
* Regulatory change
* Increased autonomy
* Significant change in business use

Reassessment should be incorporated into change-management processes.

---

### 108. AI Risk Acceptance Metrics

Management may monitor metrics such as:

* Number of accepted AI risks
* High-risk acceptances
* Expired acceptances
* Overdue remediation
* Exceptions by business unit
* Repeated exceptions
* Risk acceptance aging
* Accepted risk by category

Metrics should support decisions rather than simply measure administrative activity.

---

### 109. Risk Acceptance Reporting

Material accepted risks should be reported to appropriate governance bodies.

Reporting may include:

* Risk level
* Trend
* Business owner
* Acceptance authority
* Expiration
* Mitigation status
* Emerging concerns

High-risk items should receive greater management attention.

---

### 110. Independent Assurance of Risk Acceptance

Independent assurance should periodically evaluate whether risk acceptance is functioning as intended.

Assurance may examine:

* Authority compliance
* Risk-rating accuracy
* Evidence quality
* Exception management
* Control effectiveness
* Expired acceptances
* Escalation

This helps ensure that risk acceptance remains a genuine governance process rather than an administrative approval mechanism.

# 01.16 — AI Decision Rights and Risk Acceptance

## Part 4 — Decision Governance, Risk Acceptance Assurance and Continuous Improvement

### 111. AI Decision Governance Framework

A mature AI Security Governance program should establish a formal decision governance framework.

The framework should define:

* Decision categories
* Decision authorities
* Delegated authorities
* Approval thresholds
* Risk acceptance thresholds
* Escalation requirements
* Required evidence
* Documentation requirements
* Review mechanisms

This creates a consistent structure for AI-related decisions across the organization.

---

### 112. AI Decision Categories

AI decisions should be categorized according to their nature and impact.

Common categories include:

* Strategic decisions
* Business decisions
* Risk decisions
* Security decisions
* Privacy decisions
* Technology decisions
* Data decisions
* Compliance decisions
* Operational decisions
* Emergency decisions

Each category may have different decision authorities.

---

### 113. Strategic AI Decisions

Strategic decisions may include:

* Enterprise AI strategy
* Major AI investments
* AI transformation programs
* Strategic AI partnerships
* Significant AI operating-model changes
* Enterprise AI risk appetite

These decisions normally require senior management involvement and, where appropriate, board oversight.

---

### 114. Business AI Decisions

Business decisions may include:

* Approving AI use cases
* Funding AI initiatives
* Selecting business applications
* Defining business requirements
* Determining acceptable business outcomes

Business ownership should remain clearly identified even when technical and governance functions participate.

---

### 115. Security Decision Rights

Security decision rights may cover:

* Security architecture
* Security controls
* Security assessments
* Security exceptions
* Vulnerability remediation
* Security monitoring
* Security incidents

The CISO or delegated security authority may establish security requirements within the organization's governance framework.

---

### 116. Privacy Decision Rights

Privacy decision rights may cover:

* Privacy assessments
* Personal-data processing
* Privacy controls
* Data protection requirements
* High-risk processing
* Privacy exceptions

The relevant privacy authority should have sufficient independence to challenge business decisions involving significant privacy risk.

---

### 117. Technology Decision Rights

Technology decision rights may cover:

* AI platforms
* Infrastructure
* Architecture
* Integration
* Deployment models
* Technical standards
* Technology lifecycle

Technology authority should operate within security, risk, privacy, and business constraints.

---

### 118. Data Decision Rights

Data governance should establish authority over:

* Data ownership
* Data classification
* Data access
* Data quality
* Data usage
* Data retention
* Data sharing

AI governance should not bypass established enterprise data ownership.

---

### 119. AI Model Decision Rights

Model-related decisions may include:

* Model selection
* Model approval
* Model validation
* Model deployment
* Model modification
* Model retirement

The level of authority should depend on model risk and system impact.

---

### 120. AI Agent Decision Rights

Autonomous or semi-autonomous AI agents require specific decision rights concerning:

* Tool access
* System access
* Transaction authority
* Data access
* Autonomous actions
* Human approval
* Emergency intervention

The organization should explicitly define which actions an AI agent may perform without human approval.

---

### 121. AI Agent Authority Boundaries

An AI agent's authority should be constrained by defined boundaries.

Boundaries may include:

* Permitted systems
* Permitted data
* Permitted actions
* Transaction limits
* Time limits
* User scope
* Geographic scope

These boundaries should be technically enforceable where possible.

---

### 122. Human Decision Authority Over AI

Organizations should distinguish between:

* AI-generated recommendations
* AI-supported decisions
* AI-executed decisions

The more authority delegated to an AI system, the more important explicit governance controls become.

---

### 123. Human Override Authority

Where AI systems can make or execute consequential decisions, authorized humans should have defined override capabilities where appropriate.

Override mechanisms should specify:

* Who can override
* When override is permitted
* How override is executed
* How it is documented
* What happens afterward

Override authority should be tested rather than merely documented.

---

### 124. AI Decision Escalation Framework

AI governance should establish an escalation framework.

Escalation may move from:

**Operational Level**

→ **Business Management**

→ **Specialist Governance Function**

→ **AI Governance Committee**

→ **Executive Management**

→ **Board-Level Governance**

The escalation path should depend on the nature and severity of the issue.

---

### 125. Decision Escalation Triggers

Triggers may include:

* Risk above delegated authority
* Risk appetite breach
* Material security weakness
* Significant privacy concern
* Regulatory uncertainty
* Safety concern
* Major business impact
* Unresolved stakeholder disagreement

Escalation should occur before an unacceptable risk is knowingly introduced.

---

### 126. AI Governance Decision Deadlock

Cross-functional governance can produce disagreements.

For example:

* Business wants rapid deployment.
* Security requires additional controls.
* Privacy requires additional safeguards.
* Risk requires escalation.

The governance framework should identify who has authority to resolve such conflicts.

---

### 127. Conflict Resolution

AI governance conflicts should be resolved through defined mechanisms.

Possible approaches include:

* Evidence-based review
* Risk analysis
* Specialist consultation
* Governance committee decision
* Executive escalation

The objective is to ensure that significant decisions are made deliberately rather than through informal influence.

---

### 128. Decision Precedence

Organizations should define how competing requirements are handled.

For example, an internal business preference should not override:

* Applicable law
* Mandatory regulatory requirements
* Safety obligations
* Mandatory security controls
* Contractual obligations

Decision precedence should be established in governance documentation.

---

### 129. AI Governance Decision Records

Material decisions should produce a decision record.

The record may contain:

* Decision identifier
* AI system
* Decision type
* Decision date
* Decision authority
* Participants
* Evidence
* Risk assessment
* Conditions
* Decision outcome
* Review date

Decision records create institutional memory.

---

### 130. AI Decision Audit Trail

The organization should maintain an appropriate audit trail for significant AI decisions.

The trail may connect:

**Business Request**

→ **Risk Assessment**

→ **Security Assessment**

→ **Privacy Assessment**

→ **Governance Review**

→ **Risk Acceptance**

→ **Approval**

→ **Implementation**

This supports accountability and assurance.

---

### 131. Decision Evidence Requirements

Evidence requirements should be proportional to risk.

A low-risk AI tool may require:

* Basic use-case information
* Owner
* Risk classification
* Standard approval

A high-risk AI system may require:

* Detailed risk assessment
* Security testing
* Privacy assessment
* Model documentation
* Business impact analysis
* Governance committee approval

Risk-based evidence prevents both under-governance and unnecessary bureaucracy.

---

### 132. Decision Timeliness

Governance should balance control with business agility.

Excessive approval requirements can encourage:

* Shadow AI
* Informal workarounds
* Unauthorized deployments

Therefore, decision processes should be:

* Risk-based
* Predictable
* Transparent
* Proportionate
* Timely

Strong governance should enable safe AI adoption rather than unnecessarily obstruct it.

---

### 133. Fast-Track AI Decisions

Low-risk AI use cases may qualify for streamlined approval.

A fast-track process may use:

* Pre-approved technologies
* Standard controls
* Standard data classifications
* Automated risk assessments
* Predefined approval thresholds

Fast-track governance should still maintain appropriate accountability.

---

### 134. Pre-Approved AI Services

Organizations may maintain an approved list of AI services.

Approval may consider:

* Security
* Privacy
* Contractual terms
* Data handling
* Business suitability
* Regulatory requirements

Using pre-approved services can reduce repetitive assessments while maintaining governance.

---

### 135. AI Governance Decision Automation

Some governance decisions may be partially automated.

Automation may support:

* AI inventory
* Risk classification
* Policy checks
* Data classification
* Access approvals
* Control validation
* Exception alerts

Automated governance should itself be controlled and monitored.

---

### 136. AI Governance Decision Quality

Decision quality should be evaluated based on whether decisions are:

* Consistent
* Evidence-based
* Timely
* Proportionate
* Authorized
* Traceable

The objective is not simply to maximize the number of approvals.

---

### 137. AI Risk Acceptance Assurance

Risk acceptance processes should be periodically assessed to determine whether they operate effectively.

Assurance should examine:

* Appropriate authority
* Correct risk classification
* Adequate evidence
* Proper documentation
* Effective controls
* Appropriate escalation
* Timely reassessment

---

### 138. Testing Risk Acceptance Controls

Organizations may test whether:

* Risk acceptances are properly approved.
* Approval thresholds are respected.
* Expired acceptances are identified.
* Conditions are monitored.
* Remediation is completed.
* High-risk decisions receive appropriate escalation.

Testing should provide evidence of governance effectiveness.

---

### 139. Internal Audit Review of Decision Rights

Internal Audit may evaluate whether AI decision rights are:

* Clearly defined
* Consistently applied
* Properly documented
* Aligned with governance requirements
* Appropriately segregated

Audit findings may identify weaknesses in authority or accountability.

---

### 140. Risk Function Assurance

Risk management may independently evaluate:

* Risk ratings
* Risk appetite alignment
* Risk acceptance
* Aggregated exposure
* Escalation

The risk function should provide challenge without assuming operational ownership of the AI systems.

---

### 141. Security Assurance

Cybersecurity assurance may evaluate whether AI systems meet required security expectations.

Activities may include:

* Control assessments
* Security testing
* Architecture reviews
* Vulnerability assessments
* Monitoring validation

Security assurance supports informed risk decisions.

---

### 142. Privacy Assurance

Privacy assurance may evaluate whether AI systems appropriately manage personal-data risks.

Activities may include:

* Privacy-control testing
* Processing reviews
* Data-flow validation
* Documentation review
* Privacy-risk reassessment

Privacy assurance should align with applicable privacy governance.

---

### 143. AI Decision Metrics

Organizations may monitor decision-governance metrics such as:

* Number of AI decisions
* Approval cycle time
* High-risk approvals
* Risk acceptance volume
* Exceptions
* Escalations
* Rejected use cases
* Expired risk acceptances
* Overdue decisions

Metrics should help management identify governance bottlenecks and emerging risk.

### 144. Risk Acceptance Metrics

Important risk acceptance metrics may include:

* Total accepted AI risks
* Accepted high-risk exposures
* Average acceptance age
* Expired acceptances
* Overdue reassessments
* Risk acceptance by business unit
* Risk acceptance by risk category
* Repeated exceptions

Trend analysis is often more valuable than a single reporting period.

### 145. AI Risk Acceptance Dashboard

A governance dashboard may provide management with a consolidated view of:

* Current accepted risk
* Risk trends
* High-risk systems
* Exceptions
* Expired approvals
* Control deficiencies
* Emerging risks

Dashboards should focus attention on material issues.

---

### 146. Decision Rights Review

Decision rights should be periodically reviewed.

Review triggers may include:

* Organizational restructuring
* New AI capabilities
* New regulations
* Changes in risk appetite
* New governance committees
* Significant incidents

Outdated decision rights can create accountability gaps.

---

### 147. Decision Rights During Organizational Change

When responsibilities change, decision rights should be updated promptly.

For example, if responsibility for AI governance moves from one executive function to another, the organization should update:

* Policies
* RACI matrices
* Approval workflows
* Risk registers
* Delegation records

This prevents obsolete authorities from remaining in effect.

---

### 148. AI Governance Delegation Register

Organizations may maintain a delegation register identifying:

* Delegated authority
* Delegator
* Delegate
* Scope
* Risk threshold
* Effective date
* Expiration
* Conditions

The register provides evidence that decision authority has been formally established.

---

### 149. Periodic Revalidation of Authority

Delegated decision rights should be periodically revalidated.

Revalidation should confirm that:

* The individual remains in the role.
* The delegation remains appropriate.
* Authority limits remain correct.
* Organizational structure has not changed.
* Risk thresholds remain appropriate.

---

### 150. AI Governance Competence

Decision authority should be assigned to people with appropriate knowledge and competence.

Competence may include understanding of:

* AI technology
* Cybersecurity
* Risk management
* Privacy
* Regulatory obligations
* Business impact
* Governance requirements

Authority without adequate competence can create significant governance risk.

---

### 151. Decision Maker Training

People who approve AI systems or accept AI risks should receive appropriate training.

Training may cover:

* AI risk
* Security
* Privacy
* Risk appetite
* Decision rights
* Escalation
* Risk acceptance

Training should be proportionate to the authority assigned.

---

### 152. AI Governance Culture and Decision Behavior

Formal decision rights are effective only when supported by governance culture.

A mature culture encourages decision makers to:

* Ask questions
* Challenge assumptions
* Escalate concerns
* Document decisions
* Consider evidence
* Avoid unnecessary risk acceptance

Culture therefore complements formal governance structures.

---

### 153. Avoiding Risk Acceptance as a Shortcut

Risk acceptance should not become a mechanism for bypassing governance.

Warning signs include:

* Frequent risk acceptances
* Repeated exceptions
* Minimal evidence
* Short approval cycles for high-risk systems
* The same risk repeatedly being accepted
* Controls not being implemented

These patterns should trigger management review.

---

### 154. Risk Acceptance as a Governance Signal

High levels of accepted risk may indicate broader problems.

They may suggest:

* Weak architecture
* Inadequate investment
* Poor policy design
* Unrealistic standards
* Insufficient resources
* Weak risk culture

Risk acceptance data can therefore provide strategic insight.

---

### 155. Lessons Learned From AI Decisions

Organizations should capture lessons from significant decisions.

Lessons may arise from:

* Incidents
* Rejected AI projects
* Successful deployments
* Failed controls
* Risk acceptances
* Regulatory findings

Lessons should feed into policy, standards, procedures, and training.

---

### 156. Continuous Improvement of Decision Rights

Decision-rights structures should evolve as AI capabilities and organizational needs change.

Improvement may include:

* Adjusting thresholds
* Clarifying responsibilities
* Simplifying workflows
* Adding new decision categories
* Strengthening escalation
* Automating low-risk approvals

The goal is to maintain effective governance while supporting business agility.

---

### 157. Continuous Improvement of Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance processes should also evolve.

Organizations should periodically evaluate:

* Whether thresholds remain appropriate
* Whether accepted risks remain within appetite
* Whether controls are improving
* Whether recurring exceptions exist
* Whether new mitigation technologies are available

Continuous improvement helps prevent outdated risk decisions.

---

### 158. AI Governance Maturity and Decision Rights

Decision-rights maturity can develop from:

**Ad Hoc**

→ Individuals make decisions inconsistently.

**Repeatable**

→ Basic approval processes exist.

**Defined**

→ Decision rights and thresholds are documented.

**Managed**

→ Decisions are measured, monitored, and independently assessed.

**Optimized**

→ Decision processes continuously improve through risk intelligence, automation, and governance analytics.

Maturity should reflect actual decision capability rather than documentation alone.

---

### 159. Integrated AI Decision and Risk Acceptance Model

A mature model connects:

**AI Governance Strategy**

→ Establishes decision principles.

**Decision Rights**

→ Establish who can decide.

**Risk Assessment**

→ Establishes the level of exposure.

**Risk Appetite and Tolerance**

→ Establish acceptable boundaries.

**Controls**

→ Reduce risk.

**Residual Risk**

→ Establishes remaining exposure.

**Risk Acceptance**

→ Formalizes the decision to tolerate remaining risk.

**Escalation**

→ Moves decisions beyond delegated authority.

**Monitoring and Assurance**

→ Confirms that decisions remain appropriate.

---

### 160. AI Decision Rights and Risk Acceptance as a Governance System

AI decision rights and risk acceptance should ultimately operate as one integrated governance mechanism.

The organization must know:

* **Who can decide**
* **What they can decide**
* **What evidence they need**
* **What level of risk they can accept**
* **When they must escalate**
* **How the decision is documented**
* **How the decision is monitored**
* **When the decision must be reconsidered**

A mature AI Security Governance program does not simply assign approval responsibilities. It creates a structured system in which **authority, accountability, risk appetite, control effectiveness, residual risk, escalation, and assurance work together to ensure that AI decisions are made deliberately, consistently, and within the organization's established boundaries**.
