# 01.09 — AI Governance Across the AI Lifecycle

## Part 1 — Foundations of Lifecycle-Based AI Governance

### 1. Introduction

AI governance must operate across the entire AI system lifecycle.

An organization cannot effectively govern AI by approving a system once and then assuming that the original approval remains valid indefinitely. AI systems can change continuously through changes to models, data, prompts, users, integrations, tools, business purposes, regulations, and operating environments.

Therefore:

> **AI governance is a continuous lifecycle discipline that establishes accountability, decision rights, controls, oversight, and assurance from AI conception through retirement.**

Lifecycle-based governance connects strategic governance with the practical activities performed by business, technology, security, risk, privacy, legal, compliance, and operational teams.

---

### 2. What Is AI Governance Across the AI Lifecycle?

**AI governance across the AI lifecycle** is the structured application of governance requirements to every significant stage of an AI system's existence.

These stages may include:

* Ideation
* Business assessment
* Risk classification
* Design
* Data preparation
* Development
* Testing
* Validation
* Approval
* Deployment
* Operation
* Monitoring
* Change
* Reassessment
* Retirement

Governance establishes what must happen at each stage, who is accountable, what decisions must be made, what evidence must be produced, and what conditions must be satisfied before the system progresses.

---

### 3. Why Lifecycle-Based Governance Is Necessary

AI systems create risks that can change over time.

For example, an AI system may initially:

* Use low-sensitivity data
* Have limited users
* Provide recommendations only
* Have no access to enterprise systems

Later, the organization may:

* Connect it to sensitive information
* Increase its user population
* Give it access to enterprise applications
* Allow it to execute actions
* Integrate it with external services

The system may therefore have a significantly different risk profile even though its original business purpose appears unchanged.

Lifecycle governance provides mechanisms for managing these changes.

---

### 4. Governance Is Not a Single Approval Event

A common governance mistake is to treat approval as the end of governance.

For example:

**Initial Approval**

→ **Production**

→ **No Further Governance**

This creates a governance gap.

A more appropriate model is:

**Initial Approval**

→ **Operational Oversight**

→ **Continuous Monitoring**

→ **Change Assessment**

→ **Reassessment**

→ **Reapproval When Required**

→ **Retirement**

Governance continues throughout the system's operational life.

---

### 5. Governance Begins Before AI Development

AI governance should begin before technical development.

When a business unit proposes an AI use case, governance should determine:

* Whether the use case is permitted
* Whether AI is appropriate
* What risks exist
* What information will be processed
* Who will be affected
* What regulatory obligations apply
* What level of oversight is required

This prevents organizations from investing heavily in an AI solution before determining whether it should be built.

---

### 6. AI Governance Intake

Organizations may establish an **AI governance intake process** for proposed AI initiatives.

The intake process may capture:

* Business purpose
* AI capability
* Intended users
* Data sources
* Expected outputs
* Decision impact
* Degree of autonomy
* Third-party providers
* Geographic scope
* Initial risk indicators

The intake process provides the first structured governance record for the AI initiative.

---

### 7. AI Use-Case Governance

The AI use case should be clearly defined before detailed development begins.

Governance should establish:

* Intended purpose
* Approved users
* Approved data
* Approved outputs
* Permitted actions
* Prohibited uses
* Business owner
* System owner
* Risk owner

Clear boundaries prevent an AI system from gradually expanding beyond its original authorization without appropriate review.

---

### 8. AI Purpose Limitation

AI systems should be used for clearly defined and authorized purposes.

For example, an AI system approved to:

> Summarize internal documents

should not automatically be authorized to:

> Make employment decisions.

A change in purpose can introduce entirely different:

* Risks
* Data requirements
* Regulatory obligations
* Security requirements
* Accountability requirements

Purpose changes should therefore be governed.

---

### 9. AI Governance Scope

Lifecycle governance should cover the complete AI capability rather than only the model.

Governance scope may include:

* Business process
* Application
* Model
* Data
* Infrastructure
* APIs
* Tools
* Agents
* Users
* Vendors
* Human oversight

This broader scope reflects the fact that risks can originate outside the model itself.

---

### 10. AI Governance and Business Ownership

The business should remain accountable for the purpose and outcomes of the AI system.

Business ownership may include responsibility for:

* Business justification
* Expected outcomes
* Appropriate use
* Resource allocation
* Business risk
* Operational impact

AI governance should not transfer business accountability entirely to IT or the security function.

---

### 11. AI Governance and Technology Ownership

Technology teams may be responsible for:

* Architecture
* Infrastructure
* Application operation
* Model deployment
* Technical controls
* Availability
* Technical change management

Technology ownership should be clearly distinguished from business ownership.

---

### 12. AI Governance and Security Ownership

Security functions may establish requirements for:

* Threat management
* Identity
* Access control
* Data protection
* Application security
* Model security
* Monitoring
* Incident response

Security governance provides oversight and requirements but does not necessarily own the business purpose of every AI system.

---

### 13. AI Governance and Privacy Ownership

Privacy functions may oversee:

* Personal-data use
* Privacy impact
* Data minimization
* Retention
* Data subject rights
* Lawful processing
* Privacy controls

Privacy considerations should be integrated into lifecycle governance from the beginning.

---

### 14. AI Governance and Legal Ownership

Legal functions may assess:

* Regulatory requirements
* Contractual obligations
* Intellectual property
* Liability
* Jurisdiction
* Third-party terms

Legal review may be especially important for AI systems using external models, datasets, or services.

---

### 15. AI Governance and Risk Management

Enterprise Risk Management can provide a broader framework for identifying and managing AI-related risks.

AI risks may be incorporated into:

* Enterprise risk registers
* Risk appetite
* Risk reporting
* Risk acceptance
* Scenario analysis
* Management reporting

This connects AI governance with the organization's overall risk-management structure.

---

### 16. AI Governance and Compliance

Compliance functions may determine whether AI systems meet applicable:

* Laws
* Regulations
* Standards
* Policies
* Contracts
* Industry requirements

Compliance requirements should be translated into actionable lifecycle requirements.

---

### 17. AI Governance and Procurement

Governance begins even before an external AI service is acquired.

Procurement processes should consider:

* Vendor security
* Data processing
* Model ownership
* Subprocessors
* Service availability
* Data retention
* Contractual obligations
* Exit requirements

Third-party AI services should therefore enter the lifecycle through controlled procurement processes.

---

### 18. AI Governance and Third-Party AI

Organizations may use AI capabilities provided by:

* Cloud providers
* SaaS vendors
* Foundation-model providers
* AI application vendors
* Data providers
* Consulting firms

Governance should establish how third-party AI services are assessed, approved, monitored, and eventually terminated.

These may include:

* Access restrictions
* Logging

Design governance ensures that the final system can support the organization's risk requirements.

### 20. AI Governance and Architecture Review

Architecture review should determine whether the proposed design is consistent with:

* Business requirements
* Security requirements
* Privacy requirements
* Risk tolerance
* Regulatory obligations
* Operational requirements

The architecture should demonstrate how governance requirements will be implemented technically and operationally.

---

### 21. AI Governance During Development

Development activities should remain subject to governance requirements.

Governance may require:

* Approved development environments
* Controlled datasets
* Secure coding
* Version control
* Access management
* Change tracking
* Documentation
* Security testing

Development teams should understand which governance requirements apply to their AI work.

---

### 22. AI Governance and Data Management

Data governance is a fundamental component of AI governance.

Governance should establish expectations for:

* Data ownership
* Data classification
* Data quality
* Data provenance
* Data access
* Data retention
* Data protection
* Data use restrictions

Poor data governance can undermine both AI performance and organizational compliance.

---

### 23. AI Governance and Model Management

Models should be governed as organizational assets.

Model governance may address:

* Model ownership
* Model version
* Model purpose
* Model risk
* Validation
* Approval
* Deployment
* Monitoring
* Change
* Retirement

Model management should be connected to the broader AI lifecycle.

---

### 24. AI Governance During Testing

Testing provides evidence that an AI system satisfies defined requirements.

Governance should establish what testing is required based on the system's risk.

Testing may include:

* Functional testing
* Security testing
* Privacy testing
* Safety testing
* Performance testing
* Robustness testing
* AI-specific adversarial testing

Higher-risk systems should generally require more rigorous testing.

---

### 25. AI Governance and Validation

Validation provides assurance that an AI system is suitable for its intended purpose.

Governance should define:

* Who performs validation
* What must be validated
* What evidence is required
* What acceptance criteria apply
* Who approves the results

Validation should be sufficiently independent where the risk warrants independent review.

---

### 26. AI Governance Approval

Before deployment, governance should confirm that required conditions have been satisfied.

Approval may require evidence of:

* Risk assessment
* Security assessment
* Privacy assessment
* Testing
* Validation
* Ownership
* Monitoring
* Documentation
* Residual-risk treatment

Approval should be based on evidence rather than informal confidence.

---

### 27. AI Governance During Deployment

Deployment should remain governed through controlled release processes.

Governance may require:

* Approved model version
* Approved configuration
* Authorized infrastructure
* Validated security controls
* Production monitoring
* Appropriate access
* Documented deployment authority

Production deployment should not bypass established governance simply because development has been completed.

---

### 28. AI Governance During Operation

Once deployed, governance shifts toward continuous oversight.

Operational governance may address:

* Performance
* Security
* Privacy
* Compliance
* Model behavior
* User activity
* Incidents
* Changes
* Business value

The governance process should remain active throughout operation.

---

### 29. AI Governance Monitoring

Monitoring should determine whether the AI system continues to operate within approved boundaries.

Monitoring may examine:

* Model behavior
* Data use
* Access
* Tool invocation
* Configuration
* Security events
* Compliance status
* Risk indicators

Monitoring provides evidence that lifecycle governance remains effective.

---

### 30. AI Governance and Model Drift

Model or data drift can affect the validity of the original governance assessment.

For example:

**Original Model**

→ Approved for a defined environment

**Environment Changes**

→ Data distribution changes

**Result**

→ Original assumptions may no longer be valid

Governance should therefore establish thresholds that trigger reassessment when material drift occurs.


## Part 2 — Governance Controls and Decision Gates Across the AI Lifecycle

### 31. AI Governance During Production Operation

Once an AI system enters production, governance moves from **pre-deployment assurance** toward **continuous operational oversight**.

Operational governance should establish mechanisms for:

* Monitoring
* Risk management
* Security
* Privacy
* Compliance
* Performance
* Incident management
* Change management
* User management
* Periodic review

Production status does not eliminate governance obligations.

---

### 32. AI Governance Monitoring Framework

A governance monitoring framework should provide visibility into whether AI systems remain within their approved operating boundaries.

Monitoring may include:

* Risk indicators
* Security events
* Model performance
* Data usage
* Access activity
* Configuration changes
* Policy violations
* AI incidents
* Control exceptions

Monitoring requirements should be proportional to the AI system's risk.

---

### 33. Governance Thresholds

Governance should define thresholds that determine when intervention is required.

Examples include:

* Significant model-performance degradation
* Material security incident
* Unauthorized data access
* Major model change
* Increased autonomy
* New high-risk use
* Significant regulatory change

A threshold can trigger:

* Investigation
* Risk reassessment
* Additional testing
* Temporary suspension
* Management escalation
* Formal reapproval

---

### 34. AI Governance Alerts

Governance alerts can notify responsible stakeholders when defined conditions occur.

Examples include:

* High-risk AI system operating without current assessment
* Expired approval
* Unauthorized configuration change
* Significant model drift
* Unresolved critical security finding
* Unapproved tool integration

Automated alerts can reduce dependence on periodic manual reviews.

---

### 35. AI Governance Exceptions

Organizations may encounter situations where an AI system cannot immediately meet an established requirement.

An exception process should establish:

* Business justification
* Risk assessment
* Compensating controls
* Approval authority
* Expiration date
* Review requirements

Exceptions should be visible to appropriate governance bodies.

---

### 36. AI Governance Escalation

Material AI risks should have defined escalation paths.

Escalation may occur when:

* Risk exceeds tolerance
* A major incident occurs
* Controls fail
* The system behaves unexpectedly
* A regulatory issue emerges
* Management decisions are required

Escalation ensures that significant issues reach the appropriate decision-making authority.

---

### 37. AI Governance Decision Rights

Lifecycle governance requires explicit decision rights.

The organization should determine who can:

* Approve an AI use case
* Approve deployment
* Accept residual risk
* Approve privileged access
* Approve autonomous actions
* Approve exceptions
* Approve material changes
* Suspend an AI system
* Retire an AI system

Decision rights should correspond to organizational authority and risk.

---

### 38. AI Governance Decision Matrix

A decision matrix can map lifecycle decisions to responsible authorities.

For example:

| Decision            | Business | Security | Privacy | Risk | Executive |
| ------------------- | -------- | -------- | ------- | ---- | --------- |
| Use-case approval   | A        | C        | C       | C    | I         |
| Security approval   | C        | A        | I       | C    | I         |
| Privacy approval    | C        | C        | A       | C    | I         |
| Risk acceptance     | C        | C        | C       | A/R  | A/R*      |
| Production approval | A        | C        | C       | C    | I         |
| Material change     | A        | C        | C       | C    | I         |
| Retirement          | A        | C        | C       | C    | I         |

**A** = Accountable
**R** = Responsible
**C** = Consulted
**I** = Informed

The exact allocation should reflect the organization's operating model.

---

### 39. AI Governance Stage Gates

Stage gates provide formal control points throughout the lifecycle.

A typical model may include:

**Gate 1 — Use Case**

→ Is the proposed AI use permitted?

**Gate 2 — Design**

→ Is the architecture acceptable?

**Gate 3 — Development**

→ Are required controls implemented?

**Gate 4 — Validation**

→ Has the system been adequately tested?

**Gate 5 — Production**

→ Is deployment authorized?

**Gate 6 — Operation**

→ Does the system remain within approved boundaries?

**Gate 7 — Retirement**

→ Has the system been securely decommissioned?

---

### 40. Risk-Based Stage Gates

Not every AI system should pass through identical gates.

A low-risk system may use a streamlined approval process.

A high-risk system may require:

* Independent validation
* Security review
* Privacy review
* Legal review
* Executive approval
* Formal risk acceptance
* Continuous monitoring

Stage gates should therefore scale with risk.

---

### 41. AI Governance Control Objectives

Each lifecycle stage should have defined control objectives.

For example:

**Ideation**

Control objective:

> Ensure AI use cases are identified, documented, and appropriately assessed.

**Development**

Control objective:

> Ensure AI systems are developed using approved security, privacy, data, and engineering practices.

**Deployment**

Control objective:

> Ensure only approved and validated AI systems enter production.

**Operation**

Control objective:

> Ensure AI systems remain secure, compliant, reliable, and within approved boundaries.

**Retirement**

Control objective:

> Ensure AI systems are securely and formally decommissioned.

---

### 42. AI Governance Control Mapping

Control requirements can be mapped across the lifecycle.

For example:

| Lifecycle Stage | Governance Controls                            |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Ideation        | Use-case approval, initial risk classification |
| Design          | Architecture review, privacy assessment        |
| Development     | Secure development, data controls              |
| Testing         | Security testing, validation                   |
| Deployment      | Production approval, access controls           |
| Operation       | Monitoring, incident management                |
| Change          | Change assessment, reapproval                  |
| Retirement      | Data disposition, access removal               |

This mapping makes governance requirements operational.

---

### 43. AI Governance Evidence Requirements

Each control should identify what evidence demonstrates that the control operated.

Examples include:

* Approved use-case record
* Risk assessment
* Architecture document
* Security assessment
* Test results
* Validation report
* Approval record
* Monitoring report
* Change ticket
* Retirement certificate

Evidence requirements should be defined before the activity occurs.

---

### 44. AI Governance Documentation Lifecycle

Governance documentation should itself follow a lifecycle.

Documents should be:

* Created
* Reviewed
* Approved
* Versioned
* Updated
* Retained
* Archived
* Disposed of according to policy

Outdated governance documents can create incorrect assumptions about an AI system.

---

### 45. AI Governance Recordkeeping

Recordkeeping should provide sufficient historical information to understand significant AI decisions.

Records may include:

* Who approved the system
* What risks were known
* What controls were required
* What model was deployed
* What changes occurred
* What incidents occurred
* Why decisions were made

Recordkeeping supports accountability and assurance.

---

### 46. AI Governance Change Management

AI change management should distinguish between:

**Minor Changes**

and

**Material Changes**

Minor changes may have limited governance impact.

Material changes may significantly affect:

* Risk
* Security
* Privacy
* Safety
* Business impact
* Regulatory status

Material changes should trigger additional governance activities.

---

### 47. AI Change Classification

Changes may be classified as:

**Standard Change**

→ Pre-approved and low risk.

**Normal Change**

→ Requires assessment and authorization.

**Major Change**

→ Requires comprehensive review and potentially executive approval.

This approach integrates AI changes with established enterprise change-management practices.

---

### 48. AI Governance Triggers

Governance should establish events that automatically or manually trigger reassessment.

Triggers may include:

* Model replacement
* Significant retraining
* New data source
* New sensitive data
* New integration
* New tool
* New autonomous capability
* New jurisdiction
* New regulatory requirement
* Major security incident

Trigger-based governance helps prevent outdated approvals.

---

### 49. AI Governance Reassessment

When a governance trigger occurs, the organization should determine whether the existing assessment remains valid.

The reassessment may examine:

* Purpose
* Risk
* Security
* Privacy
* Safety
* Compliance
* Performance
* Controls

Not every change requires a complete reassessment, but every material change should be evaluated.

---

### 50. AI Governance Reapproval

Reapproval may be required when changes materially alter the AI system.

Examples include:

* Changing the model
* Expanding the user population
* Adding sensitive data
* Adding privileged tools
* Increasing autonomy
* Changing the business purpose

Reapproval confirms that the organization continues to authorize the system under its new conditions.

---

### 51. AI Governance and Model Updates

Model updates can occur through:

* New versions
* Fine-tuning
* Retraining
* Provider changes
* Configuration changes

The governance impact depends on the magnitude and nature of the change.

A minor technical update may require limited review, while a fundamental model replacement may require comprehensive reassessment.

---

### 52. AI Governance and Prompt Changes

In generative AI systems, prompts and system instructions can materially affect behavior.

Governance should therefore consider significant changes to:

* System prompts
* Developer instructions
* Guardrails
* Retrieval instructions
* Tool-selection logic

Material prompt changes may require testing and approval.

---

### 53. AI Governance and Tool Changes

Adding a new tool can significantly expand an AI system's capabilities.

For example, an AI assistant that originally had read-only access may later receive the ability to:

* Send emails
* Modify records
* Execute commands
* Initiate transactions

The new capability may require additional:

* Security controls
* Risk assessment
* Approval
* Monitoring
* Human oversight

---

### 54. AI Governance and Autonomy Changes

Increasing autonomy is a major lifecycle governance consideration.

An AI system may progress from:

**Informational**

→ **Recommendation**

→ **Assisted Action**

→ **Human-Approved Action**

→ **Autonomous Action**

Each increase in autonomy can increase potential impact and therefore may require stronger governance.

---

### 55. AI Governance and User Population Changes

Expanding AI access can change risk.

For example:

**100 internal users**

may present a different risk profile from:

**100,000 external customers.**

Changes in user population may require reassessment of:

* Access controls
* Privacy
* Security
* Capacity
* Abuse prevention
* Monitoring

---

### 56. AI Governance and Geographic Expansion

AI systems operating in additional jurisdictions may become subject to different:

* Laws
* Regulations
* Data requirements
* Contractual obligations
* Privacy requirements

Geographic expansion should therefore be treated as a potential governance trigger.

---

### 57. AI Governance and Regulatory Change

Regulatory requirements can change while an AI system remains operational.

Organizations should monitor relevant regulatory developments and determine whether existing AI systems are affected.

Where regulatory changes materially affect a system, governance should trigger:

* Gap assessment
* Risk reassessment
* Control changes
* Documentation updates
* Reapproval where necessary

---

### 58. AI Governance and Threat Evolution

The AI threat environment changes continuously.

New threats may affect:

* Models
* Applications
* APIs
* Data
* Agents
* Users
* Vendors

Threat intelligence should therefore feed into lifecycle governance.

---

### 59. AI Governance and Vulnerability Management

AI systems may contain vulnerabilities in:

* Applications
* Libraries
* APIs
* Infrastructure
* Models
* Integrations

Vulnerability management should identify, prioritize, remediate, and track these issues throughout the lifecycle.

---

### 60. AI Governance and Security Patching

Security patches should be managed through controlled processes.

Organizations should determine:

* Which components require patching
* How quickly patches must be applied
* How patches are tested
* Who approves emergency changes
* How rollback is performed

AI systems should not be exempt from established vulnerability-management processes.

---

### 61. AI Governance and Incident Management

AI incidents should enter the organization's formal incident-management process.

Incidents may involve:

* Data leakage
* Prompt injection
* Unauthorized access
* Model manipulation
* Unsafe outputs
* Malicious tool use
* Excessive autonomous activity

Incident management should identify both technical and governance implications.

---

### 62. AI Governance Incident Escalation

Incident severity should determine the level of governance escalation.

A material incident may require notification to:

* Security leadership
* AI governance
* Risk management
* Privacy
* Legal
* Executive management

Where required, external regulatory or contractual notification processes should also be considered.

---

### 63. AI Governance Post-Incident Review

After a significant AI incident, the organization should assess:

* Root cause
* Control failure
* Governance failure
* Risk assumptions
* Detection effectiveness
* Response effectiveness
* Required improvements

The review should feed lessons back into the lifecycle.

---

### 64. AI Governance Continuous Improvement

Lifecycle governance should evolve based on:

* Incidents
* Audits
* Testing
* Regulatory changes
* Threat intelligence
* Technology developments
* User feedback
* Operational experience

Continuous improvement ensures that governance does not become static while AI capabilities evolve.

---

### 65. AI Governance Lessons Learned

Lessons from one AI system should inform other systems where applicable.

For example, if an organization discovers that a particular access-control weakness affected one AI agent, the governance function should determine whether similar weaknesses exist across the broader AI environment.

This converts individual incidents into enterprise-wide learning.

---

### 66. AI Governance Portfolio Perspective

Organizations should not govern AI systems exclusively as individual projects.

They should also maintain an enterprise-level view of the AI portfolio.

Portfolio governance can identify:

* Concentration risk
* Common vulnerabilities
* Duplicate systems
* Common vendors
* Shared data sources
* Shared infrastructure
* Enterprise-wide control gaps

Portfolio-level visibility strengthens lifecycle governance.

---

### 67. AI Governance Inventory

An enterprise AI inventory should provide a consolidated view of AI systems.

Relevant attributes may include:

* AI system
* Business owner
* Technical owner
* Model
* Provider
* Risk classification
* Data classification
* Business criticality
* Lifecycle stage
* Approval status
* Review date

The inventory becomes a foundational governance capability.

---

### 68. AI Lifecycle Status

Every material AI system should have a clearly defined lifecycle status.

Possible statuses include:

* Proposed
* Under assessment
* Approved
* In development
* Under testing
* Validated
* Production
* Suspended
* Under review
* Retiring
* Retired

Lifecycle status helps governance teams understand where controls and decisions are required.

---

### 69. AI Governance Review Frequency

Review frequency should be based on risk.

For example:

**Low Risk**

→ Periodic review

**Moderate Risk**

→ More frequent review

**High Risk**

→ Continuous monitoring and formal periodic reassessment

The exact frequency should be determined by organizational policy and applicable requirements.

---

### 70. AI Governance Continuous Oversight

Continuous oversight does not necessarily mean that humans must manually review every AI action.

Instead, it can combine:

* Automated monitoring
* Risk thresholds
* Exception reporting
* Periodic human review
* Automated controls
* Management dashboards
* Escalation mechanisms

This enables governance to scale across large AI environments.


## Part 3 — AI Governance Assurance, Accountability and Lifecycle Oversight

### 71. AI Governance Assurance

AI governance assurance provides confidence that governance requirements are properly designed, implemented, and operating effectively throughout the AI lifecycle.

Assurance can examine:

* Governance processes
* Risk management
* Security controls
* Privacy controls
* Model controls
* Lifecycle approvals
* Monitoring
* Change management
* Documentation
* Accountability

Assurance should evaluate both **whether governance exists** and **whether it works in practice**.

---

### 72. AI Governance Assurance Layers

AI governance assurance can operate at multiple levels:

**Management Oversight**

→ Determines whether AI governance objectives are being achieved.

**Second-Line Oversight**

→ Provides risk, compliance, security, and privacy challenge.

**Internal Audit**

→ Provides independent assurance.

**External Assurance**

→ May provide independent assessments, certifications, or regulatory examination where applicable.

These layers should operate with clearly defined responsibilities.

---

### 73. First-Line AI Governance Responsibilities

The first line consists primarily of the teams responsible for owning and operating AI capabilities.

They should generally be responsible for:

* Identifying risks
* Implementing controls
* Operating controls
* Managing incidents
* Maintaining documentation
* Monitoring AI systems
* Escalating issues

The first line should not assume that governance responsibility belongs exclusively to a central AI governance function.

---

### 74. Second-Line AI Governance Responsibilities

Second-line functions may include:

* Risk management
* Compliance
* Information security governance
* Privacy
* Legal
* AI governance

They may establish:

* Frameworks
* Policies
* Standards
* Guidance
* Oversight
* Challenge mechanisms

The second line provides governance oversight without taking over the operational responsibilities of the first line.

---

### 75. Third-Line AI Governance Responsibilities

Internal Audit may independently evaluate the effectiveness of AI governance.

Audit activities may examine:

* AI inventory
* Risk classification
* Control design
* Control operation
* Approval processes
* Model governance
* Vendor governance
* Change management
* Incident management

Internal Audit should maintain appropriate independence from management and operational activities.

---

### 76. AI Governance Independent Validation

Higher-risk AI systems may require independent validation.

Independent validation can help reduce conflicts of interest where the same team:

* Develops the system
* Tests the system
* Approves the system

The level of independence should be proportional to risk.

---

### 77. AI Governance Segregation of Duties

Segregation of duties can prevent one individual or team from controlling all critical lifecycle decisions.

For example:

**Developer**

should not necessarily be the sole authority to:

* Validate the system
* Approve production
* Accept material residual risk

Appropriate separation improves governance integrity.

---

### 78. AI Governance Accountability

Accountability means that specific individuals or organizational functions are answerable for AI decisions and outcomes.

Accountability should exist for:

* Business purpose
* Data
* Model
* Security
* Privacy
* Risk
* Compliance
* Operations
* Governance

Accountability should remain clear even when AI systems operate autonomously.

---

### 79. AI System Owner

The **AI system owner** is responsible for the overall operation and governance of a specific AI capability.

Responsibilities may include:

* Maintaining the system inventory record
* Ensuring required assessments are completed
* Coordinating lifecycle activities
* Managing changes
* Monitoring risks
* Ensuring appropriate controls
* Escalating significant issues

The exact responsibilities should be defined by organizational policy.

---

### 80. AI Business Owner

The **business owner** is accountable for why the organization uses the AI system and what business outcome it is expected to achieve.

The business owner may determine:

* Business purpose
* Business requirements
* Expected benefits
* Acceptable use
* Business impact
* Operational priority

Business ownership should remain active throughout the lifecycle.

---

### 81. AI Model Owner

Where model ownership is separately defined, the model owner may be responsible for:

* Model documentation
