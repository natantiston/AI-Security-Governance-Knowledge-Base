# 01.04 — AI Risk, Security Risk, Safety Risk, Privacy Risk and Ethical Risk

## Part 1 — Introduction to AI Risk

### 1. Introduction

Artificial Intelligence introduces a broad range of risks that cannot be addressed through cybersecurity alone.

An AI system can create or amplify:

* Security risk
* Safety risk
* Privacy risk
* Ethical risk
* Legal and regulatory risk
* Operational risk
* Model risk
* Data risk
* Third-party risk
* Reputational risk
* Strategic risk

These risks can overlap, interact, and reinforce one another.

For example, a compromised AI system may produce harmful outputs. Those outputs may expose personal information, create regulatory violations, cause financial loss, and damage the organization's reputation.

AI Security Governance therefore requires a **multidimensional understanding of risk**.

---

## 2. What Is AI Risk?

**AI risk** is the potential for an AI system, its development, deployment, operation, or use to cause undesirable consequences to an organization, individual, society, or other affected party.

AI risk can arise from:

* The AI model
* Training data
* Input data
* System architecture
* Applications
* Users
* Administrators
* Third parties
* AI agents
* External dependencies
* Deployment environments
* Organizational decisions
* Governance failures

AI risk is therefore broader than the technical security of the model.

---

## 3. AI Risk as an Enterprise Risk Category

AI risk should not be treated as an isolated technical issue.

It can affect traditional enterprise risk categories such as:

* Strategic risk
* Operational risk
* Financial risk
* Compliance risk
* Legal risk
* Technology risk
* Cybersecurity risk
* Privacy risk
* Reputational risk
* Business continuity risk

An enterprise AI Governance program should therefore connect AI risk management with the organization's existing **Enterprise Risk Management (ERM)** framework.

---

## 4. AI Risk Versus AI Security Risk

These concepts should be distinguished.

### AI Risk

Covers the broader range of risks arising from AI.

### AI Security Risk

Focuses specifically on risks involving:

* Confidentiality
* Integrity
* Availability
* Authentication
* Authorization
* Abuse
* Attack
* Manipulation
* Compromise

AI Security Governance is therefore an important component of AI Risk Governance, but it does not cover every AI-related risk.

---

## 5. AI Security Risk

AI Security Risk is the possibility that threats, vulnerabilities, weaknesses, or malicious activities affecting an AI system may compromise the security of the system, its data, its users, or connected environments.

Examples include:

* Prompt injection
* Data poisoning
* Model manipulation
* Model theft
* Adversarial attacks
* Unauthorized access
* Data leakage
* Supply-chain compromise
* AI-generated malicious content
* Insecure APIs
* Excessive agent permissions

Security risk is a central concern of AI Security Governance.

---

## 6. Confidentiality Risk

AI systems may process highly sensitive information.

Examples include:

* Personal information
* Customer records
* Financial information
* Intellectual property
* Source code
* Business strategies
* Security information
* Credentials

Confidentiality risk occurs when information is disclosed to unauthorized parties.

AI can introduce additional confidentiality concerns through:

* Prompts
* Model outputs
* Training data
* Retrieval systems
* Logs
* APIs
* Plugins
* External AI providers

---

## 7. Integrity Risk

AI systems can produce incorrect, manipulated, or unauthorized results.

Integrity risk may arise from:

* Data poisoning
* Model manipulation
* Unauthorized model changes
* Prompt manipulation
* Configuration changes
* Compromised dependencies
* Incorrect training data
* Malicious inputs

Integrity is particularly important when AI outputs influence business decisions.

---

## 8. Availability Risk

AI systems may become unavailable because of:

* Infrastructure failure
* Cloud outages
* Denial-of-service attacks
* Provider outages
* Model-service failures
* Resource exhaustion
* Dependency failures
* Configuration errors

For business-critical AI, availability risk can become an operational and business-continuity risk.

---

## 9. Safety Risk

**AI Safety Risk** concerns the possibility that an AI system may cause physical or other significant harm through unsafe behavior.

Safety risk is particularly important for AI systems interacting with:

* Physical equipment
* Vehicles
* Industrial systems
* Healthcare processes
* Robotics
* Critical infrastructure
* Autonomous systems

A cybersecurity compromise may therefore become a safety event.

---

## 10. Security Risk and Safety Risk Are Not the Same

Security and safety are related but distinct.

**Security asks:**

> Can the system be protected from unauthorized or malicious activity?

**Safety asks:**

> Can the system operate without causing unacceptable harm?

For example, an autonomous industrial AI system may be perfectly secure against unauthorized access but still produce unsafe decisions because of a design or model failure.

Conversely, an unsafe system could also become more dangerous if an attacker compromises it.

---

## 11. Privacy Risk

AI systems can create privacy risks because they may process large quantities of personal information.

Privacy risks may involve:

* Excessive data collection
* Unauthorized processing
* Data leakage
* Re-identification
* Inappropriate retention
* Secondary use
* Profiling
* Automated decision-making
* Inadequate transparency
* Cross-border processing

Privacy Governance must therefore be integrated with AI Governance.

---

## 12. Ethical Risk

Ethical risk concerns the possibility that AI may produce outcomes inconsistent with accepted ethical principles or organizational values.

Examples include:

* Discrimination
* Unfair treatment
* Manipulation
* Lack of transparency
* Harmful use
* Exploitation
* Inappropriate surveillance
* Unjustified automated decisions

Ethical risk may exist even when an AI system is technically secure and legally compliant.

---

## 13. Legal and Regulatory Risk

AI systems may create legal and regulatory exposure.

Potential areas include:

* Data protection
* Intellectual property
* Consumer protection
* Employment
* Product liability
* Sector-specific regulation
* AI-specific regulation
* Contractual obligations
* Cross-border requirements

AI Governance should therefore identify applicable legal and regulatory obligations during the AI lifecycle.

---

## 14. Model Risk

**Model Risk** is the possibility that a model produces incorrect or inappropriate outcomes because of weaknesses in:

* Model design
* Data
* Assumptions
* Training
* Validation
* Implementation
* Monitoring

Model risk is especially important when AI outputs influence financial, operational, healthcare, security, or other high-impact decisions.

---

## 15. Data Risk

AI systems are heavily dependent on data.

Data risk may arise from:

* Poor data quality
* Incomplete data
* Biased data
* Incorrect data
* Unauthorized data
* Insufficient data
* Poor data lineage
* Inappropriate data use
* Data contamination

A fundamental principle is:

> **Poor-quality data can produce poor-quality AI outcomes.**

---

## 16. Bias and Fairness Risk

AI systems may produce biased outcomes because of:

* Biased training data
* Historical discrimination
* Unrepresentative datasets
* Model design
* Proxy variables
* Inappropriate deployment contexts

Fairness risk becomes especially important when AI affects people.

Examples include:

* Hiring
* Credit
* Insurance
* Education
* Healthcare
* Law enforcement
* Customer eligibility

---

## 17. Transparency and Explainability Risk

Some AI systems can be difficult to understand or explain.

This may create risks when stakeholders cannot determine:

* Why a decision was made
* What information influenced the result
* What limitations exist
* When human intervention is required

Transparency requirements should therefore be proportionate to the AI system's impact and risk.

---

## 18. Human Oversight Risk

AI systems may produce inappropriate results when humans:

* Trust AI outputs without verification
* Fail to challenge decisions
* Do not understand limitations
* Lack authority to intervene
* Are overwhelmed by AI-generated information

This can create **automation bias** and excessive reliance on AI.

Human oversight should therefore be deliberately designed rather than assumed.

---

## 19. Operational Risk

AI systems can introduce operational risks through:

* System failures
* Incorrect configurations
* Poor deployment
* Inadequate monitoring
* Model drift
* Integration failures
* Human error
* Process weaknesses

Operational AI risk should be incorporated into existing operational-risk management processes.

---

## 20. Third-Party AI Risk

Many organizations rely on external providers for:

* Foundation models
* Cloud AI services
* AI APIs
* AI SaaS
* Data services
* Model hosting
* AI development platforms

This creates third-party risks involving:

* Security
* Privacy
* Availability
* Data ownership
* Intellectual property
* Provider concentration
* Service changes
* Contractual limitations

The organization remains responsible for managing the risks associated with how it uses third-party AI.

---

## 21. Supply-Chain Risk

AI supply chains can contain multiple components:

**Data**

↓

**Libraries**

↓

**Models**

↓

**APIs**

↓

**Cloud Infrastructure**

↓

**Applications**

↓

**Users**

A weakness in one component can affect the entire AI system.

AI Security Governance should therefore consider the complete AI supply chain rather than only the organization's internally developed components.

---

## 22. Reputational Risk

AI incidents can damage trust.

Examples include:

* Harmful AI outputs
* Privacy violations
* Discriminatory decisions
* Security breaches
* Publicly exposed AI failures
* Misuse of generative AI

Reputational impact can occur even when financial losses are limited.

---

## 23. Strategic Risk

AI can create strategic risks when organizations:

* Adopt AI too slowly
* Adopt AI too quickly
* Depend excessively on one provider
* Fail to understand AI limitations
* Invest in unsuitable technologies
* Underestimate competitors
* Fail to establish appropriate governance

AI Governance should therefore support both **risk reduction and strategic decision-making**.

---

## 24. Interdependency of AI Risks

AI risks rarely occur independently.

For example:

**Poor Training Data**

↓

**Model Bias**

↓

**Incorrect Decision**

↓

**Customer Harm**

↓

**Regulatory Complaint**

↓

**Reputational Damage**

A single underlying weakness can therefore generate multiple risk categories.

---

## 25. AI Risk Cascade

A cyberattack may create a broader risk cascade:

**Prompt Injection**

↓

**Unauthorized Tool Invocation**

↓

**Sensitive Data Access**

↓

**Data Disclosure**

↓

**Privacy Incident**

↓

**Regulatory Exposure**

↓

**Reputational Damage**

This illustrates why AI Security Governance must understand the **business consequences** of technical AI threats.

---

## 26. Risk Classification

Organizations should establish a consistent approach to classifying AI risks.

Possible dimensions include:

* Impact
* Likelihood
* Severity
* Exposure
* Business criticality
* Human impact
* Regulatory significance
* Data sensitivity
* Security sensitivity

The resulting classification should determine the level of governance required.

---

## 27. Inherent AI Risk

**Inherent Risk** is the level of risk that exists before considering the effectiveness of controls.

For example:

An AI system processing highly sensitive personal information and making high-impact decisions may have high inherent risk.

This remains true even if the organization has implemented strong controls.

---

## 28. Control Effectiveness

Controls reduce risk when they are:

* Properly designed
* Implemented
* Operating consistently
* Monitored
* Tested

Examples include:

* Access control
* Encryption
* Human review
* Model validation
* Data-quality controls
* Monitoring
* Logging
* Incident response

Control effectiveness should be assessed rather than assumed.

---

## 29. Residual AI Risk

**Residual Risk** is the risk remaining after controls have been applied.

The basic relationship is:

**Inherent Risk**

↓

**Controls**

↓

**Residual Risk**

Management should determine whether residual risk is:

* Acceptable
* Requires further treatment
* Requires escalation
* Outside risk appetite

---

## 30. AI Risk Appetite

An organization should establish how much AI-related risk it is willing to accept.

Risk appetite may differ by:

* AI application
* Business process
* Data sensitivity
* Regulatory exposure
* Human impact
* Business criticality

For example, an organization may accept moderate AI risk for an internal productivity assistant but have extremely low tolerance for risk in an AI system supporting critical infrastructure.

---

## 31. Risk Treatment

AI risks can generally be addressed through:

### Avoid

Do not use the AI system or use case.

### Reduce

Implement controls to lower risk.

### Transfer

Transfer certain financial or contractual consequences where appropriate.

### Accept

Formally accept the remaining risk within authorized limits.

Risk treatment should be proportionate to the risk.

---

## 32. Risk Ownership

Every significant AI risk should have a clearly identified **Risk Owner**.

The Risk Owner should have authority to:

* Understand the risk
* Evaluate treatment options
* Approve remediation
* Accept residual risk where authorized
* Escalate risks exceeding authority

AI Governance should facilitate the process but should not automatically become the owner of every AI risk.

---

## 33. AI Risk Versus AI Governance

These concepts should not be confused.

**AI Risk**

→ What could go wrong?

**AI Governance**

→ Who makes the decisions and establishes oversight?

**AI Security Governance**

→ How are AI security risks governed?

**AI Security**

→ What technical and operational controls protect the AI environment?

This distinction is fundamental to designing an effective AI Governance program.

---

## 34. The AI Risk Governance Relationship

The overall relationship can be viewed as:

**AI Use Case**

↓

**AI Risk Identification**

↓

**Risk Classification**

↓

**Risk Assessment**

↓

**Control Selection**

↓

**Risk Treatment**

↓

**Risk Acceptance**

↓

**Continuous Monitoring**

↓

**Reassessment**

This creates a continuous risk-management cycle.

---

## 35. Why Multiple Risk Perspectives Are Necessary

A mature AI Security Governance program should avoid looking at AI exclusively through a cybersecurity lens.

A secure AI system can still:

* Produce discriminatory results
* Violate privacy
* Make unsafe decisions
* Breach regulatory requirements
* Create unacceptable business risk

Therefore:

> **Security is necessary for trustworthy AI governance, but security alone is not sufficient.**

This principle provides the foundation for the subsequent topics in this chapter.

## Part 2 — AI Risk Categories and Their Relationships

### 36. A Multidimensional AI Risk Model

AI risk should be viewed as a multidimensional risk environment rather than as a single category.

A practical AI risk model can include:

1. Strategic Risk
2. Business Risk
3. Operational Risk
4. Model Risk
5. Data Risk
6. Security Risk
7. Safety Risk
8. Privacy Risk
9. Ethical Risk
10. Legal and Regulatory Risk
11. Third-Party Risk
12. Supply-Chain Risk
13. Reputational Risk
14. Human and Workforce Risk
15. Resilience Risk

Not every AI system will have the same exposure across these categories.

The organization's governance approach should therefore be **risk-based and proportional**.

---

### 37. Strategic AI Risk

Strategic risk concerns whether AI adoption supports the organization's long-term objectives.

Examples include:

* Investing in unsuitable AI technologies
* Failing to adopt strategically important AI
* Overdependence on a single AI provider
* Poor AI investment decisions
* Misalignment between AI strategy and business strategy
* Failure to anticipate regulatory changes
* Failure to develop AI capabilities

Strategic AI risk is primarily concerned with whether the organization is making the **right AI decisions**, not merely whether it is operating AI securely.

---

### 38. Business AI Risk

Business risk concerns the impact of AI on business objectives and processes.

Examples include:

* Incorrect business decisions
* Poor customer outcomes
* Lost revenue
* Reduced productivity
* Incorrect recommendations
* Business-process disruption
* Failure to meet service commitments

An AI system can operate exactly as designed while still creating unacceptable business outcomes if the underlying use case is inappropriate.

---

### 39. Operational AI Risk

Operational AI risk arises from failures in the processes, people, systems, or technologies used to operate AI.

Examples include:

* Incorrect configuration
* Deployment failures
* Inadequate monitoring
* Poor change management
* Human error
* Model failures
* Integration failures
* Inadequate capacity
* Service interruptions

Operational controls should therefore be integrated into the AI lifecycle.

---

### 40. Model Risk

Model risk arises when an AI model does not perform as expected.

Potential causes include:

* Incorrect assumptions
* Poor model design
* Inadequate training
* Insufficient validation
* Poor-quality data
* Model drift
* Inappropriate deployment
* Unexpected inputs

Model risk becomes particularly important when AI outputs influence material decisions.

---

### 41. Model Performance Risk

An AI model may experience degraded performance over time.

Possible causes include:

* Changes in data
* Changes in user behavior
* Changes in business processes
* Environmental changes
* Concept drift
* Data drift
* Model degradation

Continuous monitoring is therefore necessary for AI systems that operate over extended periods.

---

### 42. Data Risk

AI depends heavily on data.

Data risk can arise from:

* Inaccurate data
* Incomplete data
* Outdated data
* Biased data
* Unauthorized data
* Poorly classified data
* Poor data lineage
* Data contamination
* Excessive data collection

Strong data governance is therefore an essential component of AI Governance.

---

### 43. Data Quality Risk

AI outputs are influenced by the quality of the underlying data.

Important data-quality characteristics include:

* Accuracy
* Completeness
* Consistency
* Timeliness
* Validity
* Uniqueness

Poor data quality may result in unreliable AI outcomes even when the underlying model is technically well designed.

---

### 44. Data Provenance Risk

Organizations should understand where AI data originates.

Data provenance should help answer:

* Where did the data come from?
* Who provided it?
* When was it collected?
* How was it modified?
* Who approved its use?
* What restrictions apply?
* Where is it stored?

Lack of provenance can make AI risk difficult to assess and audit.

---

### 45. Data Lineage

Data lineage describes how data moves and changes through the AI environment.

For example:

**Source Data**

↓

**Data Collection**

↓

**Data Cleaning**

↓

**Data Transformation**

↓

**Training Dataset**

↓

**Model**

↓

**AI Output**

Understanding this chain supports security, privacy, quality, and accountability.

---

### 46. Security Risk

AI Security Risk focuses on threats and vulnerabilities that could compromise AI systems or their supporting environments.

Security objectives commonly include:

* Confidentiality
* Integrity
* Availability
* Authenticity
* Authorization
* Accountability

AI Security Governance should integrate these objectives into the broader cybersecurity program.

---

### 47. AI Confidentiality Risk

Confidentiality risk can arise when AI systems expose information through:

* Prompts
* Responses
* Logs
* Training datasets
* Retrieval systems
* APIs
* Plugins
* Agents
* Third-party providers

Organizations should identify what information AI systems can access and where that information can flow.

---

### 48. AI Integrity Risk

Integrity risk occurs when AI systems, data, configurations, or outputs are improperly modified.

Potential causes include:

* Data poisoning
* Model tampering
* Unauthorized configuration changes
* Compromised software dependencies
* Prompt manipulation
* Unauthorized administrative access

Integrity controls should protect both the AI system and its supporting ecosystem.

---

### 49. AI Availability Risk

Availability risk occurs when an AI capability becomes unavailable or unusable.

Examples include:

* Cloud provider outage
* Model API failure
* Infrastructure failure
* Denial-of-service attack
* Capacity exhaustion
* Dependency failure

For critical AI systems, availability must be considered alongside business continuity and resilience.

---

### 50. AI Authentication and Authorization Risk

AI systems may interact with:

* Applications
* Databases
* APIs
* Files
* Cloud services
* Enterprise systems
* External tools

Improper authentication or excessive authorization can allow an AI system or attacker to access resources beyond its intended purpose.

This is especially important for AI agents.

---

### 51. Safety Risk

Safety risk concerns the possibility that AI behavior may result in physical or other serious harm.

Examples include AI supporting:

* Industrial automation
* Robotics
* Healthcare
* Transportation
* Critical infrastructure
* Autonomous systems

Safety requirements should be proportionate to the potential consequences of failure.

---

### 52. Functional Safety Versus AI Safety

Traditional functional safety generally focuses on ensuring that systems perform safely under defined conditions.

AI introduces additional complexity because AI behavior may depend on:

* Probabilistic outputs
* Training data
* Context
* Environmental changes
* Model behavior

AI Safety Governance may therefore require additional validation, monitoring, constraints, and human oversight.

---

### 53. Privacy Risk

Privacy risk arises when AI systems process personal information in ways that may create harm or violate applicable requirements.

Examples include:

* Excessive collection
* Unauthorized use
* Improper disclosure
* Profiling
* Re-identification
* Excessive retention
* Inadequate transparency

Privacy risk should be assessed from the earliest stages of AI system design.

---

### 54. Privacy by Design

Privacy should not be added after an AI system is developed.

Privacy requirements should be considered during:

**Planning**

→ What personal data is required?

**Design**

→ How should data be protected?

**Development**

→ What privacy controls are implemented?

**Deployment**

→ Are processing activities authorized?

**Operations**

→ Is privacy continuously monitored?

**Retirement**

→ Is data appropriately deleted or retained?

---

### 55. Ethical Risk

Ethical risk concerns whether an AI system's use or outcomes are consistent with accepted ethical principles and organizational values.

Potential issues include:

* Discrimination
* Manipulation
* Exploitation
* Deception
* Excessive surveillance
* Unfair treatment
* Harmful applications

Ethical considerations can arise even where no specific law has been violated.

---

### 56. Fairness Risk

Fairness risk occurs when AI produces systematically different or unjust outcomes for different individuals or groups.

Potential causes include:

* Biased data
* Historical discrimination
* Poor sampling
* Proxy variables
* Model design
* Deployment context

Fairness assessment should consider both technical performance and real-world impact.

---

### 57. Transparency Risk

Users and affected parties may not understand:

* That AI is being used
* What the AI does
* What information it uses
* What limitations exist
* How decisions are influenced

Transparency requirements should be appropriate to the AI system and its impact.

---

### 58. Explainability Risk

Some AI models can be difficult to explain.

This creates challenges when an organization must answer:

> **Why did the AI produce this result?**

Explainability requirements should be determined according to:

* Risk
* Impact
* Regulatory requirements
* Decision criticality
* Stakeholder needs

Not every AI system requires the same degree of explainability.

---

### 59. Human Oversight Risk

AI systems can create risk when humans rely excessively on AI outputs.

Potential problems include:

* Automation bias
* Inadequate review
* Poor understanding of model limitations
* Inability to intervene
* Excessive trust in AI recommendations

Human oversight should therefore be designed as a formal control where appropriate.

---

### 60. Automation Bias

**Automation bias** occurs when humans give excessive credibility to automated outputs.

For example:

> An employee accepts an AI-generated recommendation without independently checking whether it is correct.

This becomes particularly dangerous when AI is used for high-impact decisions.

---

### 61. Human-in-the-Loop

A **Human-in-the-Loop** model requires human involvement before an AI system can complete a defined action or decision.

For example:

**AI Recommendation**

↓

**Human Review**

↓

**Human Approval**

↓

**Action**

This can be appropriate for high-risk activities.

---

### 62. Human-on-the-Loop

A **Human-on-the-Loop** model allows AI to operate autonomously while humans continuously monitor the system and retain intervention authority.

For example:

**AI System**

↓

**Automated Operation**

↓

**Human Monitoring**

↓

**Human Intervention When Required**

This model may be appropriate where continuous human approval is impractical but oversight remains necessary.

---

### 63. Human-out-of-the-Loop

A **Human-out-of-the-Loop** system operates without human intervention during normal operation.

This may be acceptable for some low-risk use cases but can create significant concerns for high-impact AI.

The governance model should therefore define when autonomous operation is acceptable.

---

### 64. Legal and Regulatory Risk

AI systems may be subject to multiple legal and regulatory requirements.

Potential areas include:

* Privacy
* Cybersecurity
* Consumer protection
* Employment
* Intellectual property
* Financial services
* Healthcare
* Critical infrastructure
* Product safety
* AI-specific regulation

The applicable requirements depend on:

* Jurisdiction
* Sector
* AI use case
* Data processed
* Impact on individuals
* Business activities

---

### 65. Regulatory Change Risk

AI regulation continues to evolve.

Organizations may face risk when:

* New requirements become applicable
* Existing requirements change
* Regulatory guidance changes
* Enforcement expectations evolve

AI Governance should therefore connect to the organization's **Regulatory Change Management** process.

---

### 66. Intellectual Property Risk

AI can create intellectual property concerns involving:

* Training data
* Copyright
* Generated content
* Software code
* Proprietary information
* Third-party models
* Licensing restrictions

Organizations should establish clear requirements for the use of AI-generated and AI-assisted content.

---

### 67. Contractual Risk

AI providers may impose contractual terms involving:

* Data use
* Data retention
* Service availability
* Liability
* Intellectual property
* Model training
* Subprocessors
* Security requirements

AI Governance should work with Legal and Procurement to ensure material AI risks are appropriately addressed in contracts.

---

### 68. Third-Party Risk

AI providers may introduce risks outside the organization's direct control.

These can include:

* Security weaknesses
* Privacy issues
* Provider outages
* Model changes
* Service discontinuation
* Subprocessor dependencies
* Concentration risk

Third-party AI should therefore be incorporated into the organization's supplier-risk framework.

---

### 69. Fourth-Party AI Risk

A provider may itself depend on additional external providers.

For example:

**Organization**

↓

**AI SaaS Provider**

↓

**Foundation Model Provider**

↓

**Cloud Provider**

↓

**Data Provider**

This creates **fourth-party and extended supply-chain risk**.

Organizations should understand material dependencies where practical.

---

### 70. Concentration Risk

Concentration risk occurs when multiple business processes depend on the same AI provider or technology.

For example:

* One foundation model
* One cloud provider
* One AI API
* One AI SaaS platform

A major failure could affect multiple services simultaneously.

Critical AI dependencies should therefore be identified and monitored.

---

### 71. Resilience Risk

AI resilience concerns the ability of an organization to continue operating when AI capabilities fail.

Organizations should consider:

* Alternative providers
* Manual processes
* Backup models
* Redundant infrastructure
* Data availability
* Recovery procedures
* Service-level requirements

Critical AI systems should have appropriate continuity arrangements.

---

### 72. Reputational Risk

AI-related failures can affect stakeholder trust.

Potential events include:

* Harmful outputs
* Biased decisions
* Privacy incidents
* Security breaches
* AI misuse
* Publicly visible failures

Reputational consequences may extend well beyond the technical incident itself.

---

### 73. Workforce Risk

AI can create workforce-related risks involving:

* Job displacement
* Skill gaps
* Excessive reliance on AI
* Employee misuse
* Inadequate AI literacy
* Poor change management

Organizations should consider workforce impacts as part of AI adoption.

---

### 74. Shadow AI Risk

**Shadow AI** occurs when employees use AI tools without formal organizational authorization or governance.

Examples include:

* Public generative AI services
* Unapproved AI browser extensions
* Personal AI accounts
* Unauthorized AI APIs
* AI-enabled applications

Shadow AI can create:

* Data leakage
* Privacy risk
* Security risk
* Compliance risk
* Intellectual property risk

AI Governance should establish mechanisms for identifying and managing unauthorized AI use.

---

### 75. AI Misuse Risk

AI systems can be misused intentionally or unintentionally.

Examples include:

* Generating malicious content
* Fraud
* Social engineering
* Unauthorized surveillance
* Circumventing controls
* Creating deceptive content

Governance should consider both legitimate use and foreseeable misuse.

---

### 76. Abuse Risk

An AI system may be technically functioning as designed but still be abused by authorized users.

Examples include:

* Excessive data extraction
* Manipulative content generation
* Unauthorized automation
* Circumvention of business controls

Therefore, governance should address **how AI is used**, not only whether it works correctly.

---

### 77. AI Risk Interactions

Different risk categories can interact.

For example:

**Security Risk**

→ Compromised AI system

↓

**Privacy Risk**

→ Personal information exposed

↓

**Legal Risk**

→ Regulatory violation

↓

**Business Risk**

→ Financial impact

↓

**Reputational Risk**

→ Loss of customer trust

This demonstrates why AI risk should be assessed holistically.

---

### 78. AI Risk Dependency

Some risks depend on other risk conditions.

For example:

**Poor Data Quality**

may increase:

* Model Risk
* Fairness Risk
* Business Risk
* Safety Risk

Similarly:

**Weak Access Control**

may increase:

* Security Risk
* Privacy Risk
* Data Risk
* Operational Risk

Risk assessments should therefore identify important dependencies.

---

### 79. AI Risk Aggregation

Individual AI systems may appear low-risk when assessed separately.

However, the organization may have hundreds or thousands of AI systems.

Collectively, they may create significant:

* Security exposure
* Vendor concentration
* Data exposure
* Regulatory exposure
* Operational dependency

Enterprise AI Governance should therefore aggregate AI risks across the portfolio.

---

### 80. AI Portfolio Risk

An enterprise AI portfolio may contain:

* Low-risk productivity tools
* Customer-facing AI
* Internal decision-support systems
* High-impact AI
* Autonomous agents
* Critical AI systems

Portfolio-level governance allows management to understand the overall AI risk profile.

---

### 81. Risk-Based Governance

The principle of risk-based governance is:

> **Higher-risk AI should receive stronger governance, controls, oversight, and assurance.**

This avoids applying identical governance requirements to every AI system.

For example:

**Low Risk**

→ Simplified assessment.

**Moderate Risk**

→ Formal assessment and approval.

**High Risk**

→ Multidisciplinary review.

**Critical Risk**

→ Executive oversight and enhanced assurance.

---

### 82. Proportionality

Governance should be proportional to:

* Potential impact
* Likelihood
* Business criticality
* Regulatory significance
* Human impact
* Data sensitivity
* Security exposure

Overly restrictive governance can slow innovation.

Insufficient governance can expose the organization to unacceptable risk.

The objective is an appropriate balance.

---

### 83. Risk-Based AI Security Governance

AI Security Governance should therefore answer four fundamental questions:

1. **What AI systems exist?**
2. **What can go wrong?**
3. **What controls are required?**
4. **Who is accountable for the remaining risk?**

These questions form the foundation for the AI risk governance process.

---

### 84. AI Risk Assessment Foundation

A basic AI risk assessment should consider:

**Asset**

→ What AI capability is being protected?

**Threat**

→ What could cause harm?

**Vulnerability**

→ What weakness could be exploited?

**Impact**

→ What happens if the risk materializes?

**Likelihood**

→ How probable is the event?

**Control**

→ What reduces the risk?

**Residual Risk**

→ What remains?

This provides a structured foundation for AI risk management.

## Part 3 — Assessing, Treating, and Governing AI Risk

### 85. AI Risk Identification

AI risk identification is the process of systematically determining what could go wrong with an AI system, its supporting environment, or its intended use.

Risk identification should consider the entire AI ecosystem, including:

* Business objectives
* Users
* Data
* Models
* Applications
* Infrastructure
* Interfaces
* External services
* Vendors
* Human interactions
* Regulatory obligations

The objective is to identify risks before they become incidents.

---

### 86. AI Use Case Risk Assessment

Risk assessment should begin with the **AI use case**, not only the technology.

The organization should understand:

* What the AI system does
* Why it is being used
* Who uses it
* Who is affected
* What decisions it influences
* What information it processes
* What actions it can perform
* What happens if it fails

The same AI technology may have very different risk levels depending on how it is used.

---

### 87. Context Determines AI Risk

Consider two implementations of the same language model.

**Use Case A**

An employee uses AI to summarize internal meeting notes.

**Use Case B**

An AI system recommends whether an individual should receive a high-impact service.

The underlying model could be identical, but the governance requirements are very different because the **context and potential impact differ**.

---

### 88. AI Risk Assessment Dimensions

An organization may assess AI risk using dimensions such as:

* Purpose
* Impact
* Autonomy
* Data sensitivity
* User population
* Affected population
* Decision significance
* Security exposure
* Safety implications
* Regulatory requirements
* Third-party dependency
* Business criticality

These dimensions help determine the appropriate governance level.

---

### 89. Impact Assessment

Impact assessment determines what could happen if an AI-related risk materializes.

Potential impacts include:

* Financial loss
* Operational disruption
* Privacy harm
* Security compromise
* Physical harm
* Legal consequences
* Regulatory penalties
* Customer harm
* Employee harm
* Reputational damage

Impact should be evaluated across multiple dimensions rather than using financial loss alone.

---

### 90. Likelihood Assessment

Likelihood considers how probable a risk event is.

Factors may include:

* Threat capability
* Exposure
* Vulnerability
* Existing controls
* Historical incidents
* System complexity
* User behavior
* External dependencies

Likelihood assessments should be based on defined organizational criteria.

---

### 91. Risk Scoring

Organizations may use a qualitative or quantitative risk model.

A simple qualitative model may classify risks as:

* Low
* Moderate
* High
* Critical

A quantitative approach may use:

**Risk = Likelihood × Impact**

However, this is only a simplified representation.

Complex AI risks may require additional factors.

---

### 92. Risk Scoring Limitations

AI risk cannot always be represented accurately by a single numerical score.

Some risks involve:

* Uncertainty
* Rare but catastrophic events
* Difficult-to-measure societal impacts
* Complex dependencies
* Emerging threats
* Limited historical data

Therefore, numerical scoring should support professional judgment rather than replace it.

---

### 93. Risk Scenarios

A useful AI risk assessment technique is to develop specific risk scenarios.

For example:

> An attacker manipulates an AI application's input so that the model reveals confidential information through its output.

The scenario can then be evaluated for:

* Threat
* Vulnerability
* Impact
* Likelihood
* Controls
* Residual risk

Scenario-based analysis makes abstract AI risks easier to understand.

---

### 94. Threat Modeling for AI

AI threat modeling identifies potential ways an AI system could be attacked or abused.

It may consider:

* Attackers
* Attack paths
* Trust boundaries
* Assets
* Entry points
* Dependencies
* Security controls

Threat modeling should be performed during design and updated when the AI architecture changes.

Potential AI threat actors include:

* External attackers

* Competitors
* Nation-state actors
* Fraudsters
* Automated attackers
* Opportunistic users

Different threat actors may have different capabilities and motivations.

### 96. AI Vulnerability Identification

AI vulnerabilities may exist in:

* Models

* Third-party dependencies

AI Governance should ensure that identified vulnerabilities are assigned to appropriate owners.

### 97. AI Control Selection

Controls should be selected according to identified risks.

Possible controls include:

* Preventive controls
* Detective controls
* Corrective controls
* Compensating controls
* Technical controls
* Administrative controls
* Physical controls

The objective is not to implement every possible control, but to implement controls appropriate to the risk.

---

### 98. Preventive AI Controls

Preventive controls attempt to stop undesirable events before they occur.

Examples include:

* Access restrictions
* Secure development standards
* Input validation
* Data classification
* Model approval
* Network segmentation
* Least privilege
* Approved AI tools
* Human approval requirements

Preventive controls are particularly important for high-risk AI systems.

---

### 99. Detective AI Controls

Detective controls identify undesirable activity or behavior.

Examples include:

* Logging
* Monitoring
* Anomaly detection
* Model-performance monitoring
* Security alerts
* Data-loss monitoring
* User activity monitoring

Detection is essential because preventive controls are rarely perfect.

---

### 100. Corrective AI Controls

Corrective controls reduce the consequences of an incident or failure.

Examples include:

* Model rollback
* Account suspension
* Agent shutdown
* Data restoration
* Incident response
* Configuration correction
* Model retraining

High-risk autonomous systems should have clearly defined corrective mechanisms.

---

### 101. Compensating Controls

A compensating control provides an alternative safeguard when the preferred control cannot be implemented.

For example:

If continuous automated model validation is unavailable, the organization may introduce enhanced human review.

Compensating controls should be documented and approved.

---

### 102. Defense in Depth

AI systems should not depend on a single security control.

A defense-in-depth approach may include:

**Identity Controls**

↓

**Application Controls**

↓

**Model Controls**

↓

**Data Controls**

↓

**Monitoring**

↓

**Human Oversight**

↓

**Incident Response**

If one control fails, additional controls remain available.

---

### 103. Least Privilege for AI

AI systems should receive only the permissions necessary to perform their intended functions.

This principle is particularly important for AI agents.

An AI agent that only needs to read a specific database should not automatically receive:

* Administrative access
* Unrestricted file access
* Ability to execute arbitrary commands
* Access to unrelated business systems

Least privilege limits the potential impact of compromise or misuse.

---

### 104. AI Risk Treatment

Once risks have been assessed, the organization should determine how each risk will be treated.

Typical treatment options are:

1. Avoid
2. Reduce
3. Transfer
4. Accept

Risk treatment should be documented and assigned to an accountable owner.

---

### 105. Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance means deciding not to undertake the activity creating the unacceptable risk.

Examples include:

* Rejecting a high-risk AI use case
* Prohibiting an AI capability
* Removing an AI feature
* Choosing a non-AI alternative

Risk avoidance can be appropriate where the potential impact exceeds organizational risk appetite.

---

### 106. Risk Reduction

Risk reduction involves implementing controls to lower likelihood or impact.

Examples include:

* Human review
* Access restrictions
* Data minimization
* Security testing
* Model validation
* Monitoring
* Output filtering
* Segmentation

Risk reduction is likely to be the most common AI risk treatment approach.

---

### 107. Risk Transfer

Risk transfer involves shifting certain consequences or responsibilities through mechanisms such as:

* Insurance
* Contracts
* Service agreements
* Indemnification
* Supplier arrangements

Risk transfer does not eliminate the underlying AI risk.

The organization should continue to manage risks that remain under its control.

---

### 108. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance means formally acknowledging that residual risk will remain.

Risk acceptance should:

* Be documented
* Be performed by an authorized person
* Consider risk appetite
* Have an appropriate expiration or review period
* Include relevant conditions

AI Governance should not allow informal risk acceptance.

---

### 109. Risk Escalation

Risk should be escalated when:

* It exceeds risk appetite
* It exceeds delegated authority
* It affects multiple business units
* It has significant regulatory implications
* It may cause material harm
* It involves critical systems
* Management cannot resolve it at the operational level

Escalation paths should be defined before incidents occur.

---

### 110. Risk Acceptance Authority

Not every employee should be able to accept AI risk.

Organizations should establish levels of authority.

For example:

**Low Risk**

→ Business Manager

**Moderate Risk**

→ AI System Owner

**High Risk**

→ Risk Owner / Senior Management

**Critical Risk**

→ Executive Committee / Board-level authority, where appropriate

Actual authority levels should be defined by organizational policy.

---

### 111. Risk Exceptions

Organizations may sometimes be unable to comply fully with an AI governance requirement.

An exception process should identify:

* Requirement
* Reason for exception
* Risk created
* Compensating controls
* Risk owner
* Approval authority
* Expiration date
* Review date

Exceptions should be temporary and controlled rather than becoming permanent bypasses.

### 148. AI Risk Classification Framework

Organizations should establish a consistent classification framework.

A practical model may classify AI systems as:

**Level 1 — Low Risk**

Limited impact and low sensitivity.

Examples:

* Internal productivity assistance
* Basic text summarization
* Low-impact content generation

**Level 2 — Moderate Risk**

Potential business, privacy, security, or operational consequences.

Examples:

* Customer-support AI
* Internal decision-support tools
* AI-enabled business applications

**Level 3 — High Risk**

Significant impact on individuals, business operations, security, or regulatory obligations.

Examples:

* High-impact decision support
* Sensitive-data processing
* Critical business processes

**Level 4 — Critical Risk**

Potential for severe consequences involving safety, critical operations, major regulatory exposure, or significant societal impact.

Higher-risk classifications should trigger progressively stronger governance requirements.

### 149. Risk Classification Should Not Be Static

An AI system may change risk category during its lifecycle.

For example:

An internal AI assistant may initially process only public information.

Later, the organization may integrate it with:

* Customer databases
* Internal documents
* Enterprise applications
* Financial systems

The risk profile has changed.

The organization should therefore reassess the system rather than continue using its original classification.

Possible AI incidents include:

* Data leakage
* Model compromise
* Unsafe output
* Unauthorized AI activity
* AI service outage
* Malicious prompt manipulation
* Incorrect high-impact decision

Incident severity should consider both technical and business impact.

---

### 163. AI Incident Severity

A practical severity model might include:

**Low**

Limited impact and easily contained.

**Moderate**

Material operational or business impact.

**High**

Significant security, privacy, regulatory, or business impact.

**Critical**

Severe impact involving critical operations, major data exposure, significant human harm, or substantial regulatory consequences.

Severity criteria should be defined before incidents occur.

---

### 164. AI Risk and Change Management

Changes to AI systems can alter their risk profile.

Examples include:

* New model version
* New training data
* New prompt architecture
* New plugin
* New API
* New user group
* New geographic deployment
* New business purpose

Material changes should trigger appropriate governance review.

---

### 165. AI Model Change Risk

A model update may change:

* Accuracy
* Bias
* Security characteristics
* Resource consumption
* Output behavior
* Privacy characteristics

Organizations should therefore avoid treating model updates as ordinary software updates when their risk implications are significant.

---

### 166. AI Risk and Configuration Management

AI systems may depend on numerous configurations.

Examples include:

* Model parameters
* System prompts
* Access permissions
* Retrieval settings
* API connections
* Agent tools
* Safety filters
* Logging configurations

Unauthorized or undocumented configuration changes can introduce risk.

---

### 167. AI Risk and Access Management

Access should be based on business need and risk.

Organizations should consider:

* Who can use the AI system?
* Who can modify it?
* Who can access its data?
* Who can change prompts?
* Who can deploy models?
* Who can approve tools?
* Who can disable the system?

Privileged access should receive stronger controls and monitoring.

---

### 168. AI Risk and Segregation of Duties

Where practical, critical AI activities should be separated.

For example:

**Developer**

→ Builds the model.

**Validator**

→ Tests the model.

**Approver**

→ Authorizes deployment.

**Operator**

→ Runs the system.

**Auditor**

→ Independently evaluates governance.

This reduces the risk of uncontrolled self-approval.

### 169. AI Risk and Documentation

Documentation should be proportional to risk.

Higher-risk AI systems may require:

* System documentation

### 171. AI Risk and Assurance

* Compliance review

The assurance model should be proportional to AI risk.

Internal Audit can independently assess whether:

* Responsibilities are clear
* Risk assessments are performed

### 196. AI Risk Governance Maturity

### Developing

AI risk management is continuously monitored, measured, automated, and improved.

* Risk-based classification

* Continuous monitoring
* Independent assurance
* Executive visibility

### 198. Common AI Risk Governance Failures

Organizations should avoid:

* Treating AI as purely an IT issue
* Failing to identify AI systems
* Having no AI System Owner
* Using one risk model for every AI system
* Ignoring third-party AI
* Ignoring shadow AI
* Treating model validation as sufficient
* Assuming compliance equals security
* Assuming security equals safety
* Allowing informal risk acceptance
* Failing to reassess changed AI systems
* Reporting technical details without business impact

These weaknesses can undermine the entire AI Governance program.

### 199. Fundamental AI Risk Governance Principles

The key principles developed throughout this topic are:

1. **AI risk is broader than AI security risk.**
2. **AI security is necessary but not sufficient for responsible AI governance.**
3. **Risk should be assessed according to context and impact.**
4. **Higher-risk AI requires stronger governance.**
5. **Every material risk should have an accountable owner.**
6. **Controls should be proportional to risk.**
7. **Residual risk should be explicitly accepted or escalated.**
8. **AI risk should be continuously monitored.**
9. **AI risk should integrate with enterprise risk management.**
10. **Independent assurance should be applied where appropriate.**
11. **AI risk decisions should be documented and auditable.**
12. **AI Governance should continuously improve.**

### 200. Conclusion

AI creates a risk environment that extends far beyond traditional cybersecurity.

An organization must consider the relationship between:

**AI Risk**

→ **Security Risk**

→ **Safety Risk**

→ **Privacy Risk**

→ **Ethical Risk**

→ **Legal and Regulatory Risk**

→ **Operational Risk**

→ **Business Risk**

→ **Strategic Risk**

These risks are interconnected.

A cybersecurity control may prevent unauthorized access but cannot, by itself, guarantee that an AI system is fair.

A privacy control may protect personal information but cannot guarantee that an AI system is safe.

A model-validation process may demonstrate technical performance but cannot determine whether the organization should use the AI system in the first place.

AI Security Governance therefore requires a **holistic, risk-based, lifecycle-oriented approach**.

The fundamental governance objective is to ensure that the organization can continuously answer:

> **What AI risks exist?**

> **Who owns those risks?**

> **What controls address them?**

> **What residual risk remains?**

> **Who has authority to accept or reject that risk?**

> **How is the risk monitored over time?**

This establishes the risk foundation necessary for understanding why AI creates distinctive security and governance challenges, which leads directly into **01.05 — Why AI Creates New Security and Governance Challenges**.
