# 01.17 — AI Governance Culture and AI Literacy

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Governance Culture and AI Literacy

### 1. Introduction to AI Governance Culture

AI governance cannot depend entirely on policies, technical controls, committees, and approval processes.

Organizations also need a culture in which people understand:

* Why AI governance matters
* What responsible AI behavior means
* What security responsibilities they have
* When AI use requires additional review
* When risks should be reported
* When AI should not be used

**AI governance culture** is the collective set of behaviors, attitudes, expectations, and practices that influence how an organization manages and uses AI.

---

### 2. AI Governance Culture Versus AI Governance Framework

An AI governance framework defines formal structures and requirements.

AI governance culture determines how people behave within those structures.

For example:

**Governance Framework**

→ Requires employees to report suspected AI security incidents.

**Governance Culture**

→ Employees actually report suspicious AI behavior without hiding or ignoring it.

A strong framework with a weak culture can produce poor governance outcomes.

---

### 3. Why AI Governance Culture Matters

AI introduces governance decisions throughout the organization.

Employees may:

* Use public AI services
* Upload organizational information
* Develop AI applications
* Configure AI agents
* Select AI vendors
* Use AI-generated content
* Make decisions based on AI outputs

Therefore, AI governance cannot be limited to a specialized AI or cybersecurity team.

---

### 4. AI Governance as an Organizational Responsibility

AI governance should be treated as a shared organizational responsibility.

Different groups contribute different responsibilities.

For example:

**Employees**

→ Follow approved AI practices.

**Managers**

→ Ensure appropriate use within their teams.

**AI Teams**

→ Build and operate AI systems securely.

**Security**

→ Manage cybersecurity risks.

**Privacy**

→ Manage privacy implications.

**Risk**

→ Evaluate and challenge risk.

**Executives**

→ Establish strategic direction and accountability.

---

### 5. AI Governance Culture and Employee Behavior

Culture becomes visible through everyday behavior.

Examples of positive behavior include:

* Checking whether an AI tool is approved
* Avoiding unauthorized sensitive-data disclosure
* Questioning suspicious AI outputs
* Reporting AI-related incidents
* Following AI approval processes
* Escalating uncertain situations

These behaviors should become normal organizational practices.

---

### 6. AI Literacy

AI literacy refers to the knowledge and understanding required to use, evaluate, govern, and interact with AI appropriately.

AI literacy does not mean that every employee must become an AI engineer.

It means that people should understand enough about AI to:

* Use it appropriately
* Recognize limitations
* Identify risks
* Protect information
* Understand their responsibilities
* Make informed decisions

---

### 7. AI Literacy Versus AI Technical Expertise

AI literacy and AI technical expertise are different.

**AI Literacy**

→ General understanding necessary for safe and effective interaction with AI.

**AI Technical Expertise**

→ Specialized knowledge required to design, develop, secure, validate, or operate AI systems.

An executive may require AI literacy without needing the technical skills of an ML engineer.

---

### 8. Enterprise AI Literacy

AI literacy should exist across the enterprise.

Different roles require different levels of knowledge.

For example:

* General employees need basic AI awareness.
* Managers need operational AI governance knowledge.
* Developers need technical AI security knowledge.
* Security teams need AI-specific cybersecurity expertise.
* Risk professionals need AI risk knowledge.
* Executives need strategic AI risk and governance knowledge.

---

### 9. AI Literacy as a Governance Control

AI literacy can function as a preventive governance control.

A knowledgeable employee is more likely to:

* Recognize unsafe AI use
* Identify suspicious outputs
* Protect confidential information
* Follow approved processes
* Escalate concerns

Therefore, training and awareness should be considered part of the broader AI control environment.

---

### 10. AI Literacy and Human Judgment

AI literacy should reinforce the principle that AI outputs require appropriate human judgment.

Employees should understand that AI systems can:

* Produce inaccurate information
* Generate misleading outputs
* Misinterpret context
* Reflect limitations in training data
* Produce inconsistent results

AI output should not automatically be treated as authoritative.

---

### 11. Understanding AI Limitations

Basic AI literacy should include awareness of common limitations.

These may include:

* Hallucination
* Bias
* Data limitations
* Model uncertainty
* Context limitations
* Prompt sensitivity
* Outdated information
* Unexpected behavior

The objective is not to make employees AI researchers but to prevent inappropriate reliance on AI.

---

### 12. AI Security Awareness

AI literacy should include security awareness.

Employees should understand risks such as:

* Prompt injection
* Sensitive-data disclosure
* Malicious AI-generated content
* Unauthorized AI tools
* Credential exposure
* AI-assisted phishing
* Model manipulation
* Insecure integrations

Security awareness should be adapted to the employee's role.

---

### 13. AI Privacy Awareness

Employees should understand that AI usage can create privacy risks.

Awareness should cover:

* Personal information
* Sensitive information
* Data minimization
* Approved AI services
* Data sharing
* Retention
* External AI providers

Employees should know what information they are permitted to submit to AI systems.

---

### 14. AI Governance Awareness

Employees should understand the organization's basic governance expectations.

Awareness may include:

* Approved AI tools
* Prohibited AI uses
* AI approval requirements
* Data restrictions
* Reporting obligations
* Security requirements
* Privacy requirements

The objective is to make governance requirements understandable and actionable.

---

### 15. AI Acceptable Use

Organizations should establish clear expectations for acceptable AI use.

Acceptable-use guidance may address:

* Approved tools
* Business purposes
* Personal use
* Confidential information
* Customer information
* Code generation
* Content generation
* Automated decisions

Rules should be practical enough for employees to apply in everyday situations.

---

### 16. Shadow AI

**Shadow AI** refers to AI usage that occurs outside the organization's approved governance, security, procurement, or oversight processes.

Examples include employees independently using:

* Public generative AI services
* Unapproved AI assistants
* Personal AI accounts
* Unapproved AI coding tools
* AI browser extensions

Shadow AI can create governance visibility gaps.

---

### 17. Why Employees Use Shadow AI

Employees may use unauthorized AI because:

* Approved tools are unavailable
* Approval processes are slow
* Employees want greater productivity
* They do not understand the policy
* They do not consider the activity risky
* The organization has not provided suitable alternatives

Therefore, addressing shadow AI requires more than simply prohibiting it.

---

### 18. Managing Shadow AI Through Culture

A mature organization should combine:

* Clear policies
* Accessible approved tools
* Practical training
* Technical controls
* Monitoring
* Easy reporting
* Appropriate enforcement

Employees should have safe alternatives to unauthorized AI services.

---

### 19. AI Governance Culture and Leadership

Leadership strongly influences governance culture.

Employees observe whether leaders:

* Follow AI policies
* Respect approval processes
* Ask about risks
* Encourage reporting
* Reward responsible behavior
* Treat security as a priority

Leadership behavior therefore becomes an important cultural signal.

---

### 20. Executive AI Literacy

Executives should understand AI sufficiently to make informed governance decisions.

Executive literacy may cover:

* AI capabilities
* AI limitations
* AI risk categories
* Security risks
* Privacy risks
* Regulatory exposure
* Business opportunities
* Governance responsibilities

Executives do not need deep technical implementation knowledge to provide effective oversight.

---

### 21. Board AI Literacy

Boards increasingly need sufficient AI literacy to oversee material AI risks and opportunities.

Board-level understanding may include:

* Strategic AI use
* Material AI risks
* AI governance
* Cybersecurity implications
* Privacy implications
* Regulatory developments
* Organizational accountability

Board literacy supports effective oversight without requiring technical engineering expertise.

---

### 22. Manager AI Literacy

Managers occupy an important position between enterprise governance and employee behavior.

Managers should understand:

* Approved AI usage
* Team responsibilities
* Data restrictions
* Escalation requirements
* AI-related risks
* Incident reporting
* Employee training requirements

Managers should be capable of recognizing when AI activity requires additional governance.

---

### 23. Developer AI Literacy

Developers require deeper AI literacy because they may directly build or integrate AI systems.

Knowledge may include:

* Secure AI development
* Model risks
* Data security
* Prompt security
* Dependency risks
* AI application architecture
* Testing
* Logging
* Monitoring

Developer literacy should align with the technologies being used.

---

### 24. Security Professional AI Literacy

Cybersecurity professionals need specialized AI security knowledge.

Areas may include:

* AI attack surfaces
* Prompt injection
* Model attacks
* Data poisoning
* Model theft
* Adversarial inputs
* AI supply-chain risks
* AI-specific monitoring

This enables security teams to extend existing cybersecurity capabilities into AI environments.

---

### 25. Risk Professional AI Literacy

Risk professionals should understand how AI changes traditional risk assessment.

Knowledge may include:

* AI risk characteristics
* Model uncertainty
* AI lifecycle risks
* Third-party AI risks
* Autonomous decision-making
* AI risk aggregation

This enables AI risks to be integrated into enterprise risk management.

---

### 26. Privacy Professional AI Literacy

Privacy professionals should understand AI-related data risks.

Relevant areas may include:

* Training data
* Inference
* Personal-data processing
* Data minimization
* Profiling
* Automated decision-making
* AI provider data handling

Privacy expertise should be integrated with AI governance rather than treated as a separate activity.

---

### 27. Procurement AI Literacy

Procurement teams may influence AI risk through vendor selection.

They should understand the importance of evaluating:

* AI service providers
* Data handling
* Security requirements
* Privacy requirements
* Contractual protections
* Subprocessors
* Service dependencies

Procurement decisions can create long-term AI governance consequences.

---

### 28. Legal and Compliance AI Literacy

Legal and compliance professionals should understand how AI can affect:

* Regulatory obligations
* Contracts
* Intellectual property
* Privacy
* Liability
* Consumer protection
* Employment
* Sector-specific requirements

AI literacy enables these functions to identify issues earlier in the AI lifecycle.

---

### 29. Internal Audit AI Literacy

Internal auditors increasingly need sufficient AI knowledge to assess AI governance.

They may need to understand:

* AI governance structures
* AI risks
* AI controls
* AI lifecycle
* Model governance
* AI decision rights
* AI evidence

Internal Audit does not necessarily need to develop AI systems, but it needs enough knowledge to provide meaningful assurance.

---

### 30. Role-Based AI Literacy

AI literacy should be role-based rather than identical for everyone.

A practical structure may include:

**Level 1 — AI Awareness**

→ General workforce.

**Level 2 — AI Governance Literacy**

→ Managers and business users.

**Level 3 — AI Risk and Security Literacy**

→ Risk, security, privacy, compliance, and governance professionals.

**Level 4 — AI Technical Expertise**

→ Engineers, developers, architects, data scientists, and specialized AI security professionals.

**Level 5 — AI Executive Oversight**

→ Senior executives and board members.

The objective is to provide each role with the knowledge necessary to make safe, informed, and accountable AI decisions.

## Part 2 — Building AI Governance Culture and Role-Based AI Literacy

### 31. AI Literacy Strategy

An organization should establish a structured AI literacy strategy rather than relying on informal learning.

The strategy should define:

* Target audiences
* Required knowledge
* Learning objectives
* Training methods
* Frequency
* Assessment
* Ownership
* Measurement

AI literacy should be treated as an ongoing organizational capability.

---

### 32. AI Literacy Needs Assessment

Before developing training, organizations should determine what different groups need to understand.

The assessment may consider:

* Job responsibilities
* AI exposure
* Risk level
* Data access
* Decision authority
* Technical responsibilities
* Regulatory responsibilities

This prevents organizations from providing identical training to people with very different AI responsibilities.

---

### 33. AI Literacy Competency Framework

A competency framework can define the knowledge expected for different roles.

Competencies may include:

* AI fundamentals
* AI governance
* AI security
* AI privacy
* AI risk
* AI ethics
* AI regulations
* AI operational practices
* AI incident management

Each competency can be assigned an appropriate proficiency level.

---

### 34. AI Literacy Levels

A practical competency structure may include:

**Awareness**

→ Understand basic concepts and organizational expectations.

**Working Knowledge**

→ Apply AI governance requirements in daily activities.

**Practitioner**

→ Perform specialized AI governance, risk, security, or privacy activities.

**Advanced**

→ Design, assess, or manage complex AI environments.

**Expert**

→ Provide strategic or specialized leadership.

Not every employee needs to reach the same level.

---

### 35. AI Awareness for the General Workforce

General workforce awareness should focus on practical behavior.

Employees should understand:

* What AI tools are approved
* What information may be entered
* What information must not be entered
* How to evaluate AI outputs
* How to report concerns
* When human review is required

Training should use practical examples rather than technical theory.

### 36. AI Literacy for Managers

Managers require additional knowledge because they supervise AI use within business processes.

Training may cover:

* AI approval

* AI lifecycle management

System owners should understand that ownership extends beyond technical deployment.

* Prompt security
* Input validation
* Output handling
* Authentication

### 42. AI Literacy for Privacy Teams

### 45. AI Literacy for Internal Audit

Training may include:

* AI lifecycle

* AI governance

* Model governance
* AI security

* AI audit techniques

Specialist AI audit expertise may be required for highly technical environments.

Board education should focus on oversight.

### 49. Scenario-Based AI Training

Scenario-based training can be more effective than purely theoretical instruction.

Examples include:

* An employee wants to upload confidential information into a public AI tool.
* A developer discovers prompt injection in an AI application.
* A business unit wants to deploy an unapproved AI service.
* An AI system produces a potentially harmful output.
* A vendor changes its underlying model.

Participants should determine what actions are required.

---

### 50. AI Governance Tabletop Exercises

Organizations may conduct tabletop exercises focused on AI governance.

Exercises may simulate:

* AI security incidents
* Privacy incidents
* Model failures
* Unauthorized AI usage
* AI vendor outages
* Harmful autonomous actions

Exercises test whether people understand their decision rights and escalation responsibilities.

---

### 51. AI Incident Awareness

Employees should know how to recognize AI-related incidents.

Potential indicators include:

* Unexpected AI behavior
* Unauthorized data exposure
* Suspicious prompts
* Manipulated outputs
* Unapproved AI tools
* Security alerts
* Privacy concerns

Employees should know where and how to report these issues.

---

### 52. Psychological Safety and Reporting

AI governance culture should encourage employees to report concerns without unnecessary fear of punishment.

Employees should be able to raise:

* Security concerns
* Policy violations
* AI errors
* Unsafe behavior
* Privacy concerns
* Governance gaps

A strong reporting culture helps organizations detect problems earlier.

---

### 53. No-Blame Learning Culture

Organizations should distinguish between:

* Deliberate violations
* Negligent behavior
* Honest mistakes
* Good-faith reporting

A culture that punishes every mistake may discourage reporting.

A mature culture focuses on accountability while also learning from failures.

---

### 54. AI Governance Communication

AI governance requirements should be communicated through multiple channels.

Examples include:

* Training
* Intranet resources
* Email communications
* Manager briefings
* Awareness campaigns
* Policy portals
* Technical documentation

Communication should reinforce the same core expectations.

---

### 55. Just-in-Time AI Guidance

Employees often need guidance at the moment they use AI.

Organizations can provide contextual guidance through:

* AI tool banners
* Data-loss prevention controls
* Prompt warnings
* Approval workflows
* Embedded policy links
* Automated classification

Just-in-time guidance can be more effective than relying exclusively on annual training.

---

### 56. AI Governance Champions

Organizations may establish AI governance champions within business units.

Champions can:

* Promote responsible AI practices
* Answer basic questions
* Identify emerging issues
* Encourage policy adoption
* Communicate governance changes

Champions should complement, not replace, formal governance functions.

---

### 57. AI Governance Communities of Practice

Communities of practice can bring together people working with AI governance.

Participants may include:

* AI engineers
* Security professionals
* Risk professionals
* Privacy professionals
* Legal specialists
* Business leaders

These communities can share:

* Lessons learned
* Emerging risks
* Good practices
* Implementation challenges

---

### 58. AI Literacy Knowledge Repository

Organizations should maintain accessible AI governance learning resources.

Resources may include:

* Policies
* Standards
* FAQs
* Training materials
* AI security guidance
* Approved-tool lists
* Reporting procedures
* Practical examples

The repository should remain current.

---

### 59. AI Literacy and Policy Awareness

Training should connect directly to organizational policies.

Employees should understand not only:

> "AI can create security risks."

but also:

> "Here is what the organization requires you to do when using AI."

Practical policy interpretation is essential for effective governance.

---

### 60. AI Literacy and Decision Rights

People with AI decision authority should understand the limits of their authority.

Training should explain:

* What they may approve
* What they may reject
* What they may accept
* What must be escalated
* What evidence is required

This connects AI literacy directly to the organization's decision-rights framework.

# 01.17 — AI Governance Culture and AI Literacy

## Part 3 — AI Governance Behavior, Awareness, Accountability and Organizational Adoption

### 61. AI Governance as a Behavioral Capability

AI governance becomes effective when governance requirements are translated into everyday behavior.

Employees should understand how governance applies to:

* AI tool selection
* Data handling
* AI development
* AI deployment
* AI-generated content
* AI-assisted decisions
* AI incidents
* AI system changes

Governance should therefore be embedded into normal business activities rather than treated as a separate administrative function.

---

### 62. AI Governance Behaviors

Organizations should define the behaviors expected from employees and managers.

Examples include:

* Use only approved AI services.
* Protect sensitive information.
* Verify important AI-generated outputs.
* Follow AI approval requirements.
* Report suspicious behavior.
* Escalate uncertainty.
* Respect human oversight requirements.
* Follow security and privacy controls.

Clear behavioral expectations make governance more actionable.

---

### 63. Responsible AI Behavior

Responsible AI behavior means using AI in ways that align with organizational requirements, applicable obligations, and established governance principles.

This includes:

* Appropriate use
* Appropriate human judgment
* Appropriate data handling
* Appropriate transparency
* Appropriate security
* Appropriate escalation

Responsible behavior applies even when an AI tool is technically capable of performing an action.

---

### 64. AI Governance Accountability

Culture should reinforce that AI governance responsibilities belong to identifiable people and functions.

Accountability may apply to:

* AI system owners
* Business owners
* Developers
* Managers
* Security teams
* Risk teams
* Privacy teams
* Executives

Clear accountability prevents the assumption that "the AI system" is responsible for its own behavior.

---

### 65. Individual Employee Accountability

Employees should understand their personal responsibilities when using AI.

They may be responsible for:

* Following approved-use requirements
* Protecting information
* Verifying outputs
* Reporting issues
* Avoiding prohibited uses
* Following organizational procedures

AI should not be treated as a substitute for employee accountability.

---

### 66. Managerial Accountability

Managers have additional responsibility for ensuring that AI use within their teams remains appropriate.

Managers should:

* Understand AI usage within their teams
* Ensure employees receive appropriate training
* Identify unauthorized use
* Escalate material concerns
* Reinforce governance requirements

Managers should not assume that centralized AI teams can identify every local governance issue.

---

### 67. AI System Owner Accountability

AI system owners should remain accountable for the governance of systems under their ownership.

Responsibilities may include:

* Risk management
* Security
* Privacy
* Documentation
* Monitoring
* Change management
* Incident response
* Retirement

Ownership should continue throughout the AI lifecycle.

---

### 68. Leadership Accountability

Leadership establishes expectations for AI governance.

Leaders should demonstrate that:

* AI risk matters
* Security matters
* Reporting concerns is encouraged
* Governance requirements are not optional
* Business objectives do not automatically override risk controls

Leadership behavior strongly influences organizational culture.

---

### 69. Tone From the Top

Senior leadership should establish the tone for responsible AI use.

A strong tone from the top communicates that:

* AI innovation is encouraged.
* AI risks are taken seriously.
* Employees are expected to use AI responsibly.
* Material concerns should be escalated.
* Governance is part of business performance.

Culture is strengthened when leadership behavior consistently reflects these expectations.

---

### 70. Tone From the Middle

Middle management is equally important because managers translate executive expectations into daily operations.

Managers influence:

* Team behavior
* Productivity expectations
* Training participation
* Policy compliance
* Risk escalation

A strong governance culture requires alignment between senior leadership and operational management.

---

### 71. Incentives and AI Governance

Organizational incentives can influence AI behavior.

If employees are rewarded exclusively for:

* Speed
* Cost reduction
* AI adoption
* Automation

they may unintentionally bypass governance requirements.

Performance objectives should therefore consider appropriate risk and governance expectations.

---

### 72. AI Governance and Performance Management

Where appropriate, governance responsibilities may be incorporated into performance management.

Examples include:

* Training completion
* Policy compliance
* Security responsibilities
* Risk reporting
* Governance participation

Performance management should reinforce desired behavior without creating excessive administrative burden.

---

### 73. AI Governance and Psychological Safety

Employees should feel able to raise concerns about AI systems.

They should be able to question:

* AI outputs
* Security controls
* Data usage
* Model behavior
* Business assumptions
* Governance decisions

Questioning an AI system should be treated as a legitimate governance behavior.

---

### 74. Encouraging Constructive Challenge

A mature culture encourages employees to challenge assumptions respectfully.

For example:

> "The model produced this result, but do we have evidence that the result is reliable?"

or:

> "This AI service is convenient, but are we authorized to submit this data to it?"

Constructive challenge helps identify risks before they become incidents.

---

### 75. AI Governance and Ethical Awareness

Employees should understand that technically possible actions are not necessarily appropriate actions.

AI governance may require consideration of:

* Fairness
* Privacy
* Security
* Transparency
* Human impact
* Accountability

Ethical awareness should complement formal compliance requirements.

---

### 76. AI Governance and Human Oversight Culture

Human oversight should become a meaningful organizational practice where required.

Employees should understand:

* When human review is required
* What must be reviewed
* When AI output should be rejected
* When decisions must be escalated

Human oversight should not become a symbolic approval step.

---

### 77. Avoiding Automation Bias

**Automation bias** occurs when people place excessive trust in automated or AI-generated outputs.

AI literacy should teach employees to:

* Question unexpected results
* Validate important outputs
* Consider alternative information
* Recognize uncertainty
* Avoid blindly following AI recommendations

The appropriate level of verification should reflect the consequences of error.

---

### 78. AI Output Verification

Organizations should establish expectations for verifying AI-generated information.

Verification may be particularly important for:

* Legal information
* Financial information
* Security decisions
* Customer communications
* Technical configurations
* Regulatory interpretations
* Safety-related decisions

The higher the potential impact of an error, the stronger the verification requirements should generally be.

---

### 79. AI-Generated Content Awareness

Employees should understand that AI-generated content can contain:

* Errors
* Fabricated information
* Incorrect citations
* Biased conclusions
* Confidential information
* Intellectual-property concerns

Employees should apply appropriate review before using AI-generated content externally or for consequential purposes.

---

### 80. AI-Generated Code Awareness

Developers and technical teams should understand that AI-generated code may introduce:

* Security vulnerabilities
* Incorrect logic
* Dependency risks
* Licensing concerns
* Secrets exposure
* Poor architectural decisions

AI-generated code should therefore undergo appropriate human review and security testing.

---

### 81. AI-Generated Decision Awareness

Employees should understand that AI-generated recommendations can influence business decisions.

Examples include:

* Hiring
* Credit
* Fraud detection
* Customer prioritization
* Security investigations
* Resource allocation

Where decisions have significant consequences, additional governance and human oversight may be required.

---

### 82. AI Governance and Data Culture

AI governance culture is closely connected to organizational data culture.

Employees should understand:

* Data ownership
* Data classification
* Data quality
* Data sensitivity
* Data sharing
* Data retention

Poor data practices can create AI governance problems even when the AI technology itself is secure.

---

### 83. AI Governance and Security Culture

AI security should become part of the organization's broader security culture.

Employees should recognize that AI introduces additional attack surfaces and that familiar cybersecurity practices remain important.

Examples include:

* Identity security
* Access control
* Secure configuration
* Monitoring
* Incident reporting
* Data protection

AI security should therefore complement rather than replace established cybersecurity practices.

---

### 84. AI Governance and Privacy Culture

Privacy awareness should extend into everyday AI usage.

Employees should consider:

* What data they provide
* Why the AI system needs it
* Where the data goes
* Who can access it
* How long it may be retained

This helps make privacy principles operational.

---

### 85. AI Governance and Compliance Culture

Employees should understand that AI governance requirements may arise from:

* Law
* Regulation
* Contracts
* Industry requirements
* Organizational policies

Compliance should not be viewed simply as a legal department responsibility.

---

### 86. AI Governance and Third-Party Culture

Employees should understand that external AI services can create organizational risk.

Before using third-party AI services, employees may need to consider:

* Approval status
* Data handling
* Security
* Privacy
* Contractual requirements
* Business authorization

Third-party AI usage should align with procurement and vendor governance.

---

### 87. AI Governance and Change Culture

AI systems can change rapidly.

Employees should understand that changes such as:

* New models
* New datasets
* New integrations
* New capabilities
* New autonomous functions

may require reassessment.

AI governance should therefore become part of the organization's broader change-management culture.

---

### 88. AI Governance and Incident Culture

Organizations should encourage early reporting of AI-related problems.

An employee should not wait for complete certainty before reporting a potentially significant issue.

Early reporting enables:

* Investigation
* Containment
* Risk assessment
* Corrective action

The goal is to identify and manage problems before they become material incidents.

---

### 89. Near-Miss Reporting

AI governance should also capture near misses.

Examples include:

* An employee almost uploaded sensitive data to an unapproved AI service.
* An AI agent nearly executed an unauthorized action.
* A model generated an incorrect recommendation that was caught before use.

Near misses provide valuable information for improving controls and training.

---

### 90. Learning From AI Incidents

AI incidents should generate lessons for the organization.

Lessons may identify weaknesses in:

* Training
* Policies
* Technology
* Controls
* Monitoring
* Decision rights
* Vendor management

Incident lessons should be incorporated into governance improvements.

---

### 91. AI Governance Feedback Mechanisms

Employees should have mechanisms for providing feedback about AI governance.

Feedback may identify:

* Unclear policies
* Excessive approval requirements
* Missing approved tools
* Training gaps
* Confusing terminology
* Technical limitations

Feedback should be reviewed by appropriate governance functions.

---

### 92. Policy Usability

AI governance policies should be understandable to their intended audience.

A technically correct policy can still fail if employees cannot determine:

* What it requires
* What is prohibited
* When approval is required
* Who to contact

Usability is therefore an important component of governance effectiveness.

---

### 93. AI Governance Language

Governance documentation should use clear and consistent terminology.

Terms such as:

* AI system
* AI model
* AI service
* High-risk AI
* Sensitive data
* AI owner
* Human oversight

should have clearly understood meanings.

Consistent language improves both literacy and governance execution.

---

### 94. AI Governance Awareness Campaigns

Organizations may use targeted awareness campaigns to reinforce important issues.

Campaigns may address:

* Shadow AI
* Data protection
* Prompt security
* AI-generated phishing
* AI output verification
* Approved AI tools

Campaigns should focus on specific behaviors rather than generic messaging.

---

### 95. AI Literacy and Organizational Adoption

Successful AI adoption requires employees to understand both:

**How AI can create value**

and

**How AI can create risk**.

Training that focuses exclusively on risks may discourage useful adoption.

Training that focuses exclusively on productivity may encourage unsafe behavior.

Balanced literacy supports controlled innovation.

---

### 96. AI Governance and Innovation

Governance should enable responsible experimentation.

Organizations may create controlled environments where employees can:

* Experiment with AI
* Test new capabilities
* Evaluate tools
* Develop prototypes

without exposing production systems or sensitive information unnecessarily.

This allows innovation and governance to coexist.

---

### 97. AI Sandboxes

AI sandboxes can provide controlled environments for experimentation.

Controls may include:

* Synthetic data
* Restricted access
* Network isolation
* Logging
* Limited integrations
* No production credentials

Sandboxes can reduce the incentive to experiment using uncontrolled production environments.

---

### 98. AI Governance and Trust

Employees are more likely to follow governance requirements when they understand why the requirements exist.

Organizations should explain:

* What risk is being controlled
* Why the control matters
* What could happen if it is bypassed
* How employees can work safely within the requirements

Understanding builds trust and improves adoption.

---

### 99. AI Governance and Accountability Without Fear

Accountability should not be confused with creating a culture of fear.

A mature organization should distinguish between:

* Honest mistakes
* Good-faith decisions
* Deliberate policy violations
* Reckless behavior

Appropriate accountability encourages responsible behavior while preserving reporting and learning.

---

### 100. AI Governance Culture as an Organizational Capability

AI governance culture should ultimately become an organizational capability rather than a one-time awareness program.

A mature culture means employees naturally consider:

* Security
* Privacy
* Risk
* Human impact
* Governance
* Accountability

when using or developing AI.

AI governance becomes part of **how the organization operates**, rather than an additional process applied only when someone remembers to use it.

# 01.17 — AI Governance Culture and AI Literacy

## Part 4 — Measuring AI Literacy, Governance Culture and Continuous Improvement

### 101. Measuring AI Governance Culture

AI governance culture should be assessed rather than assumed.

Organizations should evaluate whether employees:

* Understand AI governance requirements
* Follow approved AI practices
* Recognize AI-related risks
* Report concerns
* Apply appropriate human judgment
* Understand their responsibilities

Culture measurement should combine quantitative and qualitative evidence.

---

### 102. AI Literacy Measurement

AI literacy can be measured through:

* Knowledge assessments
* Training completion
* Practical exercises
* Scenario-based assessments
* Certification
* Surveys
* Manager evaluations

Completion of training alone does not demonstrate that employees understand or apply AI governance requirements.

---

### 103. AI Knowledge Assessments

Knowledge assessments can test whether employees understand essential concepts.

Topics may include:

* AI risks
* Approved AI usage
* Data protection
* Security requirements
* Reporting
* Human oversight
* Organizational responsibilities

Questions should reflect the employee's role.

---

### 104. Scenario-Based AI Literacy Assessment

Scenario-based assessments can evaluate whether employees can apply governance principles.

For example:

> An employee wants to use a public AI service to analyze a confidential customer document.

The assessment can determine whether the employee knows:

* The data sensitivity issue
* The approved-tool requirement
* The escalation process
* The appropriate alternative

Practical assessment provides stronger evidence than simple memorization.

---

### 105. AI Literacy Completion Metrics

Organizations may track:

* Training completion rate
* Assessment completion
* Assessment scores
* Role-based training coverage
* Overdue training
* Refresher completion

These metrics provide visibility into workforce readiness.

---

### 106. AI Literacy Effectiveness Metrics

More meaningful indicators may include:

* Reduction in unauthorized AI usage
* Reduction in AI-related policy violations
* Increase in appropriate reporting
* Reduction in repeated mistakes
* Improved assessment results
* Improved incident response

These measures help determine whether training changes behavior.

---

### 107. AI Governance Culture Surveys

Employee surveys can assess perceptions such as:

* Confidence in AI policies
* Understanding of responsibilities
* Comfort reporting concerns
* Perceived leadership support
* Understanding of approved tools
* Confidence in escalation channels

Surveys should be interpreted alongside actual behavioral evidence.

---

### 108. AI Governance Culture Indicators

Potential cultural indicators include:

* Reporting frequency
* Near-miss reporting
* Policy compliance
* Exception rates
* Shadow AI usage
* Training engagement
* Governance participation
* Employee feedback

No single indicator should be treated as a complete measure of culture.

---

### 109. AI Governance Reporting Behavior

Reporting behavior can provide insight into organizational culture.

A sudden absence of reported concerns does not necessarily mean that risks have disappeared.

It may indicate:

* Lack of awareness
* Lack of trust
* Fear of consequences
* Ineffective reporting channels

Organizations should interpret reporting metrics carefully.

---

### 110. Shadow AI Measurement

Organizations should attempt to understand the level of unauthorized AI usage.

Possible indicators include:

* Unauthorized applications detected
* Unapproved AI accounts
* Unapproved browser extensions
* Data-loss prevention events
* Network traffic patterns

The objective should be to understand and reduce unmanaged risk rather than simply punish employees.

---

### 111. AI Governance Compliance Metrics

Organizations may monitor:

* Percentage of AI systems with identified owners
* Percentage with completed risk assessments
* Percentage using approved providers
* Training coverage
* Policy exceptions
* Overdue governance activities

These metrics can indicate whether governance requirements are being operationalized.

---

### 112. AI Governance Maturity Assessment

AI literacy and culture can be assessed as part of the broader AI governance maturity model.

A maturity assessment may evaluate:

* Leadership
* Workforce awareness
* Role-based competence
* Reporting culture
* Governance behavior
* Decision-making
* Continuous improvement

This provides a more comprehensive view of organizational capability.

---

### 113. AI Governance Culture Maturity

A culture maturity model may progress through:

**Level 1 — Unaware**

→ Employees have limited understanding of AI governance.

**Level 2 — Aware**

→ Basic policies and awareness exist.

**Level 3 — Defined**

→ Role-based responsibilities and training are established.

**Level 4 — Embedded**

→ Governance behavior is integrated into business processes.

**Level 5 — Adaptive**

→ Culture continuously evolves based on AI risks, incidents, and organizational learning.

---

### 114. AI Literacy Maturity

AI literacy can similarly progress through:

**Basic Awareness**

→ Employees understand fundamental concepts.

**Functional Literacy**

→ Employees can safely use approved AI tools.

**Role-Based Literacy**

→ Employees understand governance responsibilities relevant to their roles.

**Advanced Competence**

→ Specialists can manage complex AI risks.

**Strategic Literacy**

→ Leaders can make informed enterprise AI decisions.

---

### 115. AI Governance Culture Benchmarking

Organizations may benchmark their AI governance culture against:

* Internal business units
* Previous assessment periods
* Industry practices
* Governance maturity frameworks

Benchmarking should focus on meaningful capability differences rather than superficial comparisons.

---

### 116. AI Literacy Gap Analysis

Assessment results should be used to identify knowledge gaps.

Examples include:

* Developers lack AI security knowledge.
* Managers do not understand risk escalation.
* Employees misunderstand approved AI tools.
* Executives lack understanding of emerging AI risks.

Training investments should prioritize material gaps.

---

### 117. Targeted Remediation

Training remediation should be specific to the identified problem.

For example:

**Problem**

→ Employees frequently submit sensitive information to AI services.

**Response**

→ Data-handling training, approved-tool guidance, technical controls, and targeted awareness.

This is more effective than simply assigning another generic AI awareness course.

---

### 118. AI Governance Refresher Training

AI governance knowledge should be periodically refreshed.

Refresher training may be triggered by:

* Policy changes
* New AI technologies
* Major incidents
* Regulatory changes
* New threats
* Changes in organizational responsibilities

Training frequency should reflect risk and the rate of change.

---

### 119. Event-Driven AI Training

Training does not always need to follow a fixed annual cycle.

Organizations may provide targeted training following:

* A security incident
* A privacy incident
* A significant policy violation
* Introduction of a new AI platform
* Major regulatory change
* Emergence of a new AI threat

Event-driven learning can address risks while they are highly relevant.

---

### 120. Continuous AI Awareness

AI governance awareness should be reinforced continuously.

Methods may include:

* Short awareness messages
* Security alerts
* AI governance newsletters
* Practical examples
* Tool notifications
* Manager communications

Continuous reinforcement helps prevent knowledge decay.

---

### 121. AI Governance Communications During Change

When AI policies or technologies change, communications should explain:

* What changed
* Why it changed
* Who is affected
* What actions are required
* Where employees can obtain assistance

Change communication should occur before or alongside implementation.

---

### 122. AI Governance Knowledge Retention

Organizations should consider whether employees retain important governance knowledge.

Retention can be supported through:

* Repetition
* Practical exercises
* Job aids
* Scenario training
* Just-in-time guidance
* Periodic assessments

The objective is practical recall during real situations.

---

### 123. AI Governance Job Aids

Job aids can provide concise guidance for employees.

Examples include:

* AI use checklists
* Data handling decision trees
* AI approval guides
* Incident reporting cards
* Vendor evaluation checklists
* AI output verification guides

Job aids should be easily accessible when employees need them.

---

### 124. AI Governance Decision Trees

Decision trees can help employees determine whether an AI activity requires additional review.

For example:

**Does the AI system process sensitive information?**

→ Yes → Additional controls/review.

**Does the AI make consequential decisions?**

→ Yes → Enhanced governance.

**Is the AI service approved?**

→ No → Procurement/governance review.

Decision tools can make complex requirements easier to apply.

---

### 125. AI Governance Help and Support

Employees should have accessible channels for AI governance questions.

Support may be provided through:

* AI governance teams
* Security teams
* Privacy teams
* Service desks
* Knowledge portals
* AI champions

Employees should have a clear place to ask questions before taking risky actions.

---

### 126. AI Governance Consultation

Organizations should encourage employees to seek guidance before deploying unfamiliar AI capabilities.

Consultation is particularly important when:

* Data is sensitive
* AI is externally accessible
* The AI makes consequential decisions
* Autonomous actions are possible
* Regulatory requirements may apply

Early consultation can prevent costly remediation later.

---

### 127. AI Governance and Organizational Learning

AI governance should incorporate lessons from:

* Incidents
* Near misses
* Audits
* Assessments
* Employee feedback
* Regulatory developments
* Technology changes

Learning should lead to concrete improvements in:

* Policies
* Training
* Controls
* Processes
* Decision rights

---

### 128. Lessons-Learned Repository

Organizations may maintain a structured repository of AI governance lessons.

Records may capture:

* What happened
* Why it happened
* Impact
* Root cause
* Governance weakness
* Corrective action
* Training implications

The repository can support organizational learning and prevent recurrence.

---

### 129. AI Governance Culture and Incident Response

Incident response should include a cultural dimension.

After an incident, organizations should evaluate:

* Did employees know what to do?
* Was reporting timely?
* Were escalation paths understood?
* Were decision rights clear?
* Did employees feel safe reporting the issue?

This can identify weaknesses beyond technical controls.

---

### 130. AI Governance Culture and Audit Findings

Audit findings can provide evidence about governance culture.

Repeated findings may indicate:

* Poor awareness
* Weak accountability
* Ineffective training
* Unrealistic policies
* Weak management oversight

Repeated findings should therefore be treated as potential cultural signals.

---

### 131. AI Governance Culture and Regulatory Expectations

Organizations should consider whether their workforce understands applicable AI governance obligations.

Training may need to address requirements related to:

* Security
* Privacy
* Transparency
* Human oversight
* Recordkeeping
* Risk management

The specific requirements depend on jurisdiction, industry, and AI use case.

---

### 132. AI Literacy and Third-Party Personnel

AI literacy should not necessarily be limited to employees.

Depending on risk, relevant third parties may require awareness of:

* Security requirements
* Data handling
* Approved AI usage
* Incident reporting
* Contractual AI obligations

Third-party personnel can interact directly with organizational AI environments.

---

### 133. AI Literacy for Contractors

Contractors should receive appropriate governance guidance when they:

* Access AI systems
* Develop AI applications
* Process organizational data
* Operate AI infrastructure
* Support AI governance activities

Training requirements should reflect their actual access and responsibilities.

---

### 134. AI Governance Culture Across the Supply Chain

Organizations should consider AI governance culture across important suppliers and partners.

Third-party expectations may include:

* Security awareness
* Data protection
* AI usage restrictions
* Incident reporting
* Regulatory compliance

Contracts can establish these expectations, while assurance activities can evaluate adherence.

---

### 135. Measuring Leadership Engagement

Leadership engagement can be evaluated through:

* Governance meeting participation
* AI risk discussions
* Training participation
* Policy sponsorship
* Risk escalation decisions
* Investment in governance capabilities

Leadership engagement should be visible through actions rather than statements alone.

---

### 136. Measuring Governance Participation

Organizations may measure participation in:

* AI governance committees
* Risk assessments
* Training
* Tabletop exercises
* Communities of practice
* Policy reviews

Participation metrics can identify areas where governance engagement is weak.

---

### 137. AI Governance Culture and Trust

Trust should exist in multiple directions.

Employees should trust that:

* Governance requirements are reasonable.
* Reporting concerns is safe.
* Policies are consistently applied.
* Leadership takes risks seriously.

Management should trust that:

* Employees understand their responsibilities.
* Controls are being followed.
* Concerns are escalated appropriately.

This mutual trust supports effective governance.

---

### 138. Avoiding Governance Fatigue

Excessive training, policies, approvals, and awareness campaigns can produce governance fatigue.

Symptoms may include:

* Employees ignoring communications
* Repeated training without behavioral improvement
* Excessive exceptions
* Low engagement

Organizations should prioritize high-value governance activities and reduce unnecessary complexity.

---

### 139. AI Governance Simplicity

Good governance should be understandable enough to apply.

Organizations should aim for:

* Clear rules
* Simple decision paths
* Practical examples
* Accessible guidance
* Risk-based requirements

Complexity should be introduced where it manages meaningful risk, not simply because AI governance is a complex subject.

---

### 140. AI Governance Culture and Business Agility

A mature governance culture supports controlled innovation.

Employees should know:

* What they can do independently
* What requires approval
* What is prohibited
* Where to obtain assistance
* How to safely experiment

Clear boundaries can increase confidence and reduce unnecessary hesitation.

---

### 141. AI Governance Culture and Innovation Incentives

Innovation programs should incorporate governance expectations from the beginning.

AI innovation teams should consider:

* Security
* Privacy
* Risk
* Compliance
* Data governance

during ideation rather than only before production deployment.

This supports **governance by design**.

---

### 142. AI Literacy and Secure-by-Design

AI literacy should reinforce secure-by-design principles.

Developers and AI teams should understand that security should be incorporated during:

* Architecture
* Data preparation
* Development
* Testing
* Deployment

Security should not be treated solely as a post-development review.

---

### 143. AI Literacy and Privacy-by-Design

Similarly, privacy considerations should be introduced early.

AI teams should understand the importance of:

* Data minimization
* Purpose limitation
* Appropriate access
* Retention
* Privacy assessment

Early consideration reduces the likelihood of expensive redesign.

---

### 144. AI Governance Culture and Continuous Improvement

Culture should evolve based on evidence.

Organizations should continuously evaluate:

* What employees understand
* What employees actually do
* Where incidents occur
* Where policies fail
* Where governance creates unnecessary friction

Improvement should address both control weaknesses and usability problems.

---

### 145. AI Governance Culture Improvement Cycle

A continuous improvement cycle may be:

**Assess**

→ Understand current culture and literacy.

**Identify**

→ Determine gaps.

**Improve**

→ Implement training, communication, controls, or process changes.

**Measure**

→ Evaluate effectiveness.

**Learn**

→ Incorporate lessons.

**Repeat**

→ Continue improving.

Governance ensures the program remains current and aligned with organizational needs.

* Coverage

* Behavioral outcomes

* Management engagement

The program should be improved based on evidence.

### 150. AI Governance Culture and AI Literacy as Strategic Capabilities

AI governance culture and AI literacy ultimately extend beyond awareness and training.

A mature organization develops a workforce that can:

* Understand AI capabilities and limitations
* Recognize AI security, privacy, and operational risks
* Apply organizational governance requirements
* Make informed decisions
* Challenge inappropriate AI use
* Escalate material concerns
* Protect organizational information
* Use AI responsibly
* Support innovation within defined boundaries

AI literacy provides the **knowledge and competence**, while AI governance culture provides the **behavioral environment** in which that knowledge is consistently applied.

Together, they create an organizational capability in which **responsible, secure, risk-aware, and accountable AI behavior becomes part of normal business operations rather than an activity performed only by specialized governance teams**.
