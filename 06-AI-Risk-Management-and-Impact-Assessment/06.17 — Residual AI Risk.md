# 06.17 — AI Risk Treatment, Mitigation, Acceptance, and Escalation

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Risk Treatment

### 1. Introduction

Risk assessment tells the organization:

> **"What could go wrong, how serious is it, and how much risk remains?"**

Risk treatment answers the next question:

> **"What are we going to do about it?"**

For AI governance, risk treatment is particularly important because some AI risks cannot simply be eliminated through a single technical control.

AI risk treatment may require a combination of:

* Technical controls.
* Governance controls.
* Process controls.
* Human oversight.
* Contractual controls.
* Data controls.
* Monitoring.
* Training.
* Restrictions on use.
* Or, in some cases, deciding **not to use the AI system at all**.

The fundamental relationship is:

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Risk Assessment
   ↓
Residual Risk
   ↓
Risk Appetite
   ↓
Risk Treatment Decision
   ↓
Implementation
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Reassessment
```

---

# 2. What Is Risk Treatment?

**Risk treatment** is the process of selecting and implementing measures to modify risk.

In simple terms:

> **Risk assessment tells you where you are. Risk treatment determines what you do next.**

For example:

```text
Risk = High
       ↓
Evaluate Options
       ↓
Reduce
Avoid
Transfer
Accept
       ↓
Select Treatment
       ↓
Implement
       ↓
Reassess
```

---

# 3. Risk Treatment Is Not the Same as Risk Mitigation

These terms are often used interchangeably, but they can have different meanings.

### Risk treatment

The broader decision-making process for addressing risk.

### Risk mitigation

Actions intended to reduce risk.

Therefore:

> **Mitigation is one form of risk treatment.**

---

# 4. The Four Traditional Treatment Options

A common risk-management model identifies four broad responses:

1. **Avoid**
2. **Reduce / Modify**
3. **Transfer / Share**
4. **Accept / Retain**

These options can be applied individually or in combination.

---

# 5. Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance means:

> **Choosing not to undertake the activity that creates the risk.**

For AI:

```text
AI Use Case
    ↓
Risk Assessment
    ↓
Risk Unacceptable
    ↓
Do Not Deploy
```

Example:

> An organization considers an AI system for a high-impact decision, but testing shows that the system cannot meet required fairness and accuracy expectations.

Management may decide:

> **Do not deploy the system.**

---

# 6. Avoidance Does Not Mean Failure

A common misconception is:

> "If GRC recommends not deploying AI, GRC is blocking innovation."

That is incorrect.

If the risk cannot be brought within acceptable boundaries, avoiding the use case can be a legitimate risk-management decision.

A mature GRC professional should be able to say:

> **"The organization should not proceed until the identified risk can be appropriately controlled."**

---

# 7. Risk Reduction

Risk reduction means implementing controls to decrease:

* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Or both.

Example:

```text
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Security Controls
     ↓
Human Review
     ↓
Monitoring
     ↓
Residual Risk
```

This is often the most common AI risk treatment.

---

# 8. Examples of AI Risk Reduction

For an AI system handling sensitive information:

### Preventive controls

* Access control.
* Data minimization.
* Input filtering.
* Encryption.
* Secure architecture.

### Detective controls

* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* DLP alerts.
* Anomaly detection.

### Corrective controls

* Model rollback.
* Account suspension.
* Data deletion.
* Incident response.

---

# 9. Risk Transfer or Sharing

Risk transfer means shifting certain consequences or responsibilities to another party.

Examples:

* Insurance.
* Contractual arrangements.
* Vendor obligations.
* Indemnification.
* Service-level agreements.

However:

> **Transferring contractual responsibility does not automatically transfer regulatory, ethical or organizational accountability.**

---

# 10. AI Vendor Example

Suppose an organization uses an external AI provider.

The contract could require:

* Security controls.
* Incident notification.
* Data protection.
* Audit rights.
* Business continuity.
* Subprocessor controls.
* Defined service levels.

These measures can reduce or share certain risks.

But the organization should not assume:

> "The vendor is responsible, so we have no risk."

---

# 11. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance means:

> **A properly authorized decision to retain the residual risk.**

Acceptance does not mean:

> "Nobody wants to deal with it."

It means:

> "The organization understands the residual exposure and has formally decided that it is acceptable under defined authority."

---

# 12. Risk Acceptance Conditions

A good acceptance decision should identify:

* The risk.
* Residual risk rating.
* Business justification.
* Potential consequences.
* Existing controls.
* Compensating controls.
* Risk owner.
* Approval authority.
* Review date.
* Expiration date where appropriate.

---

# 13. Risk Acceptance Example

Suppose:

```text
Inherent Risk = 20
Residual Risk = 8
Risk Appetite  = 10
```

If the methodology allows the business owner to accept risk at that level:

> The risk may be formally accepted with continued monitoring.

The decision should still be documented.

---

# 14. Risk Acceptance Does Not Mean Risk Disappears

This is a critical GRC concept.

```text
Risk Accepted
      ↓
Risk Still Exists
      ↓
Monitoring Continues
      ↓
Reassessment Required
```

Acceptance changes the **decision about the risk**, not the underlying risk itself.

---

# 15. Risk Appetite

Risk treatment should be connected to **risk appetite**.

Risk appetite represents the level and type of risk an organization is willing to pursue or retain in achieving its objectives.

Example:

```text
Residual Risk = 8
Risk Appetite = 10
```

Potentially acceptable.

But:

```text
Residual Risk = 18
Risk Appetite = 10
```

The exposure requires further treatment or escalation.

---

# 16. Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance is generally more specific.

It describes the acceptable variation around objectives or defined risk levels.

For example:

> The organization may tolerate a defined level of model error within a specified operational boundary.

This can provide more actionable thresholds than a broad appetite statement.

---

# 17. Risk Capacity

**Risk capacity** is the maximum level of risk the organization can withstand before its ability to achieve objectives is materially threatened.

Conceptually:

```text
Risk Appetite
     ↓
Preferred Exposure
     ↓
Risk Tolerance
     ↓
Risk Capacity
```

These concepts should not be treated as identical.

---

# 18. Treatment Decision Framework

A practical decision process is:

```text
Is the risk within appetite?
          ↓
       Yes
          ↓
 Accept / Monitor

          No
          ↓
Can controls reduce it?
          ↓
       Yes
          ↓
Implement Treatment
          ↓
Reassess

          No
          ↓
Can the activity be avoided?
          ↓
       Yes → Avoid

       No
          ↓
Escalate / Exception / Formal Acceptance
```

---

# 19. Risk Treatment Hierarchy

When addressing AI risk, consider controls in an appropriate order.

### 1. Eliminate the risk

Do not perform the risky activity.

### 2. Reduce the risk

Change the architecture, process or model.

### 3. Control the risk

Implement safeguards.

### 4. Detect the risk

Monitor for harmful behavior.

### 5. Respond to the risk

Contain and remediate incidents.

This provides layered protection.

---

# 20. Prevention Is Usually Better Than Detection

For example:

### Weak approach

Allow confidential information into an AI system and detect leakage afterward.

### Stronger approach

Prevent confidential information from entering the system in the first place.

```text
Sensitive Data
     ↓
DLP / Classification
     ↓
BLOCK
```

This is generally stronger than:

```text
Sensitive Data
     ↓
AI System
     ↓
Leakage
     ↓
Detection
```

---

# 21. Defense in Depth

AI governance should avoid relying on a single control.

Example:

```text
Identity
   ↓
Authorization
   ↓
Data Filtering
   ↓
Prompt Protection
   ↓
Model Controls
   ↓
Output Filtering
   ↓
Human Review
   ↓
Monitoring
```

If one control fails, others may reduce the consequences.

---

# 22. Control Selection Should Be Risk-Based

Not every AI system needs the same controls.

For example:

### Low-risk AI

Internal meeting summarization.

Potential controls:

* Approved tool.
* Basic data restrictions.
* User awareness.

### High-risk AI

AI supporting significant customer decisions.

Potential controls:

* Formal validation.
* Bias assessment.
* Human oversight.
* Monitoring.
* Explainability.
* Incident management.
* Independent review.

---

# 23. Risk Treatment Must Address the Actual Risk

Suppose the risk is:

> Unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information.

Implementing:

> Employee AI training

may help, but it may not be sufficient.

Additional controls could include:

* DLP.
* Access control.
* Data classification.
* Prompt filtering.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.

The treatment should directly address the risk mechanism.

---

# 24. Control-to-Risk Mapping

A useful model is:

```text
Risk
 ↓
Cause
 ↓
Control Objective
 ↓
Control
 ↓
Evidence
 ↓
Effectiveness
 ↓
Residual Risk
```

Example:

```text
Risk:
Sensitive data disclosure

Cause:
Unauthorized prompt submission

Control Objective:
Prevent sensitive information from
being submitted to unauthorized AI tools

Controls:
DLP + Access Control + Policy
```

# 26. Human Oversight as a Treatment

Human oversight can reduce AI risk when properly designed.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Approve / Reject / Escalate
```

But merely having a person present does not automatically make the control effective.

---

# 27. Meaningful Human Oversight

Effective human oversight should consider:

* Reviewer expertise.
* Reviewer authority.
* Access to relevant information.
* Ability to challenge the AI.
* Ability to override.
* Adequate review time.
* Clear escalation procedures.

---

# 28. Human-in-the-Loop Is Not a Magic Control

A common mistake is:

> "There is a human in the loop, therefore the risk is controlled."

Not necessarily.

If the reviewer:

* Cannot understand the output.
* Cannot challenge the system.
* Has too many decisions to review.
* Automatically trusts the AI.

then the control may provide limited risk reduction.

---

# 29. Automation Bias Treatment

To reduce automation bias:

* Train reviewers.
* Require independent reasoning.
* Monitor override rates.
* Introduce mandatory review criteria.
* Avoid presenting AI recommendations as unquestionably correct.

The goal is meaningful human judgment.

---

# 32. Security Treatment

# 33. Privacy Treatment

Privacy risk can be reduced through:

* Data minimization.

* Access restrictions.
* Retention limits.

* Privacy testing.
* Data-processing controls.

---

# 34. Fairness Treatment

Where fairness is relevant:

* Assess representative data.
* Test outcomes across relevant groups.
* Monitor disparity.
* Review decision thresholds.
* Establish escalation criteria.
* Provide appropriate human review or appeal mechanisms.

The exact controls depend on the use case and applicable requirements.

---

# 35. Transparency Treatment

Depending on the use case, treatment may include:

* Documentation.
* Model cards.
* User notifications.
* Decision explanations.
* AI interaction disclosure.
* Record keeping.

Transparency requirements should be proportionate to the risk and applicable obligations.

---

# 36. Third-Party Treatment

For vendor-provided AI, treatment may include:

---

# 37. Contractual Controls

An AI vendor agreement may address:

* Data ownership.
* Data usage.
* Data retention.
* Subprocessors.
* Security requirements.
* Incident reporting.
* Regulatory cooperation.
* Service availability.
* Audit rights.
* Termination.

Contracts are useful controls but should complement—not replace—technical and governance safeguards.

---

# 38. Compensating Controls

Sometimes the preferred control cannot be implemented.

Example:

> The AI vendor cannot provide sufficient model transparency.

A compensating approach might include:

* Restricting the AI to lower-risk use cases.
* Requiring human review.
* Limiting sensitive data.
* Increasing monitoring.
* Prohibiting autonomous decisions.

The residual risk must then be reassessed.

---

# 39. Risk Treatment and Architecture

Risk treatment can influence architecture.

Example:

### Architecture A

Public AI API.

### Architecture B

Enterprise-controlled AI gateway.

### Architecture C

Private model environment.

If Architecture A creates unacceptable data exposure, the organization might redesign the solution rather than simply accepting the risk.

This demonstrates:

> **Security and GRC should influence architecture decisions early.**

# 40. Risk Treatment and AI Lifecycle

# 41. Treatment During Design

At design stage:

* Define approved use.
* Identify prohibited uses.
* Establish security requirements.
* Define data boundaries.
* Determine human oversight.
* Define monitoring.

Early treatment is usually cheaper than remediation after deployment.

---

# 42. Treatment During Development

Development controls may include:

* Secure coding.
* Data validation.
* Testing.
* Version control.
* Access control.
* Model evaluation.

---

# 43. Treatment Before Deployment

Before production:

* Complete risk assessment.
* Validate controls.
* Confirm ownership.
* Complete required approvals.
* Establish monitoring.
* Define incident procedures.

A high-risk AI system should not simply move to production because development is complete.

---

# 44. Treatment During Operations

Operational controls may include:

* Continuous monitoring.
* KRI tracking.
* Model performance monitoring.
* Security monitoring.
* Incident management.
* Periodic reassessment.

---

# 45. Treatment During Change

Material changes may require:

* New risk assessment.
* New testing.
* New approval.
* Updated documentation.
* Updated controls.

Example:

```text
Original AI
     ↓
New Model
     ↓
New Data
     ↓
New Population
     ↓
Risk Reassessment
```

---

# 46. Treatment After an Incident

After a significant AI incident:

1. Contain.
2. Investigate.
3. Determine root cause.
4. Correct the problem.
5. Reassess risk.
6. Review controls.
7. Update governance.
8. Determine whether deployment should continue.

---

# 47. Risk Treatment and Incident Response

Risk management and incident response are closely connected.

```text
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Preventive Controls
      ↓
Incident
      ↓
Response
      ↓
Recovery
      ↓
Lessons Learned
      ↓
Risk Reassessment
```

The incident should produce new risk information.

---

# 48. Treatment Effectiveness

A treatment is not successful simply because it was implemented.

Ask:

> Did the control actually reduce risk?

For example:

```text
Before:
Risk = 20

Control implemented

After:
Risk = 19
```

If the organization's objective was to reduce the risk significantly, the treatment may be inadequate.

---

# 49. Treatment Validation

Evidence can include:

* Test results.
* Audit results.
* Monitoring data.
* Control effectiveness assessments.
* Incident statistics.
* Model performance.
* User feedback.

Treatment should be evidence-based.

---

# 50. Residual Risk After Treatment

After treatment:

```text
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Treatment
     ↓
Residual Risk
```

The residual risk must then be evaluated again.

If still above appetite:

```text
Residual Risk > Appetite
          ↓
Additional Treatment
OR
Escalation
OR
Avoidance
OR
Authorized Acceptance
```

---

# 51. Treatment Priority

Not every risk can be fixed simultaneously.

Prioritize based on:

* Severity.
* Likelihood.
* Human impact.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Risk velocity.
* Scale.
* Control weakness.
* Business criticality.

---

# 53. Treatment Owner

Every treatment action should have a clear owner.

Avoid:

> "IT will fix."

Prefer:

> "AI Platform Owner is accountable for implementing the gateway controls, with Security Engineering responsible for technical validation."

Clear ownership improves accountability.

---

# 54. Treatment Deadline

High-risk treatment should normally have an appropriate target date.

Example:

```text
Risk Identified
      ↓
Treatment Assigned
      ↓
Due Date
      ↓
Validation
      ↓
Closure / Reassessment
```

Overdue high-risk actions should be visible to management.

---

# 55. Treatment Dependencies

Some treatments depend on:

* Vendor changes.
* Architecture changes.
* Budget.
* Legal review.
* Procurement.
* Data engineering.
* Model redevelopment.

Dependencies should be documented because they can affect remediation timelines.

---

# 56. Risk Escalation

Escalation is required when risk exceeds the authority or appetite of the responsible owner.

Example:

```text
Business Owner
      ↓
Risk exceeds authority
      ↓
Senior Management
      ↓
Risk Committee
      ↓
Executive Decision
```

The exact hierarchy depends on organizational governance.

---

# 57. Escalation Is Not Punishment

Escalation should be viewed as:

> **Moving a decision to the appropriate authority.**

It does not necessarily mean someone failed.

For example:

> A business owner may identify a risk that is legitimately beyond their delegated acceptance authority.

Escalation is then the correct governance mechanism.

---

# 58. Risk Treatment Decision Record

For significant AI risks, retain a decision record showing:

* Risk.
* Assessment.
* Options considered.
* Recommended treatment.
* Decision.
* Decision maker.
* Date.
* Conditions.
* Review requirements.

This creates an audit trail.

# 59. Part 1 Core Principles

Remember:

1. Risk assessment identifies and evaluates exposure.
2. Risk treatment determines what to do about it.
3. Mitigation is one form of treatment.
4. Traditional treatment options include avoid, reduce, transfer/share and accept.
5. Avoidance can be the correct governance decision.
6. Risk reduction modifies likelihood, impact or both.
7. Risk transfer does not eliminate organizational accountability.
8. Risk acceptance requires proper authorization.
9. Accepted risk still exists.
10. Risk appetite guides treatment decisions.
11. Risk tolerance provides more specific boundaries.
12. Risk capacity represents the maximum sustainable exposure.
13. Controls should address the actual risk mechanism.
14. Prevention is generally preferable to relying solely on detection.
15. Defense in depth reduces dependence on a single control.
16. AI controls should be proportionate to risk.
17. Human oversight must be meaningful.
18. Human-in-the-loop alone does not guarantee effective governance.
19. AI risk treatment should cover data, model, security, privacy, human and governance dimensions.
20. Third-party AI requires vendor-specific treatment.
21. Contracts can reduce or share certain risks but do not eliminate accountability.
22. Compensating controls can address unavoidable control gaps.
23. Risk treatment should begin early in the AI lifecycle.
24. Material changes should trigger reassessment.
25. Incidents should feed new evidence into risk assessment.
26. Treatment effectiveness must be tested.
27. Residual risk must be reassessed after treatment.
28. Treatment actions need owners and timelines.
29. Risks above appetite require additional action or authorized acceptance.
30. Escalation moves decisions to the appropriate authority.

# 61. Senior GRC Anchor

> **"Once an AI risk has been assessed, I move from risk identification to risk treatment by determining whether the exposure is within the organization's approved risk appetite and whether it can be appropriately reduced. I consider the available treatment options—avoidance, risk reduction, risk sharing or transfer, and formal acceptance—and select the approach based on the nature and severity of the risk. For AI, I look beyond traditional cybersecurity controls and consider data quality, privacy, fairness, model performance, human oversight, third-party dependencies, monitoring and governance. I make sure the selected controls address the actual risk mechanism and that their effectiveness can be demonstrated with evidence. If residual risk remains above appetite or exceeds the owner's authority, I escalate it to the appropriate level rather than allowing informal acceptance. Finally, I assign accountable owners, define treatment targets and monitoring indicators, and reassess the risk when controls are implemented, incidents occur or the AI system materially changes."**
