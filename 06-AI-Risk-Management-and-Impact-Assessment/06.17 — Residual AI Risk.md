# 06.17 — AI Risk Treatment, Mitigation, Acceptance, and Escalation

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Risk Treatment

### 1. Introduction

Risk assessment tells the organization:

> **"What could go wrong, how serious is it, and how much risk remains?"**

Risk treatment answers the next question:

> **"What are we going to do about it?"**

For AI governance, risk treatment is particularly important because some AI risks cannot simply be eliminated through a single technical control.

AI risk treatment may require a combination of:

* Technical controls.
* Governance controls.
* Process controls.
* Human oversight.
* Contractual controls.
* Data controls.
* Monitoring.
* Training.
* Restrictions on use.
* Or, in some cases, deciding **not to use the AI system at all**.

The fundamental relationship is:

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Risk Assessment
   ↓
Residual Risk
   ↓
Risk Appetite
   ↓
Risk Treatment Decision
   ↓
Implementation
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Reassessment
```

---

# 2. What Is Risk Treatment?

**Risk treatment** is the process of selecting and implementing measures to modify risk.

In simple terms:

> **Risk assessment tells you where you are. Risk treatment determines what you do next.**

For example:

```text
Risk = High
       ↓
Evaluate Options
       ↓
Reduce
Avoid
Transfer
Accept
       ↓
Select Treatment
       ↓
Implement
       ↓
Reassess
```

---

# 3. Risk Treatment Is Not the Same as Risk Mitigation

These terms are often used interchangeably, but they can have different meanings.

### Risk treatment

The broader decision-making process for addressing risk.

### Risk mitigation

Actions intended to reduce risk.

Therefore:

> **Mitigation is one form of risk treatment.**

---

# 4. The Four Traditional Treatment Options

A common risk-management model identifies four broad responses:

1. **Avoid**
2. **Reduce / Modify**
3. **Transfer / Share**
4. **Accept / Retain**

These options can be applied individually or in combination.

---

# 5. Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance means:

> **Choosing not to undertake the activity that creates the risk.**

For AI:

```text
AI Use Case
    ↓
Risk Assessment
    ↓
Risk Unacceptable
    ↓
Do Not Deploy
```

Example:

> An organization considers an AI system for a high-impact decision, but testing shows that the system cannot meet required fairness and accuracy expectations.

Management may decide:

> **Do not deploy the system.**

---

# 6. Avoidance Does Not Mean Failure

A common misconception is:

> "If GRC recommends not deploying AI, GRC is blocking innovation."

That is incorrect.

If the risk cannot be brought within acceptable boundaries, avoiding the use case can be a legitimate risk-management decision.

A mature GRC professional should be able to say:

> **"The organization should not proceed until the identified risk can be appropriately controlled."**

---

# 7. Risk Reduction

Risk reduction means implementing controls to decrease:

* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Or both.

Example:

```text
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Security Controls
     ↓
Human Review
     ↓
Monitoring
     ↓
Residual Risk
```

This is often the most common AI risk treatment.

---

# 8. Examples of AI Risk Reduction

For an AI system handling sensitive information:

### Preventive controls

* Access control.
* Data minimization.
* Input filtering.
* Encryption.
* Secure architecture.

### Detective controls

* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* DLP alerts.
* Anomaly detection.

### Corrective controls

* Model rollback.
* Account suspension.
* Data deletion.
* Incident response.

---

# 9. Risk Transfer or Sharing

Risk transfer means shifting certain consequences or responsibilities to another party.

Examples:

* Insurance.
* Contractual arrangements.
* Vendor obligations.
* Indemnification.
* Service-level agreements.

However:

> **Transferring contractual responsibility does not automatically transfer regulatory, ethical or organizational accountability.**

---

# 10. AI Vendor Example

Suppose an organization uses an external AI provider.

The contract could require:

* Security controls.
* Incident notification.
* Data protection.
* Audit rights.
* Business continuity.
* Subprocessor controls.
* Defined service levels.

These measures can reduce or share certain risks.

But the organization should not assume:

> "The vendor is responsible, so we have no risk."

---

# 11. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance means:

> **A properly authorized decision to retain the residual risk.**

Acceptance does not mean:

> "Nobody wants to deal with it."

It means:

> "The organization understands the residual exposure and has formally decided that it is acceptable under defined authority."

---

# 12. Risk Acceptance Conditions

A good acceptance decision should identify:

* The risk.
* Residual risk rating.
* Business justification.
* Potential consequences.
* Existing controls.
* Compensating controls.
* Risk owner.
* Approval authority.
* Review date.
* Expiration date where appropriate.

---

# 13. Risk Acceptance Example

Suppose:

```text
Inherent Risk = 20
Residual Risk = 8
Risk Appetite  = 10
```

If the methodology allows the business owner to accept risk at that level:

> The risk may be formally accepted with continued monitoring.

The decision should still be documented.

---

# 14. Risk Acceptance Does Not Mean Risk Disappears

This is a critical GRC concept.

```text
Risk Accepted
      ↓
Risk Still Exists
      ↓
Monitoring Continues
      ↓
Reassessment Required
```

Acceptance changes the **decision about the risk**, not the underlying risk itself.

---

# 15. Risk Appetite

Risk treatment should be connected to **risk appetite**.

Risk appetite represents the level and type of risk an organization is willing to pursue or retain in achieving its objectives.

Example:

```text
Residual Risk = 8
Risk Appetite = 10
```

Potentially acceptable.

But:

```text
Residual Risk = 18
Risk Appetite = 10
```

The exposure requires further treatment or escalation.

---

# 16. Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance is generally more specific.

It describes the acceptable variation around objectives or defined risk levels.

For example:

> The organization may tolerate a defined level of model error within a specified operational boundary.

This can provide more actionable thresholds than a broad appetite statement.

---

# 17. Risk Capacity

**Risk capacity** is the maximum level of risk the organization can withstand before its ability to achieve objectives is materially threatened.

Conceptually:

```text
Risk Appetite
     ↓
Preferred Exposure
     ↓
Risk Tolerance
     ↓
Risk Capacity
```

These concepts should not be treated as identical.

---

# 18. Treatment Decision Framework

A practical decision process is:

```text
Is the risk within appetite?
          ↓
       Yes
          ↓
 Accept / Monitor

          No
          ↓
Can controls reduce it?
          ↓
       Yes
          ↓
Implement Treatment
          ↓
Reassess

          No
          ↓
Can the activity be avoided?
          ↓
       Yes → Avoid

       No
          ↓
Escalate / Exception / Formal Acceptance
```

---

# 19. Risk Treatment Hierarchy

When addressing AI risk, consider controls in an appropriate order.

### 1. Eliminate the risk

Do not perform the risky activity.

### 2. Reduce the risk

Change the architecture, process or model.

### 3. Control the risk

Implement safeguards.

### 4. Detect the risk

Monitor for harmful behavior.

### 5. Respond to the risk

Contain and remediate incidents.

This provides layered protection.

---

# 20. Prevention Is Usually Better Than Detection

For example:

### Weak approach

Allow confidential information into an AI system and detect leakage afterward.

### Stronger approach

Prevent confidential information from entering the system in the first place.

```text
Sensitive Data
     ↓
DLP / Classification
     ↓
BLOCK
```

This is generally stronger than:

```text
Sensitive Data
     ↓
AI System
     ↓
Leakage
     ↓
Detection
```

---

# 21. Defense in Depth

AI governance should avoid relying on a single control.

Example:

```text
Identity
   ↓
Authorization
   ↓
Data Filtering
   ↓
Prompt Protection
   ↓
Model Controls
   ↓
Output Filtering
   ↓
Human Review
   ↓
Monitoring
```

If one control fails, others may reduce the consequences.

---

# 22. Control Selection Should Be Risk-Based

Not every AI system needs the same controls.

For example:

### Low-risk AI

Internal meeting summarization.

Potential controls:

* Approved tool.
* Basic data restrictions.
* User awareness.

### High-risk AI

AI supporting significant customer decisions.

Potential controls:

* Formal validation.
* Bias assessment.
* Human oversight.
* Monitoring.
* Explainability.
* Incident management.
* Independent review.

---

# 23. Risk Treatment Must Address the Actual Risk

Suppose the risk is:

> Unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information.

Implementing:

> Employee AI training

may help, but it may not be sufficient.

Additional controls could include:

* DLP.
* Access control.
* Data classification.
* Prompt filtering.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.

The treatment should directly address the risk mechanism.

---

# 24. Control-to-Risk Mapping

A useful model is:

```text
Risk
 ↓
Cause
 ↓
Control Objective
 ↓
Control
 ↓
Evidence
 ↓
Effectiveness
 ↓
Residual Risk
```

Example:

```text
Risk:
Sensitive data disclosure

Cause:
Unauthorized prompt submission

Control Objective:
Prevent sensitive information from
being submitted to unauthorized AI tools

Controls:
DLP + Access Control + Policy
```

# 26. Human Oversight as a Treatment

Human oversight can reduce AI risk when properly designed.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Approve / Reject / Escalate
```

But merely having a person present does not automatically make the control effective.

---

# 27. Meaningful Human Oversight

Effective human oversight should consider:

* Reviewer expertise.
* Reviewer authority.
* Access to relevant information.
* Ability to challenge the AI.
* Ability to override.
* Adequate review time.
* Clear escalation procedures.

---

# 28. Human-in-the-Loop Is Not a Magic Control

A common mistake is:

> "There is a human in the loop, therefore the risk is controlled."

Not necessarily.

If the reviewer:

* Cannot understand the output.
* Cannot challenge the system.
* Has too many decisions to review.
* Automatically trusts the AI.

then the control may provide limited risk reduction.

---

# 29. Automation Bias Treatment

To reduce automation bias:

* Train reviewers.
* Require independent reasoning.
* Monitor override rates.
* Introduce mandatory review criteria.
* Avoid presenting AI recommendations as unquestionably correct.

The goal is meaningful human judgment.

# 38. Compensating Controls

Sometimes the preferred control cannot be implemented.

Example:

> The AI vendor cannot provide sufficient model transparency.

A compensating approach might include:

* Restricting the AI to lower-risk use cases.
* Requiring human review.
* Limiting sensitive data.
* Increasing monitoring.
* Prohibiting autonomous decisions.

The residual risk must then be reassessed.

---

# 39. Risk Treatment and Architecture

Risk treatment can influence architecture.

Example:

### Architecture A

Public AI API.

### Architecture B

Enterprise-controlled AI gateway.

### Architecture C

Private model environment.

If Architecture A creates unacceptable data exposure, the organization might redesign the solution rather than simply accepting the risk.

This demonstrates:

> **Security and GRC should influence architecture decisions early.**

A high-risk AI system should not simply move to production because development is complete.

Material changes may require:

* New risk assessment.

     ↓

# 46. Treatment After an Incident

After a significant AI incident:

1. Contain.
2. Investigate.
3. Determine root cause.
4. Correct the problem.
5. Reassess risk.
6. Review controls.
7. Update governance.
8. Determine whether deployment should continue.

---

# 47. Risk Treatment and Incident Response

Risk management and incident response are closely connected.

---

# 48. Treatment Effectiveness

A treatment is not successful simply because it was implemented.

Ask:

> Did the control actually reduce risk?

For example:

```text
Before:
Risk = 20

Control implemented

After:
Risk = 19
```

If the organization's objective was to reduce the risk significantly, the treatment may be inadequate.

---

# 49. Treatment Validation

Evidence can include:

* Test results.
* Audit results.
* Monitoring data.
* Control effectiveness assessments.
* Incident statistics.
* Model performance.
* User feedback.

Treatment should be evidence-based.

---

# 50. Residual Risk After Treatment

After treatment:

```text

     ↓
Treatment
     ↓
Residual Risk
```

The residual risk must then be evaluated again.

If still above appetite:

```text
Residual Risk > Appetite
          ↓
Additional Treatment
OR
Escalation
OR
Avoidance
OR
Authorized Acceptance
```

Not every risk can be fixed simultaneously.

Prioritize based on:

# 56. Risk Escalation

Escalation is required when risk exceeds the authority or appetite of the responsible owner.

Example:

```text
Business Owner
      ↓
Risk exceeds authority
      ↓
Senior Management
      ↓
Risk Committee
      ↓
Executive Decision
```

The exact hierarchy depends on organizational governance.

---

# 57. Escalation Is Not Punishment

Escalation should be viewed as:

> **Moving a decision to the appropriate authority.**

It does not necessarily mean someone failed.

For example:

> A business owner may identify a risk that is legitimately beyond their delegated acceptance authority.

Escalation is then the correct governance mechanism.

# 61. Senior GRC Anchor

> **"Once an AI risk has been assessed, I move from risk identification to risk treatment by determining whether the exposure is within the organization's approved risk appetite and whether it can be appropriately reduced. I consider the available treatment options—avoidance, risk reduction, risk sharing or transfer, and formal acceptance—and select the approach based on the nature and severity of the risk. For AI, I look beyond traditional cybersecurity controls and consider data quality, privacy, fairness, model performance, human oversight, third-party dependencies, monitoring and governance. I make sure the selected controls address the actual risk mechanism and that their effectiveness can be demonstrated with evidence. If residual risk remains above appetite or exceeds the owner's authority, I escalate it to the appropriate level rather than allowing informal acceptance. Finally, I assign accountable owners, define treatment targets and monitoring indicators, and reassess the risk when controls are implemented, incidents occur or the AI system materially changes."**

## Part 2 — Risk Mitigation Design, Control Selection, Treatment Planning, and Residual Risk

### 62. Treatment Begins With the Risk Scenario

A common GRC mistake is selecting controls before understanding the risk.

The correct sequence is:

```text
Risk Scenario
      ↓
Root Cause
      ↓
Potential Consequence
      ↓
Risk Driver
      ↓
Treatment Objective
      ↓
Control Selection
```

For example:

> **Risk:** An AI assistant may disclose confidential information because users can submit sensitive data to an external model.

The treatment should address **data exposure**, not simply "AI risk" in general.

---

# 63. Treatment Objective

Before selecting a control, define what the treatment is supposed to accomplish.

Example:

> **Treatment objective:** Prevent sensitive organizational information from being submitted to unauthorized AI services.

Then controls can be selected against that objective.

```text
Risk
 ↓
Treatment Objective
 ↓
Controls
 ↓
Evidence
 ↓
Effectiveness
```

---

# 64. Risk Reduction Can Target Likelihood

Suppose:

```text
Likelihood = 4
Impact     = 5
```

A preventive control may reduce likelihood:

```text
Likelihood 4 → 2
Impact     5 → 5
```

Therefore:

```text
Inherent = 20
Residual = 10
```

The control has reduced the probability, while the potential consequence remains severe.

---

# 65. Risk Reduction Can Target Impact

Some controls primarily reduce consequences.

Example:

> An AI system can automatically disable itself when abnormal behavior is detected.

If an incident occurs, rapid shutdown can reduce the number of affected transactions.

Conceptually:

```text
Likelihood → unchanged
Impact     ↓
```

---

# 66. Some Controls Reduce Both

For example:

### Human approval

May reduce:

* Probability of an incorrect decision.
* Number of affected individuals.

Therefore:

```text
Likelihood ↓
Impact ↓
```

may both occur.

But the reduction should be supported by evidence rather than assumed.

---

# 67. Control Selection Should Follow the Risk Hierarchy

When designing treatment, consider:

1. Eliminate.
2. Prevent.
3. Reduce.
4. Detect.
5. Respond.
6. Recover.

This produces a layered treatment strategy.

---

# 68. Elimination

The strongest treatment may be removing the risky activity.

Example:

> Do not allow an AI system to make autonomous decisions affecting customers.

Instead:

```text
AI
 ↓
Recommendation Only
 ↓
Human Decision
```

The organization changes the use case itself.

---

# 69. Restrict the Scope

Risk can sometimes be reduced by limiting:

* Users.
* Data.
* Geography.
* Transactions.
* Business processes.
* Decision authority.

Example:

```text
Original:
AI → All Customer Decisions

Treatment:
AI → Low-Risk Recommendations Only
```

This can significantly reduce exposure.

---

# 70. Reduce AI Autonomy

An AI system can be redesigned from:

> **Autonomous action**

to:

> **Human-approved recommendation.**

Example:

```text
AI
 ↓
Recommendation
 ↓
Human Review
 ↓
Approval
 ↓
Action
```

Reducing autonomy can be a powerful risk treatment for high-impact use cases.

---

# 71. Segmentation

AI systems should not automatically have access to every enterprise resource.

Apply:

* Least privilege.
* Network segmentation.
* Application segmentation.
* Data segmentation.
* Role-based access.

Example:

```text
AI Assistant
     ↓
Approved Data Zone
     ✕
HR Database
     ✕
Finance Database
     ✕
Production Infrastructure
```

The goal is to reduce the potential blast radius.

---

# 72. Data Minimization

A strong treatment principle is:

> **Do not give the AI more data than it needs.**

If an AI assistant only needs customer ticket information, it should not automatically receive:

* Full customer records.
* Payment information.
* Authentication credentials.
* Internal HR information.

Data minimization reduces exposure.

---

# 73. Data Classification

AI treatment should align with data classification.

Example:

```text
Public
 ↓
Internal
 ↓
Confidential
 ↓
Restricted
```

Different classes may require different AI handling rules.

---

# 74. AI Data Handling Policy

A policy might establish:

### Allowed

Public and approved internal information.

### Restricted

Confidential information only through approved enterprise AI platforms.

### Prohibited

Passwords, credentials, highly sensitive information or other explicitly prohibited data.

The exact classification depends on the organization's policy.

---

# 75. Technical Enforcement

Policy alone may be insufficient.

If the policy says:

> "Do not submit confidential information to public AI tools."

but employees can freely do so, the organization has created a **policy without effective enforcement**.

Technical measures may include:

* DLP.
* CASB/SSE controls.
* Web filtering.
* Application restrictions.
* Endpoint controls.
* API gateways.

---

# 76. AI Gateway

An enterprise AI gateway can centralize controls.

```text
User
 ↓
AI Gateway
 ├── Identity
 ├── Authorization
 ├── DLP
 ├── Logging
 ├── Prompt Filtering
 ├── Policy Enforcement
 └── Monitoring
 ↓
Approved AI Service
```

This creates a governance control point.

---

# 77. Prompt Filtering

Prompt filtering can identify potentially risky inputs.

Examples:

* Credentials.
* Personal data.
* Confidential information.
* Restricted business information.

However:

> **Prompt filtering should not be treated as a perfect security control.**

It should form part of a layered defense.

---

# 78. Output Filtering

Controls can also inspect AI-generated outputs.

Potential objectives:

* Detect sensitive information.
* Prevent prohibited content.
* Identify potentially unsafe responses.
* Enforce business policies.

```text
User
 ↓
AI
 ↓
Output Filter
 ↓
Approved Response
```

---

# 79. Human Review

For high-impact outputs:

```text
AI Output
   ↓
Human Review
   ↓
Approve
OR
Reject
OR
Escalate
```

The review requirement should be risk-based.

---

# 80. Human Review Criteria

Do not simply say:

> "Human review required."

Define when review is mandatory.

For example:

* High-impact decisions.
* Low-confidence outputs.
* Sensitive transactions.
* Exceptions.
* Regulatory decisions.
* Unusual behavior.

---

# 81. Escalation Rules

A mature AI system should define:

> **When must the AI stop and involve a human?**

Example:

```text
Normal
 ↓
AI handles request

Uncertain
 ↓
Human review

High impact
 ↓
Mandatory human approval

Critical anomaly
 ↓
Stop + Incident escalation
```

---

# 82. Model Validation as Treatment

Model validation can reduce model risk by identifying:

* Performance problems.
* Data issues.
* Bias.
* Instability.
* Unexpected behavior.

Validation should occur before production and periodically afterward, depending on risk.

---

# 83. Independent Validation

For high-risk models, independent review can strengthen confidence.

The validator should ideally have sufficient independence from the team that developed the model.

The purpose is to provide:

> **Objective challenge.**

---

# 84. Bias and Fairness Controls

Where relevant, treatment can include:

* Dataset assessment.
* Representation analysis.
* Group-level testing.
* Fairness metrics.
* Threshold review.
* Human appeal.
* Monitoring.

The exact methodology depends on the use case.

---

# 85. Security Testing

AI systems should be tested against relevant threats.

Potential areas include:

* Prompt injection.
* Data leakage.
* Unauthorized access.
* Model manipulation.
* Malicious input.
* Excessive permissions.
* Supply-chain weaknesses.

Testing results can provide evidence for risk treatment effectiveness.

---

# 86. Red Teaming

AI red teaming attempts to deliberately identify weaknesses.

Example:

```text
Attacker Simulation
        ↓
Adversarial Input
        ↓
AI System
        ↓
Observe Failure
        ↓
Remediation
        ↓
Retest
```

For high-risk AI, red teaming can be an important treatment and assurance activity.

---

# 87. Monitoring as Treatment

Monitoring should detect changes after deployment.

Monitor:

* Model performance.
* Errors.
* Drift.
* Security events.
* Bias indicators.
* User behavior.
* Data anomalies.
* AI incidents.

---

# 88. Drift Detection

A treatment strategy may define:

> If model performance falls below an approved threshold, initiate review.

Example:

```text
Performance
     ↓
Within threshold → Continue

Below threshold
     ↓
Investigation
     ↓
Retraining / Rollback / Suspension
```

---

# 89. Rollback Capability

A mature AI system should have the ability to return to a previously approved version where practical.

```text
Version 3
   ↓
Incident
   ↓
Rollback
   ↓
Version 2
```

Rollback reduces recovery time.

---

# 90. Kill Switch

For particularly high-risk autonomous AI systems, a controlled shutdown mechanism may be appropriate.

```text
Critical Failure
      ↓
Kill Switch
      ↓
AI Stopped
      ↓
Human Investigation
```

The mechanism itself must be governed and tested.

---

# 91. Safe Defaults

When AI is uncertain or unavailable, define what happens.

For example:

> Instead of automatically approving a transaction, route it to manual review.

This is a **safe fallback**.

---

# 92. Business Continuity

AI dependencies can create operational risk.

Treatment may include:

* Manual fallback.
* Alternative provider.
* Backup model.
* Disaster recovery.
* Defined recovery procedures.

Example:

```text

        ↓
Manual Process
        ↓
Business Continues
```

---

# 93. Third-Party Risk Treatment

For external AI providers, treatment should consider:

* Vendor due diligence.

* Security requirements.
* Data processing.
* Subprocessors.
* Availability.
* Incident notification.
* Audit rights.
* Exit strategy.

---

# 94. Vendor Exit Strategy

A critical AI service should not create an impossible dependency.

Ask:

> **"What happens if we can no longer use this vendor?"**

Potential treatment:

* Data portability.
* Alternative provider.
* Internal replacement.
* Manual fallback.
* Contractual termination rights.

This is part of resilience.

---

# 95. Concentration Risk Treatment

If multiple AI services rely on one provider:

```text
Provider A
 ├── Customer Service
 ├── Fraud
 ├── Analytics
 └── HR
```

Treatment could include:

* Alternative providers.
* Service segmentation.
* Reduced dependency.
* Business continuity plans.

# 96. Contractual Risk Treatment

Contracts should clearly define responsibilities.

Important areas include:

* Data use.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Confidentiality.
* Incident notification.
* Audit.
* Subprocessors.
* Service availability.
* Termination.

But contractual language should be validated by the appropriate legal and procurement functions.

---

# 97. Treatment Through Training

Human behavior can create AI risk.

Training can cover:

* Approved AI tools.
* Prohibited data.
* Hallucination risks.
* Prompt injection.
* Verification.
* Reporting incidents.
* Human oversight.

Training is more effective when reinforced with technical controls.

---

# 98. Awareness Versus Enforcement

### Awareness

Employee knows:

> "I should not submit confidential information."

### Enforcement

Technical control prevents or detects the action.

Strong governance combines both.

```text
Policy
 +
Training
 +
Technical Enforcement
 =
Stronger Treatment
```

---

# 99. Treatment Through Process Design

Risk can be reduced by changing the workflow.

Example:

### High risk

```text
AI → Final Decision
```

### Lower risk

```text
AI → Recommendation
        ↓
Human Review
        ↓
Final Decision
```

Process design can therefore be a cybersecurity and GRC control.

---

# 100. Treatment Through Transparency

Where appropriate, users may need to know:

* They are interacting with AI.
* An AI system contributes to a decision.
* How to obtain human assistance.
* How to challenge or appeal a decision.

Transparency can support accountability and trust.

---

# 101. Appeal and Redress

For certain high-impact AI use cases, affected individuals may need a mechanism to:

* Challenge an outcome.
* Request human review.
* Correct information.
* Escalate a complaint.

This is especially relevant when AI decisions can materially affect people.

---

# 102. Treatment Through Data Correction

If AI risk originates from inaccurate data:

```text
Bad Data
   ↓
Incorrect AI Output
   ↓
Risk
```

Treatment should address the data itself.

For example:

* Validation.
* Correction.
* Source verification.
* Data lineage.
* Quality thresholds.

---

# 103. Treatment Through Model Restriction

Sometimes the correct control is not to improve the model but to restrict its use.

Example:

> A model performs adequately for internal recommendations but poorly for autonomous customer decisions.

Treatment:

> Allow recommendation use but prohibit autonomous decision-making.

This is a strong example of **risk-proportionate governance**.

---

# 104. Treatment Through Environmental Controls

AI risk can depend on where and how the model operates.

Controls may include:

* Isolated environments.
* Network restrictions.
* Secure endpoints.
* Containerization.
* Identity controls.
* Restricted APIs.

Environment controls can reduce attack surface.

---

# 105. Treatment Through Privilege Reduction

An AI agent should receive only the permissions necessary for its task.

Example:

```text
AI Agent
   ↓
Read Customer Tickets
   ✓

Delete Customer Records
   ✕

Create Admin Accounts
   ✕

Modify Production Firewall
   ✕
```

This limits the consequences of misuse or compromise.

---

# 106. AI Agent Risk

Agentic AI can create additional risk because it may:

* Call tools.
* Execute actions.
* Access systems.
* Make decisions.
* Chain multiple operations.

Risk treatment should therefore consider:

```text
Model
 +
Tools
 +
Permissions
 +
Autonomy
 +
External Systems
```

---

# 107. Agent Permission Model

A mature approach is:

```text
AI Agent
    ↓
Tool Request
    ↓
Authorization Check
    ↓
Policy Check
    ↓
Human Approval if Required
    ↓
Execute
```

The model should not automatically inherit unrestricted system privileges.

---

# 108. Segregation of Duties

High-risk AI processes may benefit from separation between:

* Development.
* Validation.
* Approval.
* Production deployment.
* Monitoring.

This reduces conflicts of interest.

---

# 109. Four-Eyes Principle

For particularly sensitive actions:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Reviewer 1
       +
Reviewer 2
       ↓
Approval
```

The level of review should be proportional to the consequences.

---

# 110. Compensating Controls

When a preferred control is unavailable, ask:

> **"What alternative control can achieve a similar risk reduction?"**

Example:

Preferred:

> Automated DLP.

Unavailable temporarily.

Compensating:

* Manual review.
* Restricted users.
* Restricted data.
* Approved use cases only.
* Enhanced monitoring.

Then reassess residual risk.

---

# 111. Control Failure

Suppose a critical AI control fails.

Do not simply record:

> "Control failed."

Determine:

1. What risk does the control address?
2. How long was it ineffective?
3. What exposure occurred?
4. Were compensating controls available?
5. Did the residual risk increase?
6. Does management need escalation?

---

# 112. Risk Treatment and Exceptions

An exception should not become a substitute for remediation.

Example:

```text
Control Gap
    ↓
Temporary Exception
    ↓
Compensating Controls
    ↓
Remediation
    ↓
Exception Closed
```

If the exception is repeatedly renewed, management should challenge whether the underlying treatment is realistic.

---

# 113. Risk Treatment Prioritization

When there are many treatment actions, prioritize:

### Critical risks

Immediate attention.

### High risks

Defined remediation plan and management oversight.

### Moderate risks

Standard treatment.

### Low risks

Monitor or accept where appropriate.

Exact thresholds depend on the organization's methodology.

---

# 114. Quick-Win Treatment

Some controls can be implemented quickly.

Example:

> Restrict access to an unapproved AI service.

This may immediately reduce exposure while a larger technical solution is being developed.

This is useful for temporary risk reduction.

---

# 115. Strategic Treatment

Other risks require architectural changes.

Example:

> Moving from uncontrolled external AI services to a governed enterprise AI platform.

This may take longer but provide stronger long-term risk reduction.

A mature treatment plan can therefore combine:

```text
Immediate Controls
       +
Short-Term Controls
       +
Strategic Controls
```

---

# 116. Treatment Roadmap

Example:

### Phase 1 — Immediate

* Block unauthorized AI tools.
* Define prohibited data.
* Assign AI owners.

### Phase 2 — Short Term

* Deploy AI gateway.
* Implement DLP.
* Establish monitoring.

### Phase 3 — Strategic

* Centralize AI governance.
* Implement automated risk assessment.
* Establish continuous assurance.

---

# 117. Cost Versus Risk

Treatment decisions should consider:

* Cost.
* Risk reduction.
* Business value.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Operational feasibility.

The cheapest control is not always the best control.

Likewise:

> The most expensive control is not automatically necessary.

---

# 118. Cost of Treatment Versus Cost of Risk

Consider:

```text
Control Cost = €100,000

Potential Risk Exposure = €10 million
```

A strong treatment may be economically justified.

But:

```text
Control Cost = €2 million

Potential Exposure = €50,000
```

A proportional alternative may be more appropriate unless legal or ethical obligations require the control.

---

# 119. Mandatory Controls

Cost-benefit analysis does not necessarily override mandatory obligations.

If a control is legally or contractually required:

> **The organization must meet the requirement regardless of whether management considers the implementation expensive.**

This is an important distinction between:

* Risk-based controls.
* Mandatory controls.

---

# 120. Risk Treatment Documentation

For each material treatment, document:

* Risk ID.
* Treatment option.
* Objective.
* Control.
* Owner.
* Due date.
* Cost where relevant.
* Dependencies.
* Evidence.
* Target residual risk.
* Approval.
* Review date.

---

# 121. Treatment Acceptance Criteria

Before closing a treatment action, define what "done" means.

Example:

> AI gateway deployed.

That alone is not sufficient.

Better:

> AI gateway deployed, configured according to approved policy, tested against defined scenarios, monitoring enabled, and evidence reviewed by Security and GRC.

---

# 122. Control Testing

Testing should answer:

> **Does the control work as intended?**

Example:

### Control

DLP blocks sensitive information from being sent to an external AI service.

### Test

Attempt to submit controlled sensitive-data samples.

### Expected result

Submission blocked and event logged.

This creates evidence of effectiveness.

---

# 123. Treatment Verification

A GRC professional should verify:

```text
Control Claimed
      ↓
Control Implemented
      ↓
Control Tested
      ↓
Control Effective
      ↓
Risk Reassessed
```

Skipping the final step can leave outdated residual-risk ratings.

---

# 124. Residual Risk Is the Real Decision Point

After controls are implemented:

> **What risk remains?**

Example:

```text
Inherent Risk = Critical
       ↓
Treatment
       ↓
Residual Risk = Moderate
```

Now management can decide whether Moderate is acceptable.

---

# 125. Residual Risk Can Still Be High

This is normal for some high-impact systems.

For example:

```text
Inherent = 25
Residual = 15
```

The controls may have substantially reduced risk.

But the remaining risk may still exceed appetite.

The correct response is not:

> "Controls are working, so we're finished."

Instead:

> **"Controls are working, but residual risk remains above appetite; additional treatment or formal escalation is required."**

---

# 126. Risk Treatment and Risk Appetite

The relationship is:

```text
Residual Risk
      ↓
Compare
      ↓
Risk Appetite
      ↓
Within Appetite?
   ↙        ↘
 Yes        No
 ↓           ↓
Accept     Treat /
Monitor    Escalate
```

This is one of the most important governance decision points.

# 129. Senior GRC Anchor

> **"When designing AI risk treatment, I first make sure the control is addressing the actual risk mechanism rather than simply adding controls because they are considered good practice. I define the treatment objective and determine whether the best response is to eliminate the use case, restrict its scope or autonomy, reduce likelihood or impact, detect adverse behavior, or improve response and recovery. For AI, this can include data minimization, access controls, AI gateways, DLP, model validation, security testing, human oversight, monitoring, rollback and third-party controls. # 06.17 — AI Risk Treatment, Mitigation, Acceptance, and Escalation

## Part 3 — Risk Acceptance, Exception Management, Escalation, and Governance Decision-Making

### 130. Risk Acceptance Is a Governance Decision

Risk acceptance is not simply a checkbox in a risk register.

It represents a deliberate management decision:

> **"We understand the remaining exposure, we understand the consequences, and an appropriately authorized person has decided that the organization can retain this risk."**

The key concept is **accountability**.

```text id="8m7h4q"
Risk Identified
      ↓
Risk Assessed
      ↓
Controls Implemented
      ↓
Residual Risk
      ↓
Management Decision
      ↓
Accept / Further Treat / Avoid / Escalate
```

---

# 131. Who Can Accept Risk?

Risk acceptance should follow the organization's **delegated risk authority**.

For example:

```text id="z0q1cw"
Low Risk
   ↓
Business Owner

Moderate Risk
   ↓
Senior Manager

High Risk
   ↓
Risk Committee / Executive

Critical Risk
   ↓
Executive / Board-level authority
```

The exact structure depends on the organization's governance framework.

A junior employee should not be able to accept an enterprise-level critical risk simply because they own the system.

---

# 132. Risk Ownership Versus Risk Acceptance Authority

These are not always the same thing.

### Risk owner

Accountable for managing the risk.

### Acceptance authority

Has authority to formally accept the residual exposure.

Example:

> The AI Product Owner may own the risk, but a critical residual risk may require approval from the CISO, risk committee, or executive management.

This distinction is extremely important in GRC.

---

# 133. Risk Owner

The risk owner should generally be responsible for:

* Understanding the risk.
* Coordinating treatment.
* Monitoring the risk.
* Ensuring actions are completed.
* Escalating when necessary.
* Maintaining appropriate documentation.

The risk owner should not necessarily be the person who technically operates the AI system.

---

# 134. Control Owner

The **control owner** is responsible for operating or maintaining the control.

Example:

```text id="x5f9gk"
Risk Owner
Business Executive

        ↓

Control Owner
Security Engineering

        ↓

Control
DLP blocking sensitive AI prompts
```

This distinction prevents accountability gaps.

---

# 135. Risk Owner ≠ Control Owner

Example:

### Risk

Sensitive information could be disclosed through AI.

### Risk owner

Chief Data Officer.

### Control owner

Security Engineering.

### AI platform owner

Enterprise AI Team.

### Compliance oversight

GRC.

Different roles can therefore participate in the same risk.

---

# 136. Risk Acceptance Criteria

Before accepting an AI risk, consider:

* Is the risk within appetite?
* Is the business benefit clearly understood?
* Have reasonable controls been implemented?
* Are mandatory requirements satisfied?
* Is the residual risk understood?
* Is the acceptance authority appropriate?
* Is there a defined review date?

If these questions cannot be answered, acceptance may be premature.

---

# 137. Temporary Risk Acceptance

Sometimes risk must be accepted temporarily.

Example:

> A required security control will not be available until the next platform release.

Management may authorize temporary acceptance.

But it should include:

* Expiration date.
* Compensating controls.
* Remediation plan.
* Responsible owner.
* Review date.

```text id="8ihzxu"
Control Gap
    ↓
Temporary Acceptance
    ↓
Compensating Control
    ↓
Remediation
    ↓
Close Exception
```

---

# 138. Permanent Risk Acceptance

Some risks may remain because eliminating them would be impractical or would undermine the business purpose.

In such cases:

> Permanent or ongoing acceptance may be appropriate if allowed by policy and properly authorized.

However, "permanent" should not mean:

> Never review it again.

Risk conditions can change.

---

# 139. Acceptance Expiration

A strong governance practice is to require periodic review.

Example:

```text id="2d3m4g"
Risk Accepted
     ↓
6-Month Review
     ↓
Still Acceptable?
   ↙       ↘
 Yes       No
 ↓          ↓
Continue   Reassess
```

This prevents outdated risk acceptance from remaining indefinitely.

---

# 140. Risk Acceptance Should Be Evidence-Based

Management should receive sufficient information to make an informed decision.

Evidence may include:

* Risk assessment.
* Control testing.
* Security testing.
* Model validation.
* Privacy assessment.
* Incident history.
* Performance results.
* Vendor assessment.

The decision should not be based solely on opinion.

---

# 141. Informed Risk Acceptance

A useful GRC principle is:

> **Risk acceptance should be informed, explicit and accountable.**

Not:

> "Nobody objected, so we proceeded."

---

# 142. Implied Acceptance

Avoid relying on implied acceptance.

Example:

> The risk was reported six months ago and management did nothing.

That does **not necessarily** mean:

> Management formally accepted the risk.

Formal governance should distinguish between:

* Known risk.
* Unresolved risk.
* Accepted risk.

---

# 143. Formal Acceptance Record

A risk acceptance record might contain:

| Field                  | Example                    |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Risk ID                | AI-021                     |
| Risk                   | AI data leakage            |
| Residual Rating        | High                       |
| Business Justification | Critical business use      |
| Controls               | DLP + gateway + monitoring |
| Compensating Controls  | Restricted data            |
| Risk Owner             | Business Executive         |
| Acceptance Authority   | Risk Committee             |
| Accepted Until         | Defined date               |
| Review                 | Quarterly                  |

---

# 144. Risk Exceptions

An **exception** usually means the organization temporarily or specifically permits deviation from a policy, standard, control requirement, or established process.

Example:

> Corporate policy prohibits sending confidential information to external AI services, but a business unit requests an exception for a specific approved use case.

The exception should be:

* Specific.
* Justified.
* Time-bound where appropriate.
* Approved.
* Monitored.

---

# 145. Exception Versus Risk Acceptance

These concepts are related but not identical.

### Exception

> Permission to deviate from a requirement.

### Risk acceptance

> Decision to retain the resulting risk.

Sometimes one situation requires both.

```text id="2h6f3p"
Policy Exception
       ↓
Creates Risk
       ↓
Risk Assessment
       ↓
Risk Acceptance
```

---

# 146. Example

Policy:

> Confidential information must not be processed through unapproved AI services.

Business request:

> Use an external AI service for a specific project.

GRC should ask:

1. Why is the exception needed?
2. What data will be processed?
3. What provider is involved?
4. What controls exist?
5. What residual risk remains?
6. Who has authority to approve the exception?
7. When does it expire?

---

# 147. Exceptions Should Not Become the Normal Process

A common governance problem is:

```text id="4a7o7m"
Exception
 ↓
Renew
 ↓
Renew
 ↓
Renew
 ↓
Permanent Workaround
```

This indicates that the organization may need to change the underlying policy, architecture or control.

Repeated exceptions should trigger management review.

---

# 148. Exception Conditions

An exception may include:

* Approved scope.
* Approved users.
* Approved data.
* Approved provider.
* Compensating controls.
* Monitoring.
* Expiration date.
* Review conditions.

The narrower the exception, the easier it is to control.

---

# 149. Risk Escalation

Escalation occurs when the risk requires a higher level of attention or authority.

Typical triggers include:

* Risk exceeds appetite.
* Risk exceeds acceptance authority.
* Critical control failure.
* Significant incident.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Material business impact.
* Rapidly increasing risk.
* Treatment deadline missed.
* Risk owner cannot remediate.

---

# 150. Risk Escalation Example

```text id="u2qk73"
Residual Risk = High
        ↓
Business Owner Authority = Moderate
        ↓
Cannot Accept
        ↓
Escalate
        ↓
Senior Risk Authority
```

This is appropriate governance.

---

# 151. Escalation Does Not Automatically Mean Stop

Escalation means the decision moves to the appropriate authority.

Management may then decide to:

* Continue with controls.
* Add treatment.
* Restrict the AI.
* Suspend deployment.
* Accept the risk.
* Terminate the use case.

---

# 152. Critical Risk Escalation

For critical AI risk, escalation may involve:

```text id="8x2p5e"
AI Owner
 ↓
Security / GRC
 ↓
CISO
 ↓
Risk Committee
 ↓
Executive Management
```

The exact route depends on organizational governance.

---

# 153. Risk Escalation Criteria

Organizations should define objective escalation criteria.

Examples:

### Financial

Potential loss exceeds defined threshold.

### Security

Critical vulnerability or major data exposure.

### Privacy

Potential significant privacy violation.

### AI performance

Performance falls below approved threshold.

### Regulatory

Potential breach of mandatory requirements.

### Human impact

AI decision could cause significant harm.

---

# 154. Risk Velocity

Risk severity is not the only consideration.

Consider **risk velocity**:

> How quickly could the risk materialize or escalate?

Example:

```text id="4j2vl1"
Risk A:
High impact
Slow development

Risk B:
High impact
Can occur within minutes
```

Risk B may require faster escalation even if both have similar risk scores.

---

# 155. Risk Concentration

AI risk can become concentrated when many processes depend on the same:

* Model.
* Vendor.
* Cloud provider.
* Dataset.
* AI platform.

Example:

```text id="m8d3t4"
One AI Provider
   ↓
Customer Service
Fraud Detection
Analytics
HR
Operations
```

A provider failure could affect the entire enterprise.

This should influence treatment and escalation.

---

# 156. Risk Aggregation

Individual risks can appear manageable but become significant when combined.

Example:

```text id="1m9x1r"
AI Vendor Risk
+
Data Risk
+
Model Risk
+
Operational Risk
+
Cyber Risk
        ↓
Enterprise AI Risk
```

GRC should consider both individual and aggregated exposure.

---

# 180. Closing a Risk Is Different From Closing an Action

This is an important distinction.

### Action closed

A remediation task is complete.

### Risk closed

The underlying risk is no longer relevant or has been eliminated.

Example:

> DLP implementation completed.

The **action** is closed.

But:

> Sensitive data disclosure risk

may still exist.

The risk should remain in the register if exposure remains.

# 187. Senior GRC Interview Anchor

> **"For risk acceptance, I make sure the decision is explicit, informed and made by the appropriate authority. I distinguish between the risk owner, control owner and acceptance authority because they may be different roles. If residual risk is within appetite and the appropriate controls are operating effectively, the risk may be formally accepted with defined monitoring and review. If it exceeds appetite or the owner's delegated authority, I escalate it rather than allowing implied acceptance. I also distinguish risk acceptance from policy exceptions: an exception permits a deviation from a requirement, while the resulting risk still needs to be assessed and appropriately accepted. For high-risk AI, I would use conditional approvals, go-live gates, compensating controls and progressive deployment where appropriate. Finally, I ensure that treatment actions are supported by evidence and that closing a remediation action does not automatically mean the underlying risk is closed. The ultimate objective is to maintain AI risk within the organization's approved appetite while preserving accountability and business value."**

# 189. Scenario 1 — Employee Uses Public GenAI

### Situation

Employees are using a public generative-AI service to summarize internal documents.

The organization discovers that some employees have entered confidential information.

### Risk

> Confidential organizational information may be disclosed to an external AI provider.

---

### Step 1 — Identify the Cause

Potential causes:

* No approved AI policy.
* Lack of employee awareness.
* No technical restriction.
* Uncontrolled external AI services.

---

### Step 2 — Assess the Risk

Consider:

* Data sensitivity.
* Number of users.
* Volume of information.
* Vendor data-handling practices.
* Potential regulatory impact.

---

### Step 3 — Immediate Treatment

Possible immediate actions:

* Restrict unauthorized AI services.
* Issue interim guidance.
* Define prohibited information.
* Investigate previous submissions.

---

### Step 4 — Strategic Treatment

Implement:

* Approved enterprise AI platform.
* AI gateway.
* DLP.
* Access control.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* User training.

---

### Step 5 — Residual Risk

Reassess after controls.

```text id="p0ckg8"
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Controls
     ↓
Residual Risk
     ↓
Within Appetite?
```

---

# 190. Interview Answer

> **"I would first determine what information is being submitted, who is submitting it, and what exposure exists with the external provider. I would apply immediate containment for unauthorized use while establishing an approved AI pathway. From a treatment perspective, I would combine policy, awareness and technical enforcement such as DLP, access controls and an enterprise AI gateway. I would then validate the controls, reassess residual risk and determine whether it is within the organization's risk appetite. Any remaining exposure above the business owner's authority would be escalated for formal decision."**

# 226. Scenario 24 — Different Teams Give Different Risk Ratings

Security rates:

> High.

Business rates:

Compliance rates:

> High.

GRC should facilitate alignment by examining:

* Definitions.

* Assumptions.
* Scope.

The goal is not to "win" the rating discussion.

The goal is:

> **A defensible and consistently applied risk assessment.**

# 227. Scenario 25 — Risk Score Is High but Actual Exposure Is Limited

* Only five users have access.

GRC should verify whether the scoring methodology properly reflects the actual exposure.

This demonstrates why:

> **Risk scoring should support judgment, not replace it.**

# 228. Scenario 26 — Risk Score Is Low but Consequences Are Severe

Risk methodology should allow appropriate escalation for exceptional circumstances.

# 239. The "Policy Is Not Enough" Principle

# 247. Final 06.17 Master Anchor

If the interviewer asks:

> **"How do you manage AI risk?"**

A strong answer is:

> **"I start by understanding the business objective and the specific AI use case, then identify and assess the associated risks based on likelihood, impact and the organization's risk methodology. I determine whether the risk can be avoided, reduced, shared or formally accepted. For mitigation, I design controls that address the actual risk mechanism, using a combination of preventive, detective and corrective measures such as access control, data minimization, DLP, model validation, security testing, human oversight, monitoring and incident response.**
>
> **Once controls are implemented, I verify their effectiveness with evidence rather than assuming implementation means the risk has been addressed. I then reassess the residual risk against the organization's risk appetite and the delegated acceptance authority. If the risk is within appetite, I document the decision and establish ongoing monitoring. If it exceeds appetite or the owner's authority, I escalate it for further treatment or formal management acceptance.**
>
> **For high-risk AI, I also consider restricting scope or autonomy, using phased deployment, compensating controls and go-live gates. I make sure exceptions are controlled and time-bound, and that material changes, incidents or control failures trigger reassessment. Ultimately, my role is to enable the organization to obtain business value from AI while ensuring that the resulting risk remains understood, controlled, accountable and within approved boundaries."**
