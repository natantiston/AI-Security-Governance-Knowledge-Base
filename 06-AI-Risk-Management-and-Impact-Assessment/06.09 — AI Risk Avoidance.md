# 06.09 — AI Risk Avoidance

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Risk Avoidance

### 1. Introduction to AI Risk Avoidance

AI risk avoidance is a risk treatment approach in which an organization **chooses not to undertake, discontinue, or fundamentally change an AI activity because the associated risk is unacceptable or cannot be adequately controlled**.

The central idea is simple:

> **If the organization cannot bring an AI risk within acceptable boundaries, it may decide not to proceed with the activity that creates the risk.**

Risk avoidance is therefore different from simply reducing, transferring, or accepting a risk.

---

### 2. The Fundamental Concept

A simplified model is:

```text
AI Activity
     ↓
Risk Identified
     ↓
Risk Evaluated
     ↓
Risk Above Acceptable Level
     ↓
Avoid the Activity
```

Instead of asking:

> "How can we control this risk?"

the organization may need to ask:

> **"Should we perform this AI activity at all?"**

---

### 3. Why AI Risk Avoidance Matters

AI can introduce risks that may be:

* Difficult to predict.
* Difficult to quantify.
* Difficult to mitigate.
* Difficult to transfer.
* Potentially severe.
* Incompatible with organizational values or obligations.

In such circumstances, continuing with the activity simply because AI provides business value may not be appropriate.

Risk avoidance provides a mechanism for making a deliberate **do-not-proceed decision**.

---

### 4. Avoidance Is a Risk Treatment Option

Risk avoidance is one possible response to identified risk.

Conceptually:

```text
Identified Risk
      ↓
Risk Treatment Decision
      ├── Avoid
      ├── Reduce
      ├── Transfer
      └── Retain
```

The appropriate choice depends on:

* Risk severity.
* Risk appetite.
* Business objectives.
* Legal requirements.
* Technical feasibility.
* Cost.
* Expected benefits.

---

### 5. Avoidance vs Risk Reduction

These are fundamentally different.

**Risk reduction**

> Continue the activity while implementing controls to reduce risk.

**Risk avoidance**

> Do not undertake the activity, or discontinue the activity creating the risk.

Example:

**Reduction**

> Deploy an AI assistant but restrict it from accessing confidential information.

**Avoidance**

> Do not deploy the AI assistant because the intended use requires unrestricted access to highly sensitive information that cannot be adequately protected.

---

### 6. Avoidance vs Risk Transfer

Risk transfer keeps the activity but reallocates some consequences.

Example:

> Use a third-party AI provider with contractual liability provisions.

Risk avoidance removes the activity or changes it so substantially that the original risk no longer exists.

Example:

> Do not use the external AI service.

---

### 7. Avoidance vs Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance means:

> The organization consciously retains the risk.

Risk avoidance means:

> The organization chooses not to undertake the activity creating the unacceptable risk.

For example:

```text
Risk Acceptance
AI Activity → Risk → Accept → Proceed

Risk Avoidance
AI Activity → Risk → Unacceptable → Do Not Proceed
```

---

### 8. Avoidance Does Not Mean Eliminating Every Risk

Risk avoidance normally targets a **specific risk-producing activity**.

An organization may avoid one AI use case while continuing to use AI elsewhere.

Example:

```text
AI Portfolio
 ├── Customer chatbot → Continue
 ├── Internal assistant → Continue
 └── Autonomous financial decision system → Avoid
```

Avoidance therefore can be selective.

---

### 9. AI Use-Case Avoidance

An organization may approve AI generally while avoiding particular use cases.

Potential reasons include:

* Excessive impact.
* Lack of reliable controls.
* Legal restrictions.
* Unacceptable privacy exposure.
* Safety concerns.
* Inability to explain decisions.
* Insufficient human oversight.
* Unacceptable operational dependency.

The decision should be based on the characteristics of the specific use case.

---

### 10. Avoidance at the AI Lifecycle

Risk avoidance can occur at different stages:

```text
Idea
 ↓
Design
 ↓
Development
 ↓
Testing
 ↓
Deployment
 ↓
Operation
 ↓
Retirement
```

Avoidance can occur before deployment or after deployment if new risks emerge.

---

### 11. Early Avoidance

The most efficient avoidance decision may occur before significant resources are committed.

For example:

```text
AI Proposal
    ↓
Preliminary Assessment
    ↓
Unacceptable Risk
    ↓
Project Stopped
```

This prevents the organization from spending substantial resources developing a system that ultimately cannot be responsibly deployed.

---

### 12. Avoidance During Development

Risk may become apparent during development.

For example:

* Testing reveals unacceptable performance.
* Security testing exposes fundamental vulnerabilities.
* Required data cannot be obtained appropriately.
* The intended use cannot be adequately governed.

The organization may then stop development.

---

### 13. Avoidance Before Deployment

An AI system may technically work but still fail governance requirements.

For example:

> The system performs well technically, but the organization cannot establish sufficient controls for its intended high-impact use.

The appropriate decision may be:

> Do not deploy.

---

### 14. Avoidance After Deployment

Avoidance can also mean discontinuing an existing AI system.

For example:

```text
Operational AI
      ↓
New Risk Discovered
      ↓
Risk Reassessment
      ↓
Risk Unacceptable
      ↓
Suspend / Retire
```

This is important because risk profiles can change over time.

---

### 15. Triggering Events for Avoidance

Potential triggers include:

* Major security vulnerabilities.
* Significant privacy concerns.
* Material model failures.
* Unacceptable performance.
* New legal requirements.
* Regulatory restrictions.
* Significant changes in business context.
* Unexpected harmful outcomes.
* Inability to establish required controls.

---

### 16. Avoidance and Risk Appetite

Risk avoidance is strongly connected to risk appetite.

A simplified decision model:

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Compare With Risk Appetite
   ↓
Within Appetite?
  ↙        ↘
Yes        No
 ↓          ↓
Proceed    Treatment
            ↓
       Can Risk Be
       Reduced?
        ↙     ↘
      Yes      No
       ↓        ↓
    Treat     Avoid
```

Avoidance becomes particularly relevant when acceptable risk cannot be achieved through reasonable treatment.

---

### 17. Risk Appetite vs Risk Capacity

These concepts should not be confused.

**Risk appetite**

> The amount and type of risk an organization is willing to pursue or retain.

**Risk capacity**

> The maximum level of risk the organization can withstand before threatening its ability to operate or meet objectives.

An AI activity may exceed either the organization's stated appetite or practical capacity.

---

### 18. Inherent Risk and Avoidance

Risk avoidance is particularly relevant when inherent risk is extremely high.

Example:

```text
Inherent AI Risk = Critical
        ↓
Controls Evaluated
        ↓
Risk remains unacceptable
        ↓
Avoid Activity
```

The organization should not assume that every high-risk activity must be made acceptable through controls.

Sometimes the appropriate decision is simply not to proceed.

---

### 19. Avoidance Through Scope Reduction

Avoidance does not always require completely abandoning AI.

The organization may remove the specific high-risk component.

Example:

```text
Original AI System
 ├── Recommendation
 ├── Automated Decision
 └── Autonomous Action
```

The organization may eliminate the autonomous-action component:

```text
Modified AI System
 └── Recommendation
       ↓
Human Decision
```

This can remove or materially change the original risk.

---

### 20. Avoidance Through Design Change

AI system architecture can sometimes be redesigned to avoid a risk.

For example:

**Original**

> AI directly accesses production systems.

**Alternative**

> AI operates in an isolated environment without production access.

The organization has changed the activity so that the original exposure is avoided or substantially altered.

---

### 21. Avoidance Through Data Restriction

An organization may avoid processing certain categories of data through AI.

For example:

```text
AI Application
   ↓
Approved Data Only
   ├── Public Data
   └── Internal Low-Sensitivity Data
```

Highly sensitive data may be excluded.

This is a form of **use-case or scope avoidance**.

---

### 22. Avoidance Through User Restriction

The organization may prohibit certain users or business units from using a specific AI capability.

For example:

> Employees may use approved AI tools for low-risk productivity activities, but not for specified high-risk decisions.

This limits the activity generating unacceptable exposure.

---

### 23. Avoidance Through Capability Restriction

An organization may disable particular AI capabilities.

Examples:

* External tool execution.
* Autonomous actions.
* Code execution.
* Access to sensitive databases.
* Unrestricted file uploads.
* Automated customer decisions.

The organization can therefore avoid specific risk-producing capabilities while retaining lower-risk functionality.

---

### 24. Avoidance Through Automation Restriction

A system may provide recommendations but not automatically execute decisions.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Human Decision
       ↓
Action
```

Instead of:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Automatic Decision
       ↓
Automatic Action
```

The second architecture may create a substantially different risk profile.

---

### 25. Avoidance and Human Control

Human involvement can change the nature of an AI use case.

However, the organization should not assume that adding a nominal human reviewer automatically makes a high-risk system acceptable.

The human role must be:

* Meaningful.
* Competent.
* Authorized.
* Able to intervene.
* Provided with sufficient information.
* Given sufficient time to make decisions.

---

### 26. Avoidance of Unacceptable Autonomy

Organizations may decide that certain autonomous AI actions are unacceptable.

For example:

> An AI system may generate recommendations but may not independently execute high-impact transactions.

This avoids the risk associated with unrestricted autonomous execution.

---

### 27. Avoidance and Security

Security considerations can justify avoiding an AI use case.

For example:

* AI requires excessive privileged access.
* Sensitive data cannot be adequately protected.
* Model architecture creates unacceptable attack exposure.
* Third-party dependencies cannot be sufficiently controlled.

The organization may determine:

> The security risk is incompatible with the intended deployment.

---

### 28. Avoidance and Privacy

An AI project may require processing information that creates unacceptable privacy exposure.

The organization may decide:

> The intended AI use will not proceed in its proposed form.

Alternatively, the organization may redesign the use case to avoid processing the problematic information.

---

### 29. Avoidance and Legal Requirements

Certain AI applications may encounter legal or regulatory constraints.

If an intended use cannot satisfy applicable requirements, continuing with deployment may not be appropriate.

The GRC process should therefore involve relevant legal and compliance functions.

---

### 30. Avoidance and Ethical Risk

Some organizations may avoid AI uses because the expected consequences conflict with:

* Organizational principles.
* Responsible AI commitments.
* Customer expectations.
* Ethical standards.

The organization should document the basis for such decisions rather than treating them as informal preferences.

---

### 31. Avoidance and Business Strategy

AI risk avoidance is not solely defensive.

An organization may deliberately avoid certain AI applications because:

* The business value is low.
* The risk is high.
* The cost of controls is excessive.
* A non-AI alternative is better.
* The capability is strategically unnecessary.

This makes avoidance a **business decision as well as a risk decision**.

---

### 32. Cost-Benefit Consideration

Consider:

```text
Expected AI Benefit
        vs
Cost of Managing AI Risk
```

If the cost and complexity of achieving acceptable risk are disproportionate to the expected benefit, management may choose avoidance.

This should be an informed business decision rather than simply a technical judgment.

---

### 33. AI Risk Avoidance Decision

A structured decision could ask:

1. What is the intended AI benefit?
2. What risks are created?
3. What controls are available?
4. How effective are those controls?
5. What risk remains?
6. Is the residual risk acceptable?
7. Is there a safer alternative?
8. Can the use case be redesigned?
9. Should the activity be stopped?

---

### 34. Avoidance as a Deliberate Decision

Risk avoidance should not be confused with failing to act.

A mature organization deliberately records:

> "The organization has decided not to proceed with this AI use case because the assessed risk cannot be reduced to an acceptable level."

That is a governance decision.

---

### 35. Documenting an Avoidance Decision

A decision record may contain:

| Field              | Example                        |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| AI Use Case        | Automated high-impact decision |
| Risk               | Excessive potential harm       |
| Inherent Risk      | Critical                       |
| Controls Evaluated | Multiple                       |
| Residual Risk      | High                           |
| Alternative        | Human-led process              |
| Decision           | Avoid                          |
| Decision Owner     | Executive Management           |
| Review Date        | Annual                         |

The example is illustrative.

---

### 36. Avoidance Decision Authority

The authority to stop an AI initiative should be defined.

Depending on organizational governance, this may involve:

* Business owner.
* AI governance committee.
* Risk committee.
* CISO.
* Privacy officer.
* Legal.
* Executive management.

Critical decisions may require higher-level approval.

---

### 37. Escalation

A risk professional should escalate when:

* Risk exceeds tolerance.
* Controls are inadequate.
* Business pressure conflicts with governance requirements.
* The use case has significant potential impact.
* There is disagreement between stakeholders.

The goal is informed decision-making.

---

### 38. Avoidance and Business Pressure

A common governance challenge occurs when the business strongly wants an AI capability despite significant risk.

A mature GRC response is not simply:

> "No."

Instead:

> "The current design creates unacceptable exposure. Let's determine whether the use case can be redesigned to achieve the business objective within acceptable risk boundaries."

This creates a **risk-informed alternative**.

---

### 39. Avoidance vs Responsible Innovation

Avoidance should not become a mechanism for preventing all innovation.

The objective is:

> **Avoid unacceptable risk, not innovation itself.**

Organizations can often redesign an AI use case to retain business value while removing the most problematic exposure.

---

### 40. The Safe Alternative Principle

When avoidance is recommended, GRC should consider:

> **Can the business objective be achieved another way?**

Possible alternatives include:

* Traditional software.
* Rule-based automation.
* Human-led processes.
* Smaller AI models.
* Restricted AI functionality.
* Internal deployment.
* Lower-risk datasets.

This makes risk avoidance constructive rather than purely restrictive.

---

### 41. Example — AI Hiring System

Suppose an organization proposes:

> Fully automated candidate rejection based on an AI-generated score.

The organization identifies significant concerns around:

* Fairness.
* Explainability.
* Potential discriminatory outcomes.
* Accountability.

One option is avoidance:

> Do not use AI to make the final hiring decision.

The organization may still use AI for lower-risk administrative assistance.

---

### 42. Example — Sensitive Data

Suppose a business wants to upload highly sensitive internal information to a public AI service.

If adequate controls cannot be established, the organization could avoid the activity:

> Do not upload the information to that service.

A safer alternative might be:

> Use an approved environment with appropriate security controls.

---

### 43. Example — Autonomous AI Action

Suppose an AI agent is proposed to make unrestricted changes to critical infrastructure.

If governance determines that the risk is unacceptable, the organization may avoid autonomous execution.

The AI could instead:

> Generate recommendations for authorized personnel to review.

The business objective may remain partially achievable without accepting the original risk.

---

### 44. Avoidance and Third-Party AI

Third-party AI services may create risks involving:

* Data exposure.
* Vendor dependency.
* Model uncertainty.
* Security.
* Availability.
* Contractual limitations.

If these cannot be adequately managed, the organization may avoid using the particular service.

This does not necessarily mean avoiding AI altogether.

---

### 45. Avoidance and Shadow AI

Organizations may identify unauthorized AI use.

For example:

```text
Employee
   ↓
Unapproved AI Tool
   ↓
Sensitive Information
```

A governance response may prohibit the use case or tool until appropriate safeguards exist.

This is an example of avoiding an uncontrolled AI activity.

---

### 46. Avoidance and AI Prohibitions

Organizations can define prohibited AI activities.

Examples might include:

* Unauthorized processing of restricted data.
* Unapproved autonomous actions.
* Use of prohibited external AI services.
* AI applications that violate organizational policy.

A prohibition is essentially a predefined avoidance decision for specified circumstances.

---

### 47. AI Acceptable Use Boundaries

A mature AI policy can define:

```text
Allowed
   ↓
Conditionally Allowed
   ↓
Restricted
   ↓
Prohibited
```

The **prohibited** category represents activities the organization has decided to avoid.

---

### 48. Avoidance and Governance Gates

AI projects can pass through governance gates.

For example:

```text
Concept
  ↓
Risk Gate
  ↓
Development
  ↓
Security Gate
  ↓
Testing
  ↓
Deployment Gate
  ↓
Operation
```

A project can be stopped when it fails a mandatory gate.

---

### 49. Stop Authority

A governance framework should establish who can stop an AI project.

For example:

```text
Critical Risk Detected
        ↓
Escalation
        ↓
Authorized Decision Maker
        ↓
STOP / REDESIGN / PROCEED
```

Without defined authority, serious risks may continue because no one is empowered to stop the initiative.

---

### 50. Avoidance and Change Management

An AI system that was acceptable at deployment may become unacceptable after a material change.

Examples:

* New model.
* New data source.
* New integration.
* New autonomous capability.
* New user population.
* New business purpose.

A material change can therefore trigger a new avoidance decision.

---

### 51. Periodic Reassessment

Avoidance decisions should be reviewed when circumstances change.

For example:

```text
Avoided Use Case
      ↓
Technology Changes
      ↓
Control Improvements
      ↓
Risk Reassessment
      ↓
Potential Reconsideration
```

Avoidance does not necessarily mean "never."

It may mean:

> **Not under the current risk conditions.**

---

### 52. Reconsidering an Avoided AI Use Case

An organization may revisit a previously avoided activity if:

* Better controls become available.
* Technology changes.
* Business circumstances change.
* Legal requirements change.
* Safer architectures become available.

The decision should be based on a new assessment rather than automatically reversed.

---

### 53. Avoidance Documentation

The organization should retain evidence of:

* Risk assessment.
* Business rationale.
* Alternatives considered.
* Controls evaluated.
* Decision authority.
* Decision date.
* Conditions for reconsideration.

This creates governance traceability.

---

### 54. Auditability

Internal audit should be able to determine:

> Why was this AI use case approved, restricted, or avoided?

Evidence may include:

* Risk assessments.
* Committee minutes.
* Decision records.
* Policies.
* Architecture reviews.
* Legal assessments.
* Security assessments.

This supports accountability.

---

### 55. Key AI Risk Avoidance Principles

The foundational principles are:

1. Risk avoidance means not undertaking or discontinuing a risk-producing activity.
2. Avoidance is a deliberate risk-treatment decision.
3. It is different from risk reduction.
4. It is different from risk transfer.
5. It is different from risk acceptance.
6. Avoidance can apply to an entire AI system.
7. Avoidance can apply to a specific AI capability.
8. Avoidance can apply to a specific dataset.
9. Avoidance can apply to a particular user group.
10. Avoidance can apply to autonomous functionality.
11. Avoidance can occur before development.
12. Avoidance can occur during development.
13. Avoidance can occur before deployment.
14. Avoidance can occur after deployment.
15. Risk appetite is an important decision input.
16. Risk avoidance does not mean avoiding AI altogether.
17. A safer alternative should be considered where practical.
18. Business objectives should be considered alongside risk.
19. Avoidance decisions should have appropriate authority.
20. Avoidance decisions should be documented.
21. Material changes can trigger reassessment.
22. Previously avoided use cases can be reconsidered when conditions change.
23. Prohibited AI activities represent predefined avoidance decisions.
24. Avoidance should prevent unacceptable exposure rather than unnecessarily blocking innovation.
25. The fundamental question is:

> **"Can this AI activity be conducted within the organization's acceptable risk boundaries?"**

If the answer is no, avoidance may be the appropriate treatment.
