# 06.09 — AI Risk Avoidance

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Risk Avoidance

### 1. Introduction to AI Risk Avoidance

AI risk avoidance is a risk treatment approach in which an organization **chooses not to undertake, discontinue, or fundamentally change an AI activity because the associated risk is unacceptable or cannot be adequately controlled**.

The central idea is simple:

> **If the organization cannot bring an AI risk within acceptable boundaries, it may decide not to proceed with the activity that creates the risk.**

Risk avoidance is therefore different from simply reducing, transferring, or accepting a risk.

---

### 2. The Fundamental Concept

A simplified model is:

```text
AI Activity
     ↓
Risk Identified
     ↓
Risk Evaluated
     ↓
Risk Above Acceptable Level
     ↓
Avoid the Activity
```

Instead of asking:

> "How can we control this risk?"

the organization may need to ask:

> **"Should we perform this AI activity at all?"**

---

### 3. Why AI Risk Avoidance Matters

AI can introduce risks that may be:

* Difficult to predict.
* Difficult to quantify.
* Difficult to mitigate.
* Difficult to transfer.
* Potentially severe.
* Incompatible with organizational values or obligations.

In such circumstances, continuing with the activity simply because AI provides business value may not be appropriate.

Risk avoidance provides a mechanism for making a deliberate **do-not-proceed decision**.

---

### 4. Avoidance Is a Risk Treatment Option

Risk avoidance is one possible response to identified risk.

Conceptually:

```text
Identified Risk
      ↓
Risk Treatment Decision
      ├── Avoid
      ├── Reduce
      ├── Transfer
      └── Retain
```

The appropriate choice depends on:

* Risk severity.
* Risk appetite.
* Business objectives.
* Legal requirements.
* Technical feasibility.
* Cost.
* Expected benefits.

---

### 5. Avoidance vs Risk Reduction

These are fundamentally different.

**Risk reduction**

> Continue the activity while implementing controls to reduce risk.

**Risk avoidance**

> Do not undertake the activity, or discontinue the activity creating the risk.

Example:

**Reduction**

> Deploy an AI assistant but restrict it from accessing confidential information.

**Avoidance**

> Do not deploy the AI assistant because the intended use requires unrestricted access to highly sensitive information that cannot be adequately protected.

---

### 6. Avoidance vs Risk Transfer

Risk transfer keeps the activity but reallocates some consequences.

Example:

> Use a third-party AI provider with contractual liability provisions.

Risk avoidance removes the activity or changes it so substantially that the original risk no longer exists.

Example:

> Do not use the external AI service.

---

### 7. Avoidance vs Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance means:

> The organization consciously retains the risk.

Risk avoidance means:

> The organization chooses not to undertake the activity creating the unacceptable risk.

For example:

```text
Risk Acceptance
AI Activity → Risk → Accept → Proceed

Risk Avoidance
AI Activity → Risk → Unacceptable → Do Not Proceed
```

---

### 8. Avoidance Does Not Mean Eliminating Every Risk

Risk avoidance normally targets a **specific risk-producing activity**.

An organization may avoid one AI use case while continuing to use AI elsewhere.

Example:

```text
AI Portfolio
 ├── Customer chatbot → Continue
 ├── Internal assistant → Continue
 └── Autonomous financial decision system → Avoid
```

Avoidance therefore can be selective.

---

### 9. AI Use-Case Avoidance

An organization may approve AI generally while avoiding particular use cases.

Potential reasons include:

* Excessive impact.
* Lack of reliable controls.
* Legal restrictions.
* Unacceptable privacy exposure.
* Safety concerns.
* Inability to explain decisions.
* Insufficient human oversight.
* Unacceptable operational dependency.

The decision should be based on the characteristics of the specific use case.

---

### 10. Avoidance at the AI Lifecycle

Risk avoidance can occur at different stages:

```text
Idea
 ↓
Design
 ↓
Development
 ↓
Testing
 ↓
Deployment
 ↓
Operation
 ↓
Retirement
```

Avoidance can occur before deployment or after deployment if new risks emerge.

---

### 11. Early Avoidance

The most efficient avoidance decision may occur before significant resources are committed.

For example:

```text
AI Proposal
    ↓
Preliminary Assessment
    ↓
Unacceptable Risk
    ↓
Project Stopped
```

This prevents the organization from spending substantial resources developing a system that ultimately cannot be responsibly deployed.

---

### 12. Avoidance During Development

Risk may become apparent during development.

For example:

* Testing reveals unacceptable performance.
* Security testing exposes fundamental vulnerabilities.
* Required data cannot be obtained appropriately.
* The intended use cannot be adequately governed.

The organization may then stop development.

---

### 13. Avoidance Before Deployment

An AI system may technically work but still fail governance requirements.

For example:

> The system performs well technically, but the organization cannot establish sufficient controls for its intended high-impact use.

The appropriate decision may be:

> Do not deploy.

---

### 14. Avoidance After Deployment

Avoidance can also mean discontinuing an existing AI system.

For example:

```text
Operational AI
      ↓
New Risk Discovered
      ↓
Risk Reassessment
      ↓
Risk Unacceptable
      ↓
Suspend / Retire
```

This is important because risk profiles can change over time.

---

### 15. Triggering Events for Avoidance

Potential triggers include:

* Major security vulnerabilities.
* Significant privacy concerns.
* Material model failures.
* Unacceptable performance.
* New legal requirements.
* Regulatory restrictions.
* Significant changes in business context.
* Unexpected harmful outcomes.
* Inability to establish required controls.

---

### 16. Avoidance and Risk Appetite

Risk avoidance is strongly connected to risk appetite.

A simplified decision model:

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Compare With Risk Appetite
   ↓
Within Appetite?
  ↙        ↘
Yes        No
 ↓          ↓
Proceed    Treatment
            ↓
       Can Risk Be
       Reduced?
        ↙     ↘
      Yes      No
       ↓        ↓
    Treat     Avoid
```

Avoidance becomes particularly relevant when acceptable risk cannot be achieved through reasonable treatment.

---

### 17. Risk Appetite vs Risk Capacity

These concepts should not be confused.

**Risk appetite**

> The amount and type of risk an organization is willing to pursue or retain.

**Risk capacity**

> The maximum level of risk the organization can withstand before threatening its ability to operate or meet objectives.

An AI activity may exceed either the organization's stated appetite or practical capacity.

---

### 18. Inherent Risk and Avoidance

Risk avoidance is particularly relevant when inherent risk is extremely high.

Example:

```text
Inherent AI Risk = Critical
        ↓
Controls Evaluated
        ↓
Risk remains unacceptable
        ↓
Avoid Activity
```

The organization should not assume that every high-risk activity must be made acceptable through controls.

Sometimes the appropriate decision is simply not to proceed.

---

### 19. Avoidance Through Scope Reduction

Avoidance does not always require completely abandoning AI.

The organization may remove the specific high-risk component.

Example:

```text
Original AI System
 ├── Recommendation
 ├── Automated Decision
 └── Autonomous Action
```

The organization may eliminate the autonomous-action component:

```text
Modified AI System
 └── Recommendation
       ↓
Human Decision
```

This can remove or materially change the original risk.

---

### 20. Avoidance Through Design Change

AI system architecture can sometimes be redesigned to avoid a risk.

For example:

**Original**

> AI directly accesses production systems.

**Alternative**

> AI operates in an isolated environment without production access.

The organization has changed the activity so that the original exposure is avoided or substantially altered.

---

### 21. Avoidance Through Data Restriction

An organization may avoid processing certain categories of data through AI.

For example:

```text
AI Application
   ↓
Approved Data Only
   ├── Public Data
   └── Internal Low-Sensitivity Data
```

Highly sensitive data may be excluded.

This is a form of **use-case or scope avoidance**.

---

### 22. Avoidance Through User Restriction

The organization may prohibit certain users or business units from using a specific AI capability.

For example:

> Employees may use approved AI tools for low-risk productivity activities, but not for specified high-risk decisions.

This limits the activity generating unacceptable exposure.

---

### 23. Avoidance Through Capability Restriction

An organization may disable particular AI capabilities.

Examples:

* External tool execution.
* Autonomous actions.
* Code execution.
* Access to sensitive databases.
* Unrestricted file uploads.
* Automated customer decisions.

The organization can therefore avoid specific risk-producing capabilities while retaining lower-risk functionality.

---

### 24. Avoidance Through Automation Restriction

A system may provide recommendations but not automatically execute decisions.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Human Decision
       ↓
Action
```

Instead of:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Automatic Decision
       ↓
Automatic Action
```

The second architecture may create a substantially different risk profile.

---

### 25. Avoidance and Human Control

Human involvement can change the nature of an AI use case.

However, the organization should not assume that adding a nominal human reviewer automatically makes a high-risk system acceptable.

The human role must be:

* Meaningful.
* Competent.
* Authorized.
* Able to intervene.
* Provided with sufficient information.
* Given sufficient time to make decisions.

---

### 26. Avoidance of Unacceptable Autonomy

Organizations may decide that certain autonomous AI actions are unacceptable.

For example:

> An AI system may generate recommendations but may not independently execute high-impact transactions.

This avoids the risk associated with unrestricted autonomous execution.

---

### 27. Avoidance and Security

Security considerations can justify avoiding an AI use case.

For example:

* AI requires excessive privileged access.
* Sensitive data cannot be adequately protected.
* Model architecture creates unacceptable attack exposure.
* Third-party dependencies cannot be sufficiently controlled.

The organization may determine:

> The security risk is incompatible with the intended deployment.

---

### 28. Avoidance and Privacy

An AI project may require processing information that creates unacceptable privacy exposure.

The organization may decide:

> The intended AI use will not proceed in its proposed form.

Alternatively, the organization may redesign the use case to avoid processing the problematic information.

---

### 29. Avoidance and Legal Requirements

Certain AI applications may encounter legal or regulatory constraints.

If an intended use cannot satisfy applicable requirements, continuing with deployment may not be appropriate.

The GRC process should therefore involve relevant legal and compliance functions.

---

### 30. Avoidance and Ethical Risk

Some organizations may avoid AI uses because the expected consequences conflict with:

* Organizational principles.
* Responsible AI commitments.
* Customer expectations.
* Ethical standards.

The organization should document the basis for such decisions rather than treating them as informal preferences.

---

### 31. Avoidance and Business Strategy

AI risk avoidance is not solely defensive.

An organization may deliberately avoid certain AI applications because:

* The business value is low.
* The risk is high.
* The cost of controls is excessive.
* A non-AI alternative is better.
* The capability is strategically unnecessary.

This makes avoidance a **business decision as well as a risk decision**.

---

### 32. Cost-Benefit Consideration

Consider:

```text
Expected AI Benefit
        vs
Cost of Managing AI Risk
```

If the cost and complexity of achieving acceptable risk are disproportionate to the expected benefit, management may choose avoidance.

This should be an informed business decision rather than simply a technical judgment.

---

### 33. AI Risk Avoidance Decision

A structured decision could ask:

1. What is the intended AI benefit?
2. What risks are created?
3. What controls are available?
4. How effective are those controls?
5. What risk remains?
6. Is the residual risk acceptable?
7. Is there a safer alternative?
8. Can the use case be redesigned?
9. Should the activity be stopped?

---

### 34. Avoidance as a Deliberate Decision

Risk avoidance should not be confused with failing to act.

A mature organization deliberately records:

> "The organization has decided not to proceed with this AI use case because the assessed risk cannot be reduced to an acceptable level."

That is a governance decision.

---

### 35. Documenting an Avoidance Decision

A decision record may contain:

| Field              | Example                        |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| AI Use Case        | Automated high-impact decision |
| Risk               | Excessive potential harm       |
| Inherent Risk      | Critical                       |
| Controls Evaluated | Multiple                       |
| Residual Risk      | High                           |
| Alternative        | Human-led process              |
| Decision           | Avoid                          |
| Decision Owner     | Executive Management           |
| Review Date        | Annual                         |

The example is illustrative.

---

### 36. Avoidance Decision Authority

The authority to stop an AI initiative should be defined.

Depending on organizational governance, this may involve:

* Business owner.
* AI governance committee.
* Risk committee.
* CISO.
* Privacy officer.
* Legal.
* Executive management.

Critical decisions may require higher-level approval.

---

### 37. Escalation

A risk professional should escalate when:

* Risk exceeds tolerance.
* Controls are inadequate.
* Business pressure conflicts with governance requirements.
* The use case has significant potential impact.
* There is disagreement between stakeholders.

The goal is informed decision-making.

---

### 38. Avoidance and Business Pressure

A common governance challenge occurs when the business strongly wants an AI capability despite significant risk.

A mature GRC response is not simply:

> "No."

Instead:

> "The current design creates unacceptable exposure. Let's determine whether the use case can be redesigned to achieve the business objective within acceptable risk boundaries."

This creates a **risk-informed alternative**.

---

### 39. Avoidance vs Responsible Innovation

Avoidance should not become a mechanism for preventing all innovation.

The objective is:

> **Avoid unacceptable risk, not innovation itself.**

Organizations can often redesign an AI use case to retain business value while removing the most problematic exposure.

---

### 40. The Safe Alternative Principle

When avoidance is recommended, GRC should consider:

> **Can the business objective be achieved another way?**

Possible alternatives include:

* Traditional software.
* Rule-based automation.
* Human-led processes.
* Smaller AI models.
* Restricted AI functionality.
* Internal deployment.
* Lower-risk datasets.

This makes risk avoidance constructive rather than purely restrictive.

---

### 41. Example — AI Hiring System

Suppose an organization proposes:

> Fully automated candidate rejection based on an AI-generated score.

The organization identifies significant concerns around:

* Fairness.
* Explainability.
* Potential discriminatory outcomes.
* Accountability.

One option is avoidance:

> Do not use AI to make the final hiring decision.

The organization may still use AI for lower-risk administrative assistance.

---

### 42. Example — Sensitive Data

Suppose a business wants to upload highly sensitive internal information to a public AI service.

If adequate controls cannot be established, the organization could avoid the activity:

> Do not upload the information to that service.

A safer alternative might be:

> Use an approved environment with appropriate security controls.

---

### 43. Example — Autonomous AI Action

Suppose an AI agent is proposed to make unrestricted changes to critical infrastructure.

If governance determines that the risk is unacceptable, the organization may avoid autonomous execution.

The AI could instead:

> Generate recommendations for authorized personnel to review.

The business objective may remain partially achievable without accepting the original risk.

---

### 44. Avoidance and Third-Party AI

Third-party AI services may create risks involving:

* Data exposure.
* Vendor dependency.
* Model uncertainty.
* Security.
* Availability.
* Contractual limitations.

If these cannot be adequately managed, the organization may avoid using the particular service.

This does not necessarily mean avoiding AI altogether.

---

### 45. Avoidance and Shadow AI

Organizations may identify unauthorized AI use.

For example:

```text
Employee
   ↓
Unapproved AI Tool
   ↓
Sensitive Information
```

A governance response may prohibit the use case or tool until appropriate safeguards exist.

This is an example of avoiding an uncontrolled AI activity.

---

### 46. Avoidance and AI Prohibitions

Organizations can define prohibited AI activities.

Examples might include:

* Unauthorized processing of restricted data.
* Unapproved autonomous actions.
* Use of prohibited external AI services.
* AI applications that violate organizational policy.

A prohibition is essentially a predefined avoidance decision for specified circumstances.

---

### 47. AI Acceptable Use Boundaries

A mature AI policy can define:

```text
Allowed
   ↓
Conditionally Allowed
   ↓
Restricted
   ↓
Prohibited
```

The **prohibited** category represents activities the organization has decided to avoid.

---

### 48. Avoidance and Governance Gates

AI projects can pass through governance gates.

For example:

```text
Concept
  ↓
Risk Gate
  ↓
Development
  ↓
Security Gate
  ↓
Testing
  ↓
Deployment Gate
  ↓
Operation
```

A project can be stopped when it fails a mandatory gate.

---

### 49. Stop Authority

A governance framework should establish who can stop an AI project.

For example:

```text
Critical Risk Detected
        ↓
Escalation
        ↓
Authorized Decision Maker
        ↓
STOP / REDESIGN / PROCEED
```

Without defined authority, serious risks may continue because no one is empowered to stop the initiative.

---

### 50. Avoidance and Change Management

An AI system that was acceptable at deployment may become unacceptable after a material change.

Examples:

* New model.
* New data source.
* New integration.
* New autonomous capability.
* New user population.
* New business purpose.

A material change can therefore trigger a new avoidance decision.

---

### 51. Periodic Reassessment

Avoidance decisions should be reviewed when circumstances change.

For example:

```text
Avoided Use Case
      ↓
Technology Changes
      ↓
Control Improvements
      ↓
Risk Reassessment
      ↓
Potential Reconsideration
```

Avoidance does not necessarily mean "never."

It may mean:

> **Not under the current risk conditions.**

---

### 52. Reconsidering an Avoided AI Use Case

An organization may revisit a previously avoided activity if:

* Better controls become available.
* Technology changes.
* Business circumstances change.
* Legal requirements change.
* Safer architectures become available.

The decision should be based on a new assessment rather than automatically reversed.

---

### 53. Avoidance Documentation

The organization should retain evidence of:

* Risk assessment.
* Business rationale.
* Alternatives considered.
* Controls evaluated.
* Decision authority.
* Decision date.
* Conditions for reconsideration.

This creates governance traceability.

---

### 54. Auditability

Internal audit should be able to determine:

> Why was this AI use case approved, restricted, or avoided?

Evidence may include:

* Risk assessments.
* Committee minutes.
* Decision records.
* Policies.
* Architecture reviews.
* Legal assessments.
* Security assessments.

This supports accountability.

---

### 55. Key AI Risk Avoidance Principles

The foundational principles are:

1. Risk avoidance means not undertaking or discontinuing a risk-producing activity.
2. Avoidance is a deliberate risk-treatment decision.
3. It is different from risk reduction.
4. It is different from risk transfer.
5. It is different from risk acceptance.
6. Avoidance can apply to an entire AI system.
7. Avoidance can apply to a specific AI capability.
8. Avoidance can apply to a specific dataset.
9. Avoidance can apply to a particular user group.
10. Avoidance can apply to autonomous functionality.
11. Avoidance can occur before development.
12. Avoidance can occur during development.
13. Avoidance can occur before deployment.
14. Avoidance can occur after deployment.
15. Risk appetite is an important decision input.
16. Risk avoidance does not mean avoiding AI altogether.
17. A safer alternative should be considered where practical.
18. Business objectives should be considered alongside risk.
19. Avoidance decisions should have appropriate authority.
20. Avoidance decisions should be documented.
21. Material changes can trigger reassessment.
22. Previously avoided use cases can be reconsidered when conditions change.
23. Prohibited AI activities represent predefined avoidance decisions.
24. Avoidance should prevent unacceptable exposure rather than unnecessarily blocking innovation.
25. The fundamental question is:

> **"Can this AI activity be conducted within the organization's acceptable risk boundaries?"**

If the answer is no, avoidance may be the appropriate treatment.

# 06.09 — AI Risk Avoidance

## Part 2 — AI Risk Avoidance Assessment and Decision Framework

### 56. AI Risk Avoidance Assessment

An AI risk avoidance decision should be based on a structured assessment rather than a subjective statement that an AI system is "too risky."

The organization should establish a clear relationship between:

```text
AI Business Objective
        ↓
AI Activity
        ↓
Risk Scenario
        ↓
Risk Assessment
        ↓
Available Treatment
        ↓
Avoidance Decision
```

The purpose is to determine whether the activity can reasonably operate within the organization's defined risk boundaries.

---

### 57. Start With the Business Objective

Before deciding whether to avoid an AI activity, GRC should understand **why the organization wants to use AI**.

Questions include:

* What business problem is being solved?
* What benefit is expected?
* Who will use the system?
* What process will be affected?
* What decision or action will AI support?
* Is AI actually necessary?

This prevents risk analysis from occurring without understanding the underlying business purpose.

---

### 58. Determine Whether AI Is Necessary

One important question is:

> **Does the business objective actually require AI?**

A business process may be achievable through:

* Traditional software.
* Rule-based automation.
* Manual processing.
* Statistical methods.
* Existing enterprise platforms.

If a lower-risk alternative provides substantially the same benefit, the organization may decide not to introduce the AI-related risk.

---

### 59. AI Necessity Assessment

A simple assessment can compare:

| Option     | Business Value | Risk         | Complexity |
| ---------- | -------------- | ------------ | ---------- |
| Manual     | Moderate       | Low          | High       |
| Rule-based | High           | Low–Moderate | Moderate   |
| AI-based   | High           | High         | High       |

The numbers are illustrative.

The purpose is to evaluate whether the additional AI risk is justified by additional business value.

---

### 60. Define the AI Activity

The organization should clearly define what it intends to do.

For example:

> "Use an AI system to automatically classify customer complaints and route them to the appropriate support team."

This is much easier to assess than:

> "Use AI for customer service."

The more precise the use case, the more precise the avoidance decision can be.

---

### 61. Define the Risk Scenario

A risk scenario should describe:

```text
Threat / Cause
      ↓
AI Activity
      ↓
Potential Event
      ↓
Business Consequence
```

For example:

> An AI classification error causes a critical customer complaint to be incorrectly categorized, resulting in delayed escalation and customer impact.

This provides a concrete basis for assessment.

---

### 62. Identify the Risk Drivers

AI risk may be driven by:

* Data quality.
* Model limitations.
* Security weaknesses.
* Excessive autonomy.
* Third-party dependency.
* Lack of human oversight.
* Privacy exposure.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Poor explainability.
* Uncertain performance.

The assessment should identify which drivers make the risk unacceptable.

---

### 63. Assess Inherent Risk

The organization should determine the risk **before considering additional controls**.

For example:

| Dimension     | Assessment |
| ------------- | ---------- |
| Likelihood    | High       |
| Impact        | High       |
| Inherent Risk | Critical   |

This establishes the initial risk position.

---

### 64. Assess Available Controls

Next determine whether controls could realistically reduce the risk.

Potential controls include:

* Access restrictions.
* Data minimization.
* Human review.
* Model testing.
* Monitoring.
* Logging.
* Segmentation.
* Rate limiting.
* Approval workflows.
* Security controls.

The important question is not merely whether controls exist, but whether they are **effective enough**.

---

### 65. Control Feasibility

A control may theoretically exist but be impractical.

For example:

> "Every AI output will be reviewed by a human."

If the AI generates 100,000 decisions per hour, meaningful human review may not be operationally feasible.

Therefore, GRC should assess:

> **Can the proposed control actually operate at the required scale?**

---

### 66. Control Effectiveness

Controls should be assessed based on their ability to address the specific risk.

Example:

```text
Risk
 ↓
Control
 ↓
Does Control Address Cause?
 ↓
Does Control Reduce Impact?
 ↓
Can Control Be Reliably Operated?
```

If the answer is no, the control may not provide sufficient treatment.

---

### 67. Residual Risk

After controls are considered:

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

The key decision is whether the residual risk is acceptable.

If:

> Residual Risk ≤ Risk Appetite

the activity may potentially proceed.

If:

> Residual Risk > Risk Appetite

additional treatment or avoidance may be necessary.

---

### 68. Control Gap

A **control gap** exists when the organization lacks an effective control required to manage a material risk.

Example:

> AI makes high-impact decisions, but there is no practical mechanism for meaningful human review.

That gap may contribute to an avoidance decision.

---

### 69. Fundamental Control Gap

Some gaps may be difficult to compensate for.

For example:

> The AI system requires unrestricted access to sensitive production data, and no technically feasible architecture can sufficiently isolate the information.

If the business requirement cannot be changed, avoidance may become appropriate.

---

### 70. Treatment Feasibility Assessment

Before choosing avoidance, GRC should ask:

1. Can the risk be reduced?
2. Can the architecture be redesigned?
3. Can the AI scope be reduced?
4. Can access be restricted?
5. Can human oversight be introduced?
6. Can a safer dataset be used?
7. Can another technology achieve the objective?
8. Can the AI service be replaced?

Only after reasonable alternatives have been assessed should the organization conclude that avoidance is necessary.

---

### 71. Avoidance Threshold

Organizations can define conditions under which avoidance should be considered.

For example:

> An AI use case should be considered for avoidance when residual risk remains above the organization's approved tolerance and no reasonably practicable treatment can reduce it to an acceptable level.

This creates consistency across AI projects.

---

### 72. Hard Stop Conditions

Some organizations may establish mandatory stop conditions.

Examples:

* Prohibited use case.
* Legal prohibition.
* Unacceptable security exposure.
* Inability to meet mandatory security requirements.
* Inability to protect required sensitive information.
* Unacceptable safety exposure.

These conditions can prevent projects from progressing regardless of expected business value.

---

### 73. Conditional Stop Conditions

Other situations may require additional remediation before proceeding.

For example:

> Deployment is prohibited until appropriate security controls are implemented.

This is different from permanent avoidance.

The organization is effectively saying:

> **"Not yet."**

rather than:

> **"Never."**

---

### 74. Temporary Avoidance

Temporary avoidance may be appropriate when:

* Technology is immature.
* Controls are incomplete.
* Vendor assurance is insufficient.
* Regulatory interpretation is uncertain.
* Required monitoring is not yet available.

The organization can pause the activity while conditions are improved.

---

### 75. Permanent Avoidance

Permanent avoidance may be appropriate when:

* The activity is prohibited.
* Risk cannot realistically be controlled.
* Business value is insufficient.
* Safer alternatives exist.
* The risk fundamentally conflicts with organizational requirements.

The decision should be documented as such.

---

### 76. Scope-Based Avoidance

Avoidance can be applied selectively.

For example:

```text
AI Project
 ├── Low-risk functionality → Approved
 ├── Moderate functionality → Controlled
 └── High-risk functionality → Avoided
```

This is often more practical than rejecting the entire project.

---

### 77. Capability-Based Avoidance

The organization may prohibit specific capabilities.

Examples:

* Autonomous execution.
* Privileged system access.
* Unrestricted external communication.
* Automated high-impact decisions.

The remaining AI functionality can potentially continue under appropriate governance.

---

### 78. Data-Based Avoidance

The organization may prohibit particular data from being processed.

Example:

```text
AI System
 ├── Public data → Allowed
 ├── Internal data → Conditional
 └── Highly sensitive data → Prohibited
```

This can significantly reduce exposure without eliminating the entire AI system.

---

### 79. Environment-Based Avoidance

An organization may permit an AI capability in one environment but prohibit it in another.

For example:

```text
Development → Allowed
Testing → Controlled
Production → Prohibited
```

until the necessary controls are implemented.

---

### 80. User-Based Avoidance

Access may be restricted according to user role.

For example:

* General employees: approved productivity use.
* Administrators: restricted.
* Privileged users: prohibited for certain AI functions.

This reduces the population exposed to the risk.

---

### 81. Geographic or Jurisdictional Avoidance

An organization operating across jurisdictions may determine that an AI use case cannot be deployed in a particular jurisdiction because the required governance conditions cannot be met.

The organization can therefore:

> Avoid deployment in that specific jurisdiction.

The broader AI program may continue elsewhere where the risk can be appropriately managed.

---

### 82. Vendor-Based Avoidance

Sometimes the problem is not the AI use case itself but the provider.

For example:

> The organization needs an AI capability, but the proposed provider cannot meet required security or governance requirements.

The organization may avoid:

> **That vendor or service**

rather than avoiding the business use case.

---

### 83. Architecture-Based Avoidance

An AI architecture may create unacceptable exposure.

For example:

```text
AI
 ↓
Direct Production Access
 ↓
Critical Systems
```

The organization may reject that architecture.

A safer architecture could isolate AI from critical systems.

The avoidance decision therefore targets the **unsafe architecture**, not necessarily the business objective.

---

### 84. Dependency-Based Avoidance

An AI system may depend excessively on:

* One provider.
* One model.
* One cloud platform.
* One data source.
* One proprietary interface.

If the dependency creates unacceptable concentration risk, the organization may avoid the dependency or redesign the architecture.

---

### 85. Performance-Based Avoidance

AI systems can sometimes produce unacceptable error rates.

For example:

> An AI model does not achieve the minimum accuracy required for a critical business process.

If the error cannot be reduced sufficiently, the organization may avoid using AI for that process.

---

### 86. Explainability-Based Avoidance

Some use cases may require a level of explanation that the AI system cannot reliably provide.

If meaningful explanation is necessary for:

* Governance.
* Customer communication.
* Internal accountability.
* Decision review.

and the system cannot provide it, the organization may avoid the use case.

---

### 87. Human Oversight Feasibility

Human oversight should be assessed realistically.

Questions include:

* Who reviews the AI output?
* How quickly must they act?
* What information do they receive?
* Can they override the AI?
* Do they have authority?
* Are enough qualified people available?

If meaningful oversight cannot be provided, avoidance may be considered.

---

### 88. Security Feasibility

GRC should determine whether security requirements can realistically be achieved.

Consider:

* Identity management.
* Privilege.
* Network isolation.
* Encryption.
* Monitoring.
* Logging.
* Data protection.
* Model security.

If critical security controls cannot be implemented, the organization may avoid deployment.

---

### 89. Privacy Feasibility

The organization should determine whether the intended processing can be performed in an acceptable manner.

If the required privacy protections cannot be implemented, the organization may:

* Change the use case.
* Remove certain data.
* Use another architecture.
* Select another provider.
* Avoid the activity.

---

### 90. Business Continuity Feasibility

For critical AI services, assess:

> What happens if the AI system becomes unavailable?

If no practical fallback exists and the dependency creates unacceptable business risk, the organization may avoid using the AI system for that critical process until resilience is improved.

---

### 91. Avoidance Decision Matrix

A simple decision matrix can be used:

| Question                      | Result   |
| ----------------------------- | -------- |
| Business value significant?   | Yes      |
| Inherent risk acceptable?     | No       |
| Controls available?           | Yes      |
| Controls effective?           | No       |
| Safer architecture available? | Yes      |
| Residual risk acceptable?     | No       |
| Decision                      | Redesign |

Avoidance should therefore not always be the immediate answer.

---

### 92. Expanded Decision Matrix

| Condition                             | Possible Decision     |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Low risk                              | Proceed               |
| Moderate risk + effective controls    | Proceed with controls |
| High risk + controls feasible         | Treat                 |
| High risk + controls inadequate       | Redesign              |
| Critical risk + alternative available | Avoid current design  |
| Prohibited activity                   | Avoid                 |
| Uncertain risk                        | Pause and assess      |

This creates consistent decision-making.

---

### 93. Risk-Based Decision Tree

```text
              AI Use Case
                   ↓
             Identify Risk
                   ↓
          Assess Inherent Risk
                   ↓
          Can Risk Be Controlled?
             ↙           ↘
           Yes            No
            ↓              ↓
       Apply Controls    Consider Avoidance
            ↓              ↓
      Assess Residual   Alternative?
            ↓           ↙        ↘
       Acceptable?     Yes        No
        ↙     ↘         ↓          ↓
      Yes      No     Redesign   Avoid
       ↓        ↓
    Proceed   More Treatment
```

This provides a practical governance structure.

---

### 94. Avoidance Decision Record

A formal decision record can include:

**Use Case**

> AI-based customer decision support.

**Risk**

> Potentially harmful incorrect decisions.

**Assessment**

> Residual risk remains above approved tolerance.

**Treatment options considered**

* Human review.
* Restricted scope.
* Additional testing.
* Alternative technology.

**Conclusion**

> Current implementation should not proceed.

**Decision**

> Avoid current deployment model.

---

### 95. Conditions for Reconsideration

A good avoidance decision can specify what would need to change.

For example:

> Reconsider when the organization can demonstrate sufficient performance, security controls, human oversight, and governance capability.

This turns avoidance into a measurable governance condition.

---

### 96. Avoidance Review

Avoidance decisions should be periodically reviewed when relevant.

The organization can ask:

* Has technology improved?
* Have controls improved?
* Has the business requirement changed?
* Has the regulatory environment changed?
* Is there now a safer provider?
* Is there a better architecture?

This prevents outdated decisions from becoming permanent barriers.

---

### 97. GRC Documentation Requirements

The GRC record should demonstrate:

```text
Why the AI was proposed
        ↓
What risks were identified
        ↓
What controls were considered
        ↓
Why controls were insufficient
        ↓
What alternatives were evaluated
        ↓
Why avoidance was selected
        ↓
Who approved the decision
```

This provides defensible governance.

---

### 98. Evidence Supporting Avoidance

Evidence may include:

* AI risk assessment.
* Security assessment.
* Privacy assessment.
* Architecture review.
* Model evaluation.
* Vendor assessment.
* Legal assessment.
* Business impact analysis.
* Risk committee decision.
* Alternative solution analysis.

The exact evidence depends on the use case.

---

### 99. Avoidance and Accountability

The decision should have an accountable owner.

Avoidance should not be:

> "GRC rejected the project."

Instead:

> "The authorized risk owner decided that the current use case should not proceed because the assessed residual risk exceeds the organization's approved tolerance."

This reinforces management accountability.

---

### 100. Part 2 Key Takeaways

AI risk avoidance assessment should establish whether an AI activity can operate within acceptable risk boundaries.

The core process is:

```text
BUSINESS OBJECTIVE
        ↓
AI USE CASE
        ↓
RISK SCENARIO
        ↓
INHERENT RISK
        ↓
CONTROL FEASIBILITY
        ↓
CONTROL EFFECTIVENESS
        ↓
RESIDUAL RISK
        ↓
RISK APPETITE
        ↓
TREAT / REDESIGN / AVOID
```

Key principles:

1. Understand the business objective first.
2. Determine whether AI is actually necessary.
3. Define the AI use case precisely.
4. Identify concrete risk scenarios.
5. Assess inherent risk.
6. Evaluate available controls.
7. Assess control feasibility.
8. Assess control effectiveness.
9. Determine residual risk.
10. Compare residual risk with risk appetite.
11. Consider safer alternatives.
12. Consider reducing AI scope.
13. Consider restricting capabilities.
14. Consider restricting data.
15. Consider restricting users.
16. Consider changing architecture.
17. Consider changing providers.
18. Distinguish temporary from permanent avoidance.
19. Establish hard-stop conditions where appropriate.
20. Document the decision and its rationale.
21. Assign accountable decision authority.
22. Define conditions under which an avoided use case could be reconsidered.
23. Ensure the decision is auditable.

The central question is:

> **"Can the organization's business objective be achieved without exposing the organization to unacceptable AI risk?"**

# 06.09 — AI Risk Avoidance

## Part 3 — AI Risk Avoidance Implementation, Governance, and Enterprise Application

### 101. Implementing an AI Risk Avoidance Decision

Once an organization decides to avoid an AI activity, the decision must be translated into practical controls and governance actions.

A decision such as:

> "This AI use case will not be permitted."

must be supported by mechanisms that actually prevent the activity from occurring.

A practical implementation model is:

```text
Avoidance Decision
       ↓
Define Prohibition / Restriction
       ↓
Assign Responsibility
       ↓
Implement Preventive Controls
       ↓
Monitor Compliance
       ↓
Handle Exceptions
       ↓
Review Decision
```

---

### 102. Avoidance Policy

Organizations can formally define AI activities that are not permitted.

An AI policy may establish:

* Prohibited use cases.
* Restricted capabilities.
* Restricted data.
* Restricted users.
* Required approvals.
* Technical enforcement requirements.
* Exception procedures.

The policy converts risk decisions into organizational rules.

---

### 103. Prohibited AI Use Cases

A prohibited-use register can provide a centralized record.

Example:

| Use Case                                                   | Status     | Reason                     |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------- | -------------------------- |
| Unapproved autonomous critical-system actions              | Prohibited | Excessive operational risk |
| Uploading restricted information to unapproved AI services | Prohibited | Data exposure              |
| Automated high-impact decisions without required oversight | Prohibited | Governance risk            |

The examples are illustrative.

---

### 104. Preventive Enforcement

An avoidance decision is stronger when technical controls prevent prohibited activity.

Examples include:

* Application allowlisting.
* Network restrictions.
* Identity controls.
* DLP.
* API restrictions.
* Endpoint controls.
* Cloud access controls.
* CASB controls.
* AI gateway policies.

The objective is:

> **Make the prohibited activity technically difficult or impossible where practical.**

---

### 105. Administrative Enforcement

Technical controls should be supported by administrative controls.

Examples:

* AI acceptable-use policy.
* Employee training.
* Manager accountability.
* Approval workflows.
* Vendor requirements.
* Disciplinary procedures.
* Exception management.

This creates multiple enforcement layers.

---

### 106. Technical and Administrative Alignment

A mature governance model combines:

```text
Policy
   +
Process
   +
People
   +
Technology
```

If policy prohibits an activity but technical controls allow unrestricted execution, the avoidance decision may be difficult to enforce.

---

### 107. AI Access Governance

Avoidance may require controlling access to AI capabilities.

An organization can implement:

```text
User
 ↓
Identity Verification
 ↓
Authorization
 ↓
AI Capability
 ↓
Approved / Denied
```

This can prevent unauthorized users from accessing prohibited AI functionality.

---

### 108. Role-Based AI Restrictions

Different roles may have different AI permissions.

Example:

| Role                       | AI Capability                |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| General User               | Standard productivity AI     |
| Developer                  | Approved development AI      |
| Administrator              | Restricted                   |
| Privileged Operator        | Additional approval          |
| High-impact Decision Maker | Specific governance controls |

The exact model depends on organizational requirements.

---

### 109. Data-Level Enforcement

Avoidance may require preventing specific data types from entering AI systems.

Controls can include:

* Data classification.
* DLP.
* Content inspection.
* Data masking.
* Tokenization.
* API filtering.
* Upload restrictions.

The objective is to prevent prohibited data processing.

---

### 110. Example — Sensitive Data Restriction

Suppose organizational policy prohibits employees from sending highly sensitive information to an external AI service.

A technical enforcement model might be:

```text id="jv3b2k"
User
 ↓
AI Service
 ↓
DLP Inspection
 ↓
Sensitive Data Detected
 ↓
Request Blocked
```

This provides stronger assurance than policy language alone.

---

### 111. Capability-Level Enforcement

AI platforms may expose multiple capabilities.

For example:

```text
AI Platform
 ├── Text Generation
 ├── File Access
 ├── Web Access
 ├── Code Execution
 ├── Tool Calling
 └── Autonomous Actions
```

The organization may allow some capabilities while avoiding others.

This enables **granular risk avoidance**.

---

### 112. Autonomous Action Restrictions

A particularly important control is restricting autonomous actions.

For example:

```text
AI
 ↓
Recommendation
 ↓
Human Approval
 ↓
Execution
```

instead of:

```text
AI
 ↓
Decision
 ↓
Automatic Execution
```

The organization can therefore avoid the risk associated with unrestricted autonomy.

---

### 113. Environment Segmentation

AI functionality may be permitted in controlled environments but prohibited in sensitive environments.

For example:

```text
Development → Allowed
Test → Controlled
Production → Restricted
Critical Production → Prohibited
```

This can provide a practical risk boundary.

---

### 114. Network Segmentation

Network controls can support AI risk avoidance.

For example:

```text
AI Environment
      │
      ├── Approved Systems
      │
      └── Critical Systems → Blocked
```

This prevents an AI system from interacting with systems that create unacceptable exposure.

---

### 115. Privilege Restriction

Avoidance may require limiting AI privileges.

For example:

> An AI application may read approved information but cannot execute administrative commands.

This removes a high-risk capability from the AI system.

---

### 116. API-Level Restrictions

Organizations can restrict AI integrations through APIs.

Examples:

* Block specific endpoints.
* Restrict API scopes.
* Limit tool access.
* Restrict write operations.
* Require approval for sensitive actions.
* Block access to protected systems.

This allows technical enforcement of governance decisions.

---

### 117. AI Gateway Controls

An enterprise AI gateway can potentially provide centralized enforcement.

A simplified architecture:

```text
Users / Applications
        ↓
    AI Gateway
        ↓
Policy Evaluation
        ↓
 ┌──────┴──────┐
Allow          Block
 ↓               ↓
AI Service     Request Denied
```

This can centralize AI access governance.

---

### 118. Monitoring Avoided Activities

Avoidance should be monitored.

Monitoring may identify:

* Unauthorized AI applications.
* Attempts to access prohibited services.
* Restricted data transfers.
* Unauthorized AI integrations.
* Attempts to bypass controls.
* Unapproved autonomous functions.

This supports continuous enforcement.

---

### 119. Shadow AI Detection

Employees may use AI services outside approved channels.

For example:

```text
Employee
    ↓
Unapproved AI Tool
    ↓
Sensitive Information
```

Organizations can use monitoring and discovery processes to identify such activity.

The objective is to determine:

> What AI is actually being used?

rather than relying solely on approved application inventories.

---

### 120. AI Discovery

AI governance programs can maintain an inventory of:

* AI applications.
* AI models.
* AI providers.
* Business owners.
* Data sources.
* Users.
* Integrations.
* Risk classifications.

This helps identify systems that should be subject to avoidance requirements.

---

### 121. AI Inventory and Avoidance

An AI inventory can identify:

```text
AI System
   ↓
Use Case
   ↓
Risk Classification
   ↓
Allowed / Restricted / Prohibited
```

This creates traceability between governance requirements and deployed systems.

---

### 122. Exception Management

A mature avoidance program should define how exceptions are handled.

A user should not simply bypass the prohibition.

Instead:

```text
Business Need
      ↓
Exception Request
      ↓
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Compensating Controls
      ↓
Authorized Decision
      ↓
Approved / Rejected
```

---

### 123. Exception Does Not Mean Automatic Approval

An exception process should not become a mechanism for routinely bypassing governance.

The requester should demonstrate:

* Business necessity.
* Specific use case.
* Risk.
* Proposed controls.
* Duration.
* Scope.
* Responsible owner.

---

### 124. Time-Bound Exceptions

Where exceptions are appropriate, they should generally have:

* Start date.
* End date.
* Scope.
* Owner.
* Conditions.
* Review requirements.

For example:

> Temporary approval for 30 days while an approved AI solution is evaluated.

This prevents temporary exceptions from becoming permanent uncontrolled practices.

---

### 125. Compensating Controls

A restricted AI activity may sometimes be permitted if alternative safeguards sufficiently reduce exposure.

Examples:

* Isolated environment.
* Synthetic data.
* Human approval.
* Restricted access.
* Enhanced monitoring.
* Limited functionality.

The compensating controls should be documented and tested.

---

### 126. Avoidance and Risk Acceptance

An important governance distinction:

> An employee or project team should not independently override a formal avoidance decision simply because they are willing to accept the risk.

Risk acceptance must occur through the organization's defined authority.

This protects the integrity of the risk framework.

---

### 127. Unauthorized Override

An unauthorized override may create:

* Policy violation.
* Security exposure.
* Compliance issues.
* Accountability problems.
* Audit findings.

The organization should have mechanisms to detect and address unauthorized deviations.

---

### 128. Governance Committee Role

An AI governance or risk committee may:

* Review high-risk use cases.
* Approve restrictions.
* Review exceptions.
* Monitor prohibited activities.
* Review material incidents.
* Reassess avoidance decisions.

The committee should operate according to clearly defined authority.

---

### 129. Business Owner Responsibility

The business owner should understand that avoiding a use case does not necessarily mean abandoning the business objective.

The owner can work with GRC to identify:

* Alternative technologies.
* Reduced scope.
* Safer architectures.
* Manual alternatives.
* Controlled AI implementations.

This supports responsible innovation.

---

### 130. GRC Responsibility

GRC can provide:

* Risk methodology.
* Risk criteria.
* Governance requirements.
* Assessment templates.
* Decision records.
* Exception procedures.
* Monitoring requirements.
* Reporting.

GRC should facilitate the decision rather than automatically making every business decision itself.

---

### 131. Security Responsibility

Security teams may assess:

* Architecture.
* Access.
* Data protection.
* Threat exposure.
* Vulnerabilities.
* Monitoring.
* Integration risks.

Security findings may support a decision to avoid a particular AI architecture or capability.

---

### 132. Privacy Responsibility

Privacy functions may assess:

* Data use.
* Purpose.
* Data minimization.
* Processing risks.
* User rights.
* Data flows.

If the intended processing cannot be appropriately governed, privacy findings may contribute to avoidance.

---

### 133. Legal Responsibility

Legal teams may assess:

* Applicable legal requirements.
* Contractual restrictions.
* Liability.
* Intellectual-property considerations.
* Regulatory requirements.

Legal conclusions can form part of the evidence supporting an avoidance decision.

---

### 134. Architecture Review

Enterprise architecture should assess whether the proposed design creates unnecessary exposure.

Questions may include:

* Can the AI system be isolated?
* Can sensitive data be excluded?
* Can privileges be reduced?
* Can external connectivity be removed?
* Can a safer architecture achieve the same objective?

Architecture review can therefore identify ways to avoid specific risks without abandoning the overall business goal.

---

### 135. Avoidance Through Secure Architecture

Consider:

```text
Unsafe Design
AI → Production Database → Write Access

Alternative
AI → Controlled Interface → Read-Only Data
                         ↓
                    Human Approval
```

The second architecture may remove several risk-producing characteristics of the first.

---

### 136. Avoidance and Third-Party Selection

A vendor can also be rejected when it cannot satisfy required governance conditions.

Assessment areas can include:

* Security.
* Privacy.
* AI governance.
* Data handling.
* Resilience.
* Contractual requirements.
* Transparency.
* Subprocessors.

The decision can therefore be:

> Avoid this provider.

rather than:

> Avoid the entire AI use case.

---

### 137. Vendor Exit

If a deployed vendor becomes unacceptable, the organization may need to discontinue the service.

Potential triggers include:

* Material security failure.
* Repeated contractual violations.
* Unacceptable model changes.
* Loss of required assurance.
* Significant service degradation.
* Inability to meet governance requirements.

This requires an operational exit strategy.

---

### 138. AI Service Suspension

Avoidance does not always require immediate permanent retirement.

A service may be:

* Suspended.
* Restricted.
* Isolated.
* Disabled temporarily.

For example:

> Suspend an AI integration until a critical security issue is remediated.

This provides an emergency risk-treatment mechanism.

---

### 139. Emergency Avoidance

An organization should be capable of rapidly disabling AI functionality when a critical risk emerges.

A simplified process:

```text
Critical Risk
     ↓
Emergency Assessment
     ↓
Authorized Decision
     ↓
Disable / Isolate
     ↓
Investigate
     ↓
Remediate or Retire
```

This is particularly important for systems with significant operational impact.

---

### 140. Kill Switch Concept

Critical AI systems may benefit from a mechanism to rapidly disable AI functionality.

A kill switch could be:

* Administrative.
* Application-level.
* API-level.
* Network-level.

The objective is to provide a controlled means of stopping AI activity when necessary.

---

### 141. Fail-Safe Design

Where AI failure could create significant consequences, the organization can design systems to fail safely.

For example:

```text
AI Failure
    ↓
Automatic Stop
    ↓
Manual Process
```

rather than:

```text
AI Failure
    ↓
Continue Uncontrolled
```

Fail-safe design can support avoidance of catastrophic failure modes.

---

### 142. Avoidance and Business Continuity

If an AI system is disabled, the organization needs to know:

> What happens next?

Possible alternatives include:

* Manual processing.
* Backup system.
* Alternative provider.
* Traditional software.
* Reduced service.

Avoidance should therefore be considered alongside continuity planning.

---

### 143. Avoidance and Disaster Recovery

AI services may be part of disaster-recovery planning.

The organization should determine whether:

* AI is essential.
* AI can be temporarily disabled.
* A fallback process exists.
* A secondary provider exists.
* Data can be recovered.
* Critical operations can continue without AI.

This determines whether AI dependence itself creates unacceptable exposure.

---

### 144. Avoidance and Operational Resilience

Operational resilience asks:

> Can the business continue if the AI capability is unavailable?

If the answer is no, the organization should understand the dependency before making AI a critical operational component.

---

### 145. Avoidance and Vendor Concentration

If several business processes depend on the same AI provider, a provider failure can create systemic exposure.

A risk-aware organization may therefore:

* Limit concentration.
* Use multiple providers.
* Maintain fallback capabilities.
* Avoid making nonessential processes dependent on the same provider.

---

### 146. Avoidance and AI Change Management

Every material AI change should potentially trigger a governance review.

Examples:

* New model.
* New data.
* New integration.
* New users.
* New geography.
* New functionality.
* Increased autonomy.

The question is:

> **Does the change create a risk that the organization previously decided to avoid?**

### 167. Avoidance and Data Minimization

```text
Send Everything to AI
```

use:

```text
Identify Required Data
      ↓
Exclude Unnecessary Data
      ↓
Process Minimum Necessary Information
```

This can remove entire categories of exposure.

---

### 168. Avoidance and Least Privilege

The principle of least privilege can also support avoidance.

Instead of allowing:

> AI → unrestricted enterprise access

use:

> AI → minimum required access.

This prevents certain high-impact actions from becoming possible.

---

### 169. Avoidance and Defense in Depth

Risk avoidance should not stand alone.

A mature architecture may combine:

```text
Avoid
  +
Restrict
  +
Detect
  +
Respond
  +
Recover
```

Avoidance removes unnecessary exposure, while other controls address risks that remain.

---

### 170. Enterprise Decision Principle

A mature AI governance program should recognize:

> **Not every technically possible AI capability should be deployed.**

Technology capability and governance permission are different concepts.

```text
Technically Possible
        ≠
Organizationally Acceptable
```

This is one of the most important principles in AI risk governance.

---

### 171. Strategic Avoidance

Strategic avoidance occurs when management deliberately chooses not to enter a particular AI risk area.

For example:

> An organization may decide that a particular category of autonomous AI is outside its strategic risk appetite.

This is a portfolio-level decision rather than an individual technical control.

---

### 172. Portfolio-Level Avoidance

Organizations may decide:

```text
AI Portfolio
 ├── Productivity → Pursue
 ├── Customer Support → Pursue
 ├── Analytics → Pursue
 └── High-Risk Autonomous Decisions → Avoid
```

This helps align AI investment with enterprise strategy.

---

### 173. Avoidance and Investment Decisions

Risk avoidance can influence investment.

Before funding an AI initiative, management can ask:

* Is the use case acceptable?
* Is the risk manageable?
* Is there a safer alternative?
* Does the initiative fit risk appetite?
* Is the expected value sufficient?

A project that fails these criteria may not receive investment.

---

### 174. AI Risk Avoidance Governance Model

A mature model can be summarized as:

```text
                  AI Strategy
                      ↓
                AI Use Case
                      ↓
                Risk Assessment
                      ↓
          ┌───────────┴───────────┐
          ↓                       ↓
      Acceptable              Unacceptable
          ↓                       ↓
       Controls              Can Redesign?
                                  ↓
                           ┌──────┴──────┐
                           ↓             ↓
                          Yes            No
                           ↓             ↓

                           ↓

```

### 175. Part 3 Key Takeaways

AI risk avoidance becomes effective only when the organization can translate a governance decision into operational enforcement.

Key principles:

1. Avoidance decisions require implementation.
2. Policies should clearly define prohibited activities.
3. Technical controls should enforce important restrictions.
4. Administrative controls should support technical enforcement.
5. AI access should be governed.
6. Data restrictions can prevent specific AI risks.
7. Capability restrictions can remove high-risk functionality.
8. Autonomous actions can be restricted.
9. Network and environment segmentation can support avoidance.
10. Privilege should be limited.
11. API access can be restricted.
12. AI gateways can centralize enforcement.
13. Unauthorized AI activity should be monitored.
14. Shadow AI should be considered.
15. AI inventories support governance.
16. Exceptions should follow formal approval.
17. Exceptions should generally be limited in scope and duration.
18. Risk acceptance should not be used to bypass formal avoidance decisions.
19. Business owners remain accountable for their AI use cases.
20. GRC provides governance and challenge.
21. Security, privacy, legal, and architecture functions provide specialized assessment.
22. Critical AI functionality may require emergency suspension capabilities.
23. AI systems should have appropriate fallback mechanisms.
24. Vendor failure may justify discontinuing a particular AI service.
25. Material AI changes should trigger reassessment.
26. Avoidance decisions should be documented.
27. Avoidance controls should be tested.
28. Unauthorized activity should trigger root-cause analysis.
29. Approved alternatives can improve compliance.
30. Avoidance should support responsible innovation rather than unnecessarily prevent it.
31. Avoidance can operate at the use-case, capability, data, architecture, vendor, environment, or portfolio level.
32. The objective is not to eliminate every AI risk.
33. The objective is to **prevent exposure that the organization has determined it should not undertake**.

The core implementation principle is:

> **A risk avoidance decision is effective only when the organization can demonstrate that the prohibited or unacceptable AI activity is actually prevented, restricted, or discontinued in practice.**

# 06.09 — AI Risk Avoidance

## Part 4 — Advanced AI Risk Avoidance, GRC Practice, and Interview Application

### 176. AI Risk Avoidance as a Governance Decision

At an advanced GRC level, AI risk avoidance should be understood as a **management decision about whether the organization is willing to be exposed to a particular AI risk**.

It is not merely a technical decision.

The organization is effectively deciding:

> "This activity does not provide sufficient value, cannot be controlled adequately, or falls outside our acceptable risk boundaries; therefore, we will not undertake it."

This distinction is important because GRC professionals must be able to explain **why an organization should stop an activity**, not merely how to control it.

---

### 177. The Risk Avoidance Hierarchy

AI risk avoidance can be viewed at multiple levels:

```text
Enterprise
   ↓
Business Portfolio
   ↓
AI Program
   ↓
AI Use Case
   ↓
AI Capability
   ↓
AI Component
```

Avoidance can occur at any of these levels.

For example:

* Enterprise → avoid an entire category of AI.
* Portfolio → avoid high-impact autonomous AI.
* Use case → avoid AI-based employee decisions.
* Capability → avoid autonomous execution.
* Component → remove an unsafe integration.

---

### 178. Avoidance at the Enterprise Level

Enterprise-level avoidance is normally associated with strategic risk appetite.

Management may decide that certain AI activities are fundamentally incompatible with the organization's:

* Risk appetite.
* Strategic objectives.
* Regulatory obligations.
* Ethical commitments.
* Operational capabilities.

This becomes an enterprise governance principle rather than an individual project decision.

---

### 179. Avoidance at the Business-Process Level

An organization may decide:

> "AI will not be used to make the final decision in this business process."

AI may still be used for:

* Analysis.
* Recommendations.
* Classification.
* Administrative assistance.

The high-impact decision itself remains outside the AI system.

This is a form of **process-level risk avoidance**.

---

### 180. Avoidance at the Capability Level

Sometimes the risk comes from a particular AI capability rather than the overall system.

For example:

```text id="j3e9bn"
AI Platform
 ├── Generate Content → Allowed
 ├── Analyze Data → Allowed
 ├── Access Sensitive Data → Restricted
 └── Execute Critical Actions → Avoided
```

This provides a more precise governance boundary.

---

### 181. Avoidance at the Component Level

A specific component may introduce unacceptable exposure.

Examples:

* External plugin.
* Autonomous agent.
* Third-party API.
* Untrusted model.
* High-privilege integration.

The organization can remove that component while retaining the rest of the solution.

---

### 182. Avoidance vs Elimination of Risk

Risk avoidance does not always eliminate every risk associated with the business process.

Instead, it eliminates or prevents a **specific source of unacceptable exposure**.

Example:

> Removing autonomous AI execution does not eliminate all operational risk, but it eliminates the specific risk created by autonomous execution.

This distinction is important in risk analysis.

---

### 183. Avoidance and Risk Source Removal

A useful GRC principle is:

> **If the source of risk can be removed without eliminating the business objective, removing it may be preferable to controlling it indefinitely.**

For example:

```text id="t8n1df"
Risk Source
   ↓
Autonomous Execution
   ↓
Remove Capability
   ↓
AI Remains
   ↓
Human Execution
```

---

### 184. Avoidance by Design

Avoidance can be incorporated into system design from the beginning.

Instead of building a capability and later attempting to secure it:

```text id="8k09zn"
Build
 ↓
Discover Risk
 ↓
Restrict
```

the organization can use:

```text id="7l4n3x"
Identify Risk
 ↓
Remove Unsafe Capability
 ↓
Build Safer Design
```

This is a more proactive approach.

---

### 185. Avoidance and Secure-by-Architecture

A mature architecture asks:

> "What functionality should the AI system never have?"

Examples:

* No direct administrative access.
* No unrestricted internet access.
* No access to restricted information.
* No autonomous financial transactions.
* No direct changes to critical systems.

These boundaries reduce the number of risk scenarios that need to be managed.

---

### 186. Avoidance and Attack Surface

Every additional AI capability can potentially increase attack surface.

For example:

```text id="c9p6pi"
AI
 ↓
Tools
 ↓
APIs
 ↓
Databases
 ↓
Production Systems
```

Removing unnecessary integrations can reduce exposure.

The principle is:

> **Do not provide an AI capability simply because the technology allows it.**

---

### 187. Avoidance and Least Functionality

A related security principle is **least functionality**.

The AI system should have only the capabilities necessary for its approved purpose.

Example:

> A customer-service chatbot may need access to product information but not administrative access to enterprise infrastructure.

Unnecessary capability can be avoided.

---

### 188. Avoidance and Least Privilege

Least privilege answers:

> "What access does the AI need?"

Avoidance asks:

> **"Does the AI need this capability at all?"**

This distinction is important.

If a capability is unnecessary, removing it is often stronger than merely restricting it.

---

### 189. Avoidance and Defense-in-Depth

Avoidance should not be viewed as an alternative to every other control.

A mature architecture may use:

```text id="x3jq8a"
Avoid unnecessary capability
          ↓
Restrict remaining capabilities
          ↓
Monitor activity
          ↓
Detect abnormal behavior
          ↓
Respond to incidents
```

Avoidance reduces the number of risks that remain to be managed.

---

### 190. Avoidance and Threat Modeling

Threat modeling can identify capabilities that should not exist.

For example:

```text id="z9o9u0"
AI Capability
      ↓
Threat Scenario
      ↓
Potential Impact
      ↓
Can Capability Be Removed?
      ↙             ↘
    Yes              No
     ↓                ↓
  Avoid          Apply Controls
```

This integrates risk avoidance into security engineering.

---

### 191. Avoidance and Abuse Cases

GRC and security teams can identify potential abuse cases.

Examples:

* Prompt manipulation.
* Unauthorized data retrieval.
* Excessive tool access.
* Malicious autonomous actions.
* Data exfiltration.

If a capability is unnecessary and materially enables an abuse case, the organization can remove the capability.

---

### 192. Avoidance and Attack Paths

An AI system can create attack paths such as:

```text id="b8c3gy"
Attacker
   ↓
AI Interface
   ↓
Prompt Manipulation
   ↓
Tool Access
   ↓
Privileged API
   ↓
Critical System
```

One effective strategy is to eliminate unnecessary links in the attack path.

For example:

> Remove the AI's privileged API access.

This can be more effective than attempting to control every possible prompt.

---

### 193. Avoidance and System Boundaries

AI governance should establish clear system boundaries.

Questions include:

* What can the AI access?
* What can it modify?
* What can it execute?
* What can it communicate with?
* What data can it process?
* What decisions can it influence?

Anything outside the legitimate business requirement should be considered for removal or prohibition.

---

### 194. Avoidance and High-Impact Decisions

A high-impact AI use case requires particularly careful consideration.

Examples can include decisions affecting:

* Employment.
* Financial outcomes.
* Access to important services.
* Safety.
* Security.
* Individual rights.

Where the organization cannot establish sufficient governance, avoiding automated decision-making may be appropriate.

---

### 195. Avoidance and Human-in-the-Loop

Human involvement can reduce certain risks, but the organization should avoid **false assurance**.

A process should not be considered adequately controlled merely because a human is technically present.

The human must be able to:

* Understand the AI output.
* Challenge it.
* Reject it.
* Override it.
* Investigate unusual cases.

If these conditions cannot be met, the use case may still require avoidance.

---

### 196. Avoidance and Human-on-the-Loop

A human who only monitors AI activity after decisions have been made may have limited ability to prevent harm.

Therefore, GRC should distinguish:

```text
Human Present
      ≠
Meaningful Human Control
```

The actual authority and intervention capability matter.

---

### 197. Avoidance and Explainability

If stakeholders cannot reasonably understand how an AI system reaches consequential outputs, this may create governance problems.

Where explanation is essential and cannot be adequately provided, the organization may avoid using the AI system for that particular decision.

---

### 198. Avoidance and Model Uncertainty

AI models can behave unpredictably outside their expected operating conditions.

If the organization cannot sufficiently understand or constrain that uncertainty for a critical use case, management may decide not to deploy the model there.

The key principle is:

> **Uncertainty itself can be a risk factor.**

---

### 199. Avoidance and Model Drift

AI behavior may change as:

* Data changes.
* Models are updated.
* Providers modify services.
* User behavior changes.
* Operating environments change.

If the organization cannot maintain sufficient control over these changes, continued use may become unacceptable.

---

### 200. Avoidance and Third-Party Model Changes

External AI providers may modify:

* Models.
* Features.
* Training methods.
* Infrastructure.
* Data-processing practices.

If material changes occur without adequate organizational control, the organization may restrict or discontinue the service.

---

### 201. Avoidance and Unknown Dependencies

AI systems can depend on:

* Foundation models.
* Cloud services.
* APIs.
* Data providers.
* Plugins.
* Open-source components.

If a critical dependency cannot be sufficiently understood or governed, it may become a reason for avoidance.

---

### 202. Avoidance and Supply-Chain Risk

AI supply chains can be complex:

```text id="qf2xjy"
Organization
    ↓
AI Application
    ↓
AI Provider
    ↓
Foundation Model
    ↓
Cloud Infrastructure
    ↓
Data / Third Parties
```

The organization may not control every layer.

If critical risk cannot be sufficiently governed across the chain, avoidance may be appropriate.

---

### 203. Avoidance and Concentration Risk

Heavy dependence on one AI provider can create concentration risk.

For example:

> Multiple critical business processes depend on one AI provider.

Management may decide to avoid making additional processes dependent on that provider.

This limits systemic exposure.

---

### 204. Avoidance and Strategic Dependency

An organization should distinguish between:

> Using AI as a tool

and:

> Becoming operationally dependent on AI.

If AI becomes essential to a critical process, the organization may inherit additional:

* Availability risk.
* Vendor risk.
* Cost risk.
* Change risk.
* Exit risk.

Avoidance can be used to prevent unnecessary dependency.

---

### 205. Avoidance and Exit Strategy

Before adopting a critical AI service, organizations should consider:

> **"If we need to stop using this service tomorrow, can we?"**

If the answer is no, the organization may have created excessive dependency.

A practical governance requirement is therefore to understand:

* Data portability.
* Alternative providers.
* Manual fallback.
* Contract termination.
* Migration effort.

---

### 206. Avoidance and Lock-In

Vendor lock-in can become an AI governance concern.

For example:

```text id="k1v4ab"
AI Provider
    ↓
Proprietary Model
    ↓
Proprietary APIs
    ↓
Business-Critical Process
```

If migration becomes extremely difficult, future risk increases.

The organization may avoid unnecessary lock-in through architectural decisions.

---

### 207. Avoidance and Cost Risk

AI risk is not limited to cybersecurity or compliance.

Uncontrolled AI usage can create:

* Unexpected compute costs.
* API consumption costs.
* Scaling costs.
* Licensing costs.

If the economics of a use case are fundamentally unfavorable, avoiding the AI deployment may be appropriate.

---

### 208. Avoidance and Operational Complexity

AI introduces governance and operational requirements.

These may include:

* Monitoring.
* Model evaluation.
* Data management.
* Security testing.
* Human oversight.
* Vendor management.
* Change management.

If the organization cannot support these requirements, the AI use case may be inappropriate.

---

### 209. AI Governance Capability as a Constraint

An organization should ask:

> **"Do we have the capability to govern this AI system responsibly?"**

Required capabilities may include:

* Skilled personnel.
* Security expertise.
* Data governance.
* AI governance.
* Monitoring.
* Incident response.
* Risk management.

If the required governance capability does not exist and cannot reasonably be established, avoidance may be necessary.

---

### 210. Avoidance and Organizational Maturity

The same AI use case may be acceptable for a mature organization but inappropriate for an organization without the necessary governance capabilities.

Therefore:

> **Risk is partly determined by organizational capability.**

---

### 211. AI Risk Avoidance and Three Lines Model

AI avoidance decisions can involve the three lines model.

**First line — Business / Operations**

Owns the AI activity and associated risks.

**Second line — Risk / Compliance / GRC**

Provides oversight, challenge, methodology, and monitoring.

**Third line — Internal Audit**

Provides independent assurance.

This helps maintain appropriate separation of responsibilities.

---

### 212. Avoidance and Risk Ownership

The risk owner should ultimately understand:

> What risk is being avoided and why?

The risk owner should not delegate accountability entirely to GRC.

GRC supports the decision-making process but management remains responsible for the risk.

---

### 213. Avoidance and Board-Level Risk

Some AI risks may be significant enough to reach executive or board-level governance.

Examples:

* Material regulatory exposure.
* Significant safety implications.
* Major customer impact.
* Enterprise-wide operational dependency.
* Strategic reputational risk.

The decision to avoid such activities may therefore require senior governance approval.

---

### 214. Risk Appetite Statement

An organization can express AI boundaries through its risk appetite.

For example:

> "The organization has zero tolerance for unauthorized AI processing of restricted information."

or:

> "The organization does not permit autonomous AI execution of defined critical operational activities."

Such statements provide strategic direction for specific avoidance decisions.

---

### 215. Zero-Tolerance Areas

Zero tolerance should be used carefully.

It may be appropriate for clearly defined conditions where the organization cannot accept exposure.

Examples:

* Certain prohibited data processing.
* Unauthorized critical-system access.
* Clearly prohibited AI activities.

The condition must be precisely defined to avoid ambiguity.

---

### 216. Risk Criteria vs Risk Appetite

These should not be confused.

**Risk criteria**

> How the organization assesses risk.

**Risk appetite**

> How much risk the organization is willing to accept.

**Avoidance**

> A treatment decision when the organization determines the activity should not be undertaken or continued.

These concepts work together.

---

### 217. Avoidance and Risk-Based Prioritization

Not every AI risk requires avoidance.

GRC should prioritize based on:

* Severity.
* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Exposure.
* Regulatory significance.
* Business criticality.
* Control effectiveness.

Avoidance should be proportionate to the risk.

---

### 218. Avoidance and Proportionality

A low-risk AI application should not automatically receive the same restrictions as a high-impact AI system.

For example:

```text id="x4x3f4"
Internal Writing Assistant
        ↓
Low Impact
        ↓
Standard Controls

Autonomous Critical-System Agent
        ↓
Very High Impact
        ↓
Potential Avoidance
```

Governance should remain risk-based.

---

### 219. Avoidance and Business Value

Avoidance decisions should consider both:

```text
Risk
+
Business Value
```

A high-risk activity with minimal business value is a strong candidate for avoidance.

A high-value activity with high risk may justify investment in safer architecture and stronger controls.

---

### 220. Avoidance and Opportunity Cost

Stopping an AI project can also create costs.

Potential consequences include:

* Lost efficiency.
* Competitive disadvantage.
* Delayed innovation.
* Additional manual work.

Therefore, management should understand both the:

> **Risk of proceeding**

and the:

> **Risk of not proceeding.**

---

### 221. Avoidance Is Not Automatically the Safest Business Decision

Avoiding an AI system may introduce other risks.

For example:

> A manual process may have significant operational error and staffing risks.

Therefore, the organization should compare alternatives rather than assuming:

> "No AI = No risk."

---

### 222. Comparative Risk Analysis

A useful approach is:

| Option     | Benefit  | Risk     | Operational Burden |
| ---------- | -------- | -------- | ------------------ |
| AI         | High     | High     | Moderate           |
| Manual     | Moderate | Moderate | High               |
| Rule-Based | High     | Low      | Moderate           |

The organization can compare alternatives using consistent criteria.

---

### 223. Avoidance and Risk Optimization

The objective is not necessarily:

> Minimize AI risk at any cost.

The objective is:

> **Optimize business value while keeping risk within acceptable boundaries.**

Avoidance becomes appropriate when the balance cannot reasonably be achieved.

---

### 224. Executive Decision Language

For executive communication, avoid highly technical language where possible.

Instead of:

> "The model's attack surface contains several unresolved privilege-escalation vectors."

A GRC executive summary might state:

> "The proposed architecture creates a level of privileged access that cannot currently be reduced to an acceptable risk level."

This communicates the governance implication.

---

### 225. Avoidance Recommendation Structure

A strong recommendation can follow:

**Situation**

> The organization proposes an AI capability.

**Risk**

> The capability introduces material exposure.

**Assessment**

> Existing controls cannot sufficiently reduce the exposure.

**Alternatives**

> Safer alternatives were evaluated.

**Recommendation**

> Avoid the current implementation.

**Condition**

> Reconsider if defined controls or architecture improvements become available.

---

### 226. Example — Autonomous IT Operations

**Proposal**

> AI agent can automatically modify network configurations.

**Risk**

> Incorrect or manipulated AI actions could disrupt critical infrastructure.

**Assessment**

> Current controls cannot provide sufficient assurance for unrestricted autonomous execution.

**Decision**

> Avoid autonomous execution.

**Alternative**

> AI may generate configuration recommendations for authorized human approval.

This is a strong example of risk-informed design.

---

### 227. Example — Public AI and Confidential Data

**Proposal**

> Employees use a public AI service to analyze confidential corporate documents.

**Risk**

> Potential unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information.

**Assessment**

> The organization cannot establish sufficient control over the external processing environment.

**Decision**

> Avoid sending confidential information to the service.

**Alternative**

> Use an approved enterprise environment or sanitized information.

---

### 228. Example — High-Impact Automated Decision

**Proposal**

> AI automatically determines eligibility for a consequential business service.

**Risk**

> Incorrect or unfair decisions could create significant harm.

**Assessment**

> Required governance and oversight cannot currently be established.

**Decision**

> Avoid fully automated decision-making.

**Alternative**

> Use AI only as decision support with meaningful human authority.

---

### 229. Example — Third-Party AI Dependency

**Proposal**

> Make a critical customer service dependent on one external AI provider.

**Risk**

> Provider outage or material service change could disrupt operations.

**Assessment**

> No adequate fallback currently exists.

**Decision**

> Avoid making the process fully dependent on the provider.

**Alternative**

> Maintain an independent fallback process.

---

### 230. Example — AI Project Cancellation

Sometimes avoidance means cancelling a project entirely.

Example:

```text id="6sl9e8"
AI Project
   ↓
Critical Risks Identified
   ↓
Controls Evaluated
   ↓
Business Value Reassessed
   ↓
Risk > Value
   ↓
Project Cancelled
```

This is a legitimate risk-management outcome.

---

### 231. Avoidance Decision Quality

A high-quality avoidance decision should be:

* Evidence-based.
* Risk-based.
* Proportionate.
* Documented.
* Authorized.
* Traceable.
* Reviewable.
* Consistent with organizational policy.

It should not simply reflect an individual's personal concern.

---

### 232. Common Mistake — Avoid Everything

An immature governance program may respond:

> "AI is risky, so prohibit AI."

This is usually too broad.

A mature approach asks:

> Which specific AI activities create unacceptable risk?

---

### 233. Common Mistake — Never Avoid AI

The opposite mistake is:

> "We can control every risk."

This is also inappropriate.

Some risks may be:

* Too severe.
* Too uncertain.
* Too expensive to control.
* Legally prohibited.
* Fundamentally incompatible with the organization's objectives.

Avoidance must remain available as a legitimate treatment.

---

### 234. Common Mistake — Treating Policy as Enforcement

Another common mistake is:

> "The policy says employees cannot do it, therefore the risk is controlled."

Policy alone does not prove effective risk treatment.

The organization should determine whether the prohibition is:

* Communicated.
* Understood.
* Technically enforced where appropriate.
* Monitored.
* Audited.

---

### 235. Common Mistake — Ignoring Business Alternatives

A GRC team may say:

> "No."

without understanding what the business is actually trying to accomplish.

A better approach is:

> "This design should not proceed, but let's identify a safer way to achieve the business objective."

This positions GRC as a business-enabling function.

---

### 236. Common Mistake — Assuming Human Review Solves Everything

Adding a human reviewer does not automatically eliminate AI risk.

The organization should verify:

* Human authority.
* Human competence.
* Review quality.
* Workload.
* Timing.
* Override capability.

If these are inadequate, the risk may remain unacceptable.

---

### 237. Common Mistake — Ignoring Residual Risk

An organization may implement many controls and assume the risk is solved.

The correct question is:

> **What risk remains after treatment?**

If residual risk remains above tolerance, avoidance may still be necessary.

---

### 238. Common Mistake — Ignoring Change

A use case may be acceptable today but unacceptable after:

* New functionality.
* New data.
* New model.
* New provider.
* New integration.

Avoidance decisions must therefore remain connected to change governance.

---

### 239. Common Mistake — No Exit Strategy

Organizations sometimes deploy AI without considering:

> "How do we stop using it?"

A mature AI governance program should understand how an AI capability can be:

* Disabled.
* Replaced.
* Migrated.
* Isolated.
* Reverted to manual operation.

---

### 240. Common Mistake — Confusing Technical Feasibility With Governance Approval

A system may be technically capable of performing an action.

That does not mean the organization should allow it.

```text id="j8m2vw"
Technical Capability
        ≠
Governance Authorization
```

This distinction is central to AI governance.

---

### 241. Advanced GRC Interview Framework

When asked:

> **"How would you decide whether to avoid an AI risk?"**

A strong answer is:

> "I would first understand the business objective and define the specific AI use case. Then I would identify and assess the relevant risks, evaluate whether effective and practical controls can reduce those risks to within the organization's risk appetite, and consider safer alternatives or redesign options. If the residual risk remains unacceptable, or the activity is prohibited or cannot be reasonably controlled, I would recommend avoiding the activity. I would document the rationale, obtain the appropriate risk-owner approval, implement the necessary restrictions, and define conditions for future reassessment."

---

### 242. Advanced Interview Anchor Words

Remember:

**Business Objective → Use Case → Risk → Controls → Residual Risk → Appetite → Alternatives → Avoid → Authority → Enforcement → Review**

These anchor words allow you to reconstruct the full answer without memorizing every sentence.

---

A practical enterprise workflow can be:

```text id="i7v1dk"
          ↓

          ↓

          ↓

          ↓

          ↓

          ↓

    ├── Treat

          ↓

```

### 247. Risk Avoidance Maturity

A simplified maturity model:

AI risk avoidance is continuously monitored, measured, automated where appropriate, and aligned with enterprise strategy.

### 250. Final Principles of AI Risk Avoidance

The most important principles to retain are:

1. **Avoidance is a legitimate AI risk treatment.**
2. It means not undertaking, restricting, suspending, or discontinuing an activity that creates unacceptable risk.
3. Avoidance is different from reducing risk.
4. Avoidance is different from transferring risk.
5. Avoidance is different from accepting risk.
6. Avoidance can target a complete AI system.
7. It can target a use case.
8. It can target a capability.
9. It can target data.
10. It can target an architecture.
11. It can target a vendor.
12. It can target a dependency.
13. It can target a specific environment or user population.
14. Avoidance should be based on evidence.
15. Business objectives must be understood.
16. AI necessity should be considered.
17. Safer alternatives should be evaluated.
18. Inherent risk should be understood.
19. Controls should be evaluated for effectiveness and feasibility.
20. Residual risk should be compared with risk appetite.
21. Prohibited activities should have clear governance boundaries.
22. Avoidance decisions require appropriate authority.
23. Policy should be supported by technical enforcement where appropriate.
24. Exceptions should be formally governed.
25. Avoidance should be monitored.
26. Unauthorized activity should be detected.
27. Avoidance controls should be tested.
28. Critical AI systems should have appropriate suspension and fallback capabilities.
29. Material changes should trigger reassessment.
30. Previously avoided activities may be reconsidered when conditions change.
31. Avoidance should not automatically mean avoiding AI altogether.
32. The goal is to avoid **unacceptable exposure**, not eliminate innovation.
33. GRC should help the organization find safer ways to achieve legitimate business objectives.
34. Management remains accountable for the final risk decision.
35. The strongest avoidance strategy is often to **remove the unnecessary source of risk at the design level**.
