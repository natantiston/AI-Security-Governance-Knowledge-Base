# 06.04 — AI Risk Analysis

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Risk Analysis

### 01. Introduction to AI Risk Analysis

AI risk analysis is the process of determining the **nature and characteristics of identified AI risks** so that the organization can understand their significance.

Risk identification asks:

> **What could go wrong?**

Risk analysis asks:

> **How likely is it, how severe could it be, and what factors determine the level of exposure?**

A simplified relationship is:

```text
AI Risk Identified
       ↓
Risk Analysis
       ↓
Likelihood + Consequence + Uncertainty
       ↓
Understanding of Risk Level
```

Risk analysis therefore transforms a documented risk scenario into a more meaningful understanding of the organization's exposure.

---

### 02. Risk Identification vs Risk Analysis

These activities are related but different.

**Risk identification:**

> "The AI assistant may disclose confidential information to an unauthorized employee."

**Risk analysis:**

> "How plausible is this scenario, how frequently could it occur, how much information could be exposed, and how severe would the consequences be?"

The distinction can be represented as:

```text
Identification
"What can happen?"
        ↓
Analysis
"How could it happen and
how significant could it be?"
```

---

### 03. Purpose of Risk Analysis

AI risk analysis helps an organization:

* Understand the characteristics of identified risks.
* Estimate likelihood.
* Estimate potential consequences.
* Understand risk drivers.
* Identify uncertainty.
* Compare different risk scenarios.
* Support risk evaluation.
* Support management decision-making.
* Determine where additional analysis is required.

It provides the analytical foundation for informed risk decisions.

---

### 04. Why AI Risk Analysis Is Different

Traditional IT risk analysis can often rely on relatively stable systems and measurable failure patterns.

AI systems may introduce additional uncertainty because of:

* Probabilistic outputs.
* Model drift.
* Data variability.
* Non-deterministic behavior.
* Complex dependencies.
* Human interaction.
* Emergent behavior.
* Changing models.
* Changing external data.
* Uncertain future use.

Therefore, AI risk analysis often requires more than a simple:

```text
Likelihood × Impact
```

calculation.

---

### 05. The AI Risk Analysis Perspective

A mature analysis considers several dimensions:

```text
                 AI RISK
                    │
       ┌────────────┼────────────┐
       ↓            ↓            ↓
   Likelihood    Consequence   Uncertainty
       │            │            │
       └────────────┼────────────┘
                    ↓
              Risk Analysis
```

Additional factors may include:

* Exposure.
* Frequency.
* Duration.
* Scale.
* Detectability.
* Reversibility.
* Control effectiveness.
* Dependency.
* Human involvement.

---

### 06. Risk Analysis Starts With the Risk Scenario

Analysis should begin with a clearly defined scenario.

Example:

> An enterprise AI assistant may provide confidential financial information to an employee who does not have authorization to access the underlying documents.

This scenario provides the basis for analysis.

Without a reasonably specific scenario, numerical scoring can become arbitrary.

---

### 07. Analyze the Cause

The first analytical question is:

> **Why could the risk occur?**

Possible causes include:

* Weak authorization.
* Poor data classification.
* Incorrect configuration.
* Inadequate training data.
* Model limitations.
* User manipulation.
* Malicious input.
* Third-party failure.
* Human error.

Example:

```text
Cause:
Incorrect authorization enforcement

        ↓

Risk Event:
Unauthorized retrieval

        ↓

Consequence:
Confidentiality breach
```

Understanding the cause helps determine the plausibility of the event.

---

### 08. Analyze the Risk Event

The risk event is the event that actually creates exposure.

Examples:

* AI generates incorrect information.
* AI exposes confidential data.
* AI makes an unauthorized decision.
* AI executes an unintended transaction.
* AI becomes unavailable.
* AI produces discriminatory output.
* AI fails to detect a security event.

The event should be sufficiently specific to support analysis.

---

### 09. Analyze the Consequence

The next question is:

> **If the risk event occurs, what happens?**

Potential AI consequences include:

### Security

* Confidentiality loss.
* Integrity loss.
* Availability loss.

### Privacy

* Unauthorized disclosure.
* Excessive processing.
* Loss of control over personal data.

### Operational

* Service disruption.
* Incorrect business process.
* Increased manual workload.

### Financial

* Direct financial loss.
* Fraud.
* Compensation.
* Remediation cost.

### Legal and regulatory

* Regulatory action.
* Litigation.
* Contractual consequences.

### Human and social

* Physical harm.
* Psychological harm.
* Discrimination.
* Loss of opportunity.

### Reputational

* Customer distrust.
* Negative publicity.
* Loss of confidence.

---

### 10. Direct and Indirect Consequences

AI risk analysis should distinguish direct from indirect consequences.

Example:

```text
AI Error
   ↓
Incorrect Customer Decision
```

This is the direct consequence.

The indirect consequences could be:

```text
Incorrect Decision
       ↓
Customer Complaint
       ↓
Regulatory Investigation
       ↓
Financial Penalty
       ↓
Reputational Damage
```

A risk may therefore have a relatively small immediate consequence but significant downstream consequences.

---

### 11. Primary and Secondary Consequences

A similar distinction can be made between primary and secondary consequences.

**Primary:**

> AI incorrectly rejects a legitimate transaction.

**Secondary:**

> Customer experiences financial disruption.

**Further consequence:**

> Customer complaint and potential regulatory scrutiny.

Analysis should consider the entire reasonable consequence chain.

---

### 12. Likelihood Analysis

Likelihood represents the plausibility or probability that a risk scenario will occur within the defined context.

A basic qualitative scale might be:

| Level              | Description                       |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------- |
| 1 — Rare           | Highly unlikely                   |
| 2 — Unlikely       | Could occur but not expected      |
| 3 — Possible       | Could reasonably occur            |
| 4 — Likely         | Expected to occur                 |
| 5 — Almost Certain | Expected frequently or repeatedly |

The exact scale should be defined by the organization's risk methodology.

---

### 13. Likelihood Is Not the Same as Frequency

These concepts should not automatically be treated as identical.

**Frequency:**

> How often an event occurs.

**Likelihood:**

> How plausible it is that the event will occur within the assessment context.

For example, an AI failure may be low-frequency but still highly plausible during a particular condition.

---

### 14. Historical Evidence

Historical information can support likelihood analysis.

Sources may include:

* Previous AI incidents.
* Internal incidents.
* Near misses.
* Testing results.
* Vendor incidents.
* Industry incidents.
* Security intelligence.
* Operational statistics.

Example:

> If similar AI deployments have repeatedly experienced authorization failures, this may increase confidence that the scenario is credible.

Historical evidence should be relevant to the actual environment.

---

### 15. Testing Evidence

Testing can provide stronger evidence than assumptions.

Examples:

* Red-team testing.
* Penetration testing.
* Model testing.
* Adversarial testing.
* User acceptance testing.
* Security validation.
* Performance testing.

For example:

```text
Test:
100,000 AI requests

Unauthorized retrieval:
35 successful cases
```

This provides empirical evidence that the scenario is technically possible.

The result still needs contextual interpretation before assigning an organizational likelihood.

---

### 16. Expert Judgment

Historical data will not always exist for emerging AI technologies.

In such cases, expert judgment may be necessary.

Relevant experts may include:

* AI engineers.
* Cybersecurity professionals.
* Privacy specialists.
* Business owners.
* Legal specialists.
* Domain experts.
* GRC professionals.

Expert judgment should be documented rather than presented as unexplained certainty.

---

### 17. Assumptions in AI Risk Analysis

Every significant assumption should be identified.

Examples:

> "The model provider is assumed to maintain the documented security architecture."

> "The AI agent is assumed to have access to the production CRM."

> "The current model version is assumed to remain unchanged during the assessment period."

Assumptions matter because changing an assumption can change the risk analysis.

---

### 18. Uncertainty

AI risk analysis often contains uncertainty.

Uncertainty can arise from:

* Limited historical data.
* New models.
* Incomplete documentation.
* Unknown model behavior.
* Changing providers.
* Limited testing.
* Uncertain user behavior.
* Incomplete threat intelligence.

A mature assessment does not hide uncertainty.

It explicitly records it.

---

### 19. Confidence in the Analysis

An organization can record confidence separately from risk level.

For example:

| Analysis Confidence | Meaning                                     |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| High                | Strong evidence supports the assessment     |
| Medium              | Reasonable evidence with some assumptions   |
| Low                 | Significant uncertainty or limited evidence |

Example:

> Likelihood: Possible
> Impact: High
> Confidence: Medium

This communicates that the numerical or qualitative assessment is not equally certain in every case.

---

### 20. AI-Specific Likelihood Drivers

Several factors can influence AI risk likelihood.

### Exposure

How accessible is the AI system?

```text
Internal
   ↓
Partner
   ↓
Customer
   ↓
Public Internet
```

Greater exposure can increase some types of risk.

---

### 21. User Population

Analyze who can interact with the system.

Examples:

* 5 administrators.
* 500 employees.
* 100,000 customers.
* Public users.

The larger and less controlled the user population, the greater the opportunity for certain risk scenarios.

---

### 22. Interaction Volume

AI risk may be affected by transaction volume.

Example:

```text
10 requests/day
vs.
1,000,000 requests/day
```

Even a low-probability failure can produce meaningful exposure when the number of interactions is very large.

---

### 23. Model Complexity

More complex AI systems may introduce additional uncertainty.

Consider:

* Multiple models.
* Agentic behavior.
* Tool use.
* Retrieval systems.
* External APIs.
* Memory.
* Autonomous actions.

Complexity can increase the number of possible failure paths.

---

### 24. Autonomy as a Risk Driver

AI autonomy can influence both likelihood and consequence.

Compare:

```text
AI generates recommendation
        ↓
Human approves
```

with:

```text
AI generates recommendation
        ↓
AI executes action
```

The second architecture may create a materially different risk profile.

---

### 25. Permission Level

An AI system with read-only access has a different risk profile from an AI agent capable of modifying production systems.

Example:

```text
Read
 ↓
Write
 ↓
Delete
 ↓
Financial Transaction
 ↓
Autonomous External Action
```

As authority increases, potential consequences may increase substantially.

---

### 26. Data Sensitivity

The sensitivity of data processed by AI affects risk analysis.

Example:

```text
Public Information
      ↓
Internal Information
      ↓
Confidential Information
      ↓
Sensitive Personal Data
      ↓
Highly Critical Information
```

An identical technical failure can have very different consequences depending on the data involved.

---

### 27. Data Quality

Poor-quality data can increase AI risk.

Potential problems include:

* Incomplete data.
* Incorrect data.
* Outdated data.
* Duplicated data.
* Biased data.
* Misclassified data.
* Unrepresentative data.

Example:

```text
Poor Data
   ↓
Poor Model Output
   ↓
Poor Business Decision
```

---

### 28. Model Performance

Risk analysis should consider the model's actual performance for the intended use.

Relevant measures can include:

* Accuracy.
* Precision.
* Recall.
* False-positive rate.
* False-negative rate.
* Error rate.
* Calibration.

The appropriate metric depends on the AI use case.

---

### 29. Model Drift

Model performance may change over time because the operating environment changes.

```text
Training Environment
       ↓
Production Environment
       ↓
Environmental Change
       ↓
Performance Drift
       ↓
Risk Increase
```

Therefore, historical model performance may not accurately represent future performance.

---

### 30. Data Drift

Data distributions can change independently of the model.

Example:

```text
Historical Customers
       ↓
Model Training

New Customer Population
       ↓
Different Data Distribution
```

This can affect the reliability of AI outputs.

---

### 31. Environmental Change

AI risk analysis should consider external changes such as:

* Market conditions.
* Threat landscape.
* User behavior.
* Regulations.
* Technology.
* Business processes.

A model that performs well today may behave differently under materially changed conditions.

---

### 32. Human Dependence

Human involvement can influence both likelihood and consequence.

Consider:

```text
AI Output
    ↓
Expert Review
    ↓
Final Decision
```

versus:

```text
AI Output
    ↓
Uncritical Acceptance
```

The level of human verification can materially change the risk.

---

### 33. Automation Bias

Users may place excessive confidence in AI recommendations.

This creates a potential chain:

```text
AI Recommendation
      ↓
Human Trust
      ↓
No Independent Verification
      ↓
Incorrect Decision
```

Risk analysis should consider the actual behavior of users, not only the documented process.

---

### 34. Human Override Capability

A human override can reduce certain risks.

However, risk analysis should ask:

* Is the override actually available?
* Is it technically effective?
* Can users recognize when intervention is required?
* Do users have enough time to intervene?
* Are users trained?
* Is the override regularly tested?

A documented human-in-the-loop process does not automatically guarantee effective human oversight.

---

### 35. Reversibility

Analyze whether an incorrect AI action can be reversed.

**Highly reversible:**

> AI generates an incorrect draft.

**Partially reversible:**

> AI sends an incorrect customer communication.

**Difficult to reverse:**

> AI makes an irreversible financial or operational decision.

Reversibility can significantly influence consequence analysis.

---

### 36. Detection Capability

Risk analysis should consider how quickly an undesirable event would be detected.

Example:

```text
Immediate Detection
        ↓
Rapid Containment
        ↓
Limited Exposure
```

versus:

```text
Delayed Detection
        ↓
Continued AI Operation
        ↓
Expanded Exposure
```

Detection capability can therefore influence the overall risk profile.

---

### 37. Containment Capability

Consider how quickly the organization can stop or limit the AI system.

Questions include:

* Can access be revoked?
* Can the model be disabled?
* Can the AI agent be stopped?
* Can affected APIs be disconnected?
* Can user sessions be terminated?
* Can the system be isolated?

Strong containment capability may reduce potential exposure.

---

### 38. Duration of Exposure

Analyze how long the risk could remain active.

For example:

```text
5 minutes
vs.
5 hours
vs.
5 days
vs.
5 months
```

A risk with the same event probability can have significantly different consequences depending on exposure duration.

---

### 39. Scale of Exposure

Consider the number of:

* Users.
* Transactions.
* Records.
* Decisions.
* Systems.
* Countries.
* Customers.

A failure affecting one transaction differs significantly from a failure affecting millions.

---

### 40. Blast Radius

Blast radius describes how broadly an AI failure can propagate.

Example:

```text
Single User
     ↓
Single Department
     ↓
Business Unit
     ↓
Enterprise
     ↓
Multiple Customers / Regions
```

Understanding blast radius is essential for AI risk analysis.

---

### 41. Dependency Analysis

Analyze dependencies on:

* Cloud infrastructure.
* AI model providers.
* Data providers.
* APIs.
* Identity services.
* Network services.
* Monitoring systems.

A highly dependent AI system may have a larger exposure to external failures.

---

### 42. Third-Party Model Risk

If the organization uses an external model provider, analyze:

* Provider reliability.
* Model change frequency.
* Service availability.
* Security posture.
* Data-processing practices.
* Contractual commitments.
* Provider concentration.

Third-party dependencies can materially influence risk.

---

### 43. Risk Concentration

Risk analysis should consider whether multiple AI systems depend on the same component.

Example:

```text
AI Customer Service
        │
AI HR Assistant ──→ Same AI Provider
        │
AI Finance Assistant
```

A single provider failure could therefore produce multiple simultaneous impacts.

---

### 44. Risk Correlation

AI risks can be correlated.

For example:

```text
Data Quality Problem
        ↓
Model Performance Problem
        ↓
Decision Quality Problem
        ↓
Operational Impact
```

Treating these as completely independent risks may underestimate enterprise exposure.

---

### 45. Risk Interactions

One risk can increase the likelihood or consequence of another.

Example:

```text
Weak IAM
   +
AI Agent Autonomy
   ↓
Higher Potential for Unauthorized Action
```

Risk analysis should therefore consider interactions rather than analyzing every risk in complete isolation.

---

### 46. Scenario-Based Analysis

A useful AI risk-analysis method is to construct realistic scenarios.

Example:

> An attacker submits a crafted document containing malicious instructions. The AI retrieval system processes the document and the agent follows the embedded instructions, causing it to access information outside the user's intended request.

The analysis can then examine:

* Probability.
* Preconditions.
* Attack complexity.
* Required privileges.
* Detection.
* Exposure.
* Consequences.

---

### 47. Best-Case, Expected-Case, and Worst-Case

For uncertain AI risks, consider multiple scenarios.

```text
Best Case
   ↓
Limited consequence

Expected Case
   ↓
Material consequence

Worst Credible Case
   ↓
Severe consequence
```

The worst-case scenario should be **credible**, not merely theoretically imaginable.

---

### 48. Worst Credible Case

A useful question is:

> **What is the most severe consequence that is reasonably credible given the system's actual architecture, exposure, and dependencies?**

This avoids both:

* Underestimating risk.
* Exaggerating unrealistic scenarios.

---

### 49. Scenario Analysis Example

Consider an AI agent with access to customer records.

**Scenario A — Low exposure**

The agent produces one incorrect answer and a human corrects it.

**Scenario B — Moderate exposure**

The agent repeatedly provides incorrect customer information.

**Scenario C — High exposure**

The agent incorrectly modifies thousands of customer records before detection.

Each scenario can be analyzed separately.

---

### 50. Qualitative AI Risk Analysis

Not every organization needs precise numerical probabilities.

A qualitative approach may use:

```text
Likelihood:
Rare / Unlikely / Possible / Likely / Almost Certain

Consequence:
Minor / Moderate / Major / Severe / Critical
```

The important requirement is that each category has a clear organizational definition.

---

### 51. Quantitative AI Risk Analysis

Where reliable data exists, quantitative analysis can estimate:

* Probability.
* Expected loss.
* Frequency.
* Exposure.
* Financial impact.

Example:

```text
Expected Annual Loss
=
Probability of Event
×
Financial Consequence
```

However, quantitative precision should not be confused with analytical certainty.

---

### 52. Avoid False Precision

Suppose an organization states:

> "The probability of this AI failure is exactly 3.72%."

If the number is based only on subjective judgment, the precision is misleading.

It may be more appropriate to state:

> "The likelihood is estimated as possible, based on limited testing and expert judgment."

The methodology should match the quality of available evidence.

---

### 53. Evidence-Based Analysis

A strong risk analysis can identify the evidence behind the assessment:

```text
Likelihood
   ↓
Historical Incidents
   +
Testing Results
   +
Threat Intelligence
   +
Expert Judgment
```

Similarly:

```text
Consequence
   ↓
Business Impact Analysis
   +
Data Classification
   +
Regulatory Requirements
   +
Stakeholder Analysis
```

This creates a defensible analytical foundation.

---

### 54. AI Risk Analysis Record

A practical record might contain:

| Field         | Example                                |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Risk ID       | AI-R-001                               |
| Risk Event    | Unauthorized data retrieval            |
| Cause         | Weak authorization                     |
| Likelihood    | Possible                               |
| Consequence   | Major                                  |
| Exposure      | 10,000 users                           |
| Duration      | Potentially continuous                 |
| Detection     | Moderate                               |
| Reversibility | Low                                    |
| Confidence    | Medium                                 |
| Evidence      | Security testing                       |
| Assumptions   | Current architecture remains unchanged |

This creates a much richer picture than a single risk score.

---

### 55. Core Principle of AI Risk Analysis

The central principle is:

> **AI risk analysis should combine evidence, scenario reasoning, likelihood assessment, consequence analysis, uncertainty, system characteristics, and organizational context to develop a defensible understanding of AI risk.**

A mature analyst does not simply ask:

> "What score should I give this risk?"

Instead, the analyst asks:

> **"What evidence and reasoning justify our understanding of how this risk could occur and what could happen if it does?"**

## Part 2 — AI Risk Analysis Methods, Factors, and Analytical Techniques

### 56. Analytical Structure of AI Risk

A structured AI risk analysis can be represented as:

```text
Risk Scenario
      ↓
Risk Drivers
      ↓
Likelihood Analysis
      ↓
Consequence Analysis
      ↓
Uncertainty Analysis
      ↓
Control / Exposure Considerations
      ↓
Overall Risk Analysis
```

The purpose is to understand **why the risk has its particular characteristics**, rather than simply assigning a score.

---

### 57. Risk Drivers

Risk drivers are conditions that influence the likelihood or consequence of a risk.

Examples include:

* System exposure.
* Data sensitivity.
* Model accuracy.
* User population.
* Autonomy.
* Privilege level.
* Transaction volume.
* Threat capability.
* Control effectiveness.
* Third-party dependency.
* Detection capability.
* Recovery capability.

A risk analyst should identify the major drivers before determining the risk level.

---

### 58. Likelihood Drivers and Consequence Drivers

Not every factor affects risk in the same way.

For example:

**Likelihood driver:**

> Public internet exposure may increase the opportunity for malicious interaction.

**Consequence driver:**

> Access to highly confidential information may increase the severity of a successful event.

Therefore:

```text
Likelihood Drivers
        +
Consequence Drivers
        ↓
Risk Analysis
```

---

### 59. Threat Capability

For security-related AI risks, consider the capability of potential threat actors.

Factors may include:

* Technical skill.
* Resources.
* Access.
* Motivation.
* Knowledge of the AI system.
* Ability to manipulate inputs.
* Ability to exploit dependencies.

A publicly exposed AI service may face a much broader threat population than an internal system.

---

### 60. Threat Opportunity

Risk analysis should consider whether the threat actually has an opportunity to interact with the AI system.

For example:

```text
Threat Actor
     ↓
Internet Exposure
     ↓
AI Interface
     ↓
Attack Opportunity
```

If an AI system is isolated from external users, certain attack scenarios may become less plausible.

---

### 61. Vulnerability Exploitability

A vulnerability does not automatically mean that a risk event will occur.

Analyze:

* Can the weakness actually be reached?
* Does exploitation require privileged access?
* Is special knowledge required?
* Is exploitation technically difficult?
* Are compensating controls present?

This prevents the analysis from overstating theoretical vulnerabilities.

---

### 62. Attack Complexity

For AI security scenarios, attack complexity can affect likelihood.

Compare:

```text
Simple:
Enter malicious prompt
```

with:

```text
Complex:
Compromise external data source
+
Manipulate retrieval content
+
Bypass validation
+
Trigger autonomous tool
```

The second scenario may require substantially more effort.

---

### 63. Required Privileges

Analyze what level of access is necessary.

Example:

```text
Unauthenticated User
      ↓
Authenticated User
      ↓
Privileged User
      ↓
Administrator
```

A scenario requiring administrator privileges may have a different likelihood from one accessible to every public user.

---

### 64. Attack Preconditions

Some AI risk scenarios require multiple conditions.

Example:

```text
1. Attacker accesses AI interface
        ↓
2. Malicious content enters retrieval source
        ↓
3. AI processes malicious instruction
        ↓
4. Tool is available
        ↓
5. Authorization is insufficient
        ↓
6. Unauthorized action occurs
```

The more restrictive the necessary conditions, the more carefully likelihood should be analyzed.

---

### 65. Exposure Analysis

Exposure describes the degree to which the AI system is exposed to potential risk.

Consider:

* Number of users.
* Number of interfaces.
* Number of integrations.
* Internet accessibility.
* Geographic reach.
* Data volume.
* Transaction volume.

Exposure can influence both likelihood and consequence.

---

### 66. Data Exposure

For AI systems, examine:

```text
Data Type
   ↓
Sensitivity
   ↓
Volume
   ↓
Accessibility
   ↓
Potential Exposure
```

For example, accidental disclosure of one public document is fundamentally different from disclosure of a large confidential customer database.

---

### 67. Data Volume

Risk consequence can increase with the quantity of data affected.

Example:

```text
10 records
     vs.
10,000 records
     vs.
10 million records
```

Data volume should therefore be considered when analyzing confidentiality and privacy scenarios.

---

### 68. Decision Criticality

Analyze how important the AI-supported decision is.

A useful conceptual scale is:

```text
Administrative
     ↓
Operational
     ↓
Financial
     ↓
Customer-impacting
     ↓
Human-impacting
     ↓
Safety-critical
```

The consequences of an incorrect AI output generally become more significant as decision criticality increases.

---

### 69. Decision Reversibility

A decision that can easily be corrected may create less exposure than one that cannot.

Example:

```text
Incorrect Draft
→ Easily corrected

Incorrect Account Update
→ Requires remediation

Incorrect Irreversible Decision
→ Difficult or impossible to reverse
```

Reversibility is therefore an important consequence factor.

---

### 70. Scale and Magnitude

Risk analysis should distinguish between:

**Probability of an error**

and

**Magnitude of the resulting exposure.**

For example:

```text
Low error rate
+
Very high transaction volume
=
Potentially material aggregate impact
```

This is especially relevant to automated AI systems.

---

### 71. Duration

Duration measures how long the risk condition can persist.

Example:

```text
5 minutes
1 hour
1 day
1 month
```

A longer duration may provide more opportunity for:

* Additional affected users.
* Additional transactions.
* Additional data exposure.
* Further propagation.

---

### 72. Speed of Propagation

AI systems can propagate errors quickly.

Example:

```text
Incorrect Model Output
       ↓
Automation
       ↓
10,000 Actions
       ↓
Impact
```

Risk analysis should consider not only whether an error occurs but how rapidly it can spread.

---

### 73. Blast Radius Analysis

The blast radius identifies the maximum reasonable scope of impact.

Possible levels:

```text
Individual
Department
Business Unit
Country
Enterprise
Multiple Organizations
```

Blast radius is especially important for shared AI platforms.

---

### 74. Geographic Impact

An AI incident can affect different jurisdictions.

Consider:

* Countries.
* Regions.
* Data locations.
* Customer locations.
* Regulatory environments.

A globally deployed AI system may have a larger consequence profile than a system restricted to one internal location.

---

### 75. Stakeholder Exposure

Risk analysis should identify who may be affected.

Possible stakeholders:

* Employees.
* Customers.
* Suppliers.
* Partners.
* Applicants.
* Citizens.
* Regulators.
* Communities.

Different stakeholders may experience different consequences from the same event.

---

### 76. Vulnerability of Affected Populations

Some populations may be more vulnerable to AI-related consequences.

Examples can include:

* Individuals dependent on automated decisions.
* Users with limited alternatives.
* People unable to easily challenge a decision.
* Populations particularly affected by inaccurate or biased outcomes.

This factor can influence consequence analysis.

---

### 77. Human Harm Analysis

For AI systems affecting people, analyze potential human consequences separately from technical consequences.

Example:

```text
Model Error
    ↓
Incorrect Decision
    ↓
Individual Harm
```

Potential consequences may include:

* Financial harm.
* Loss of opportunity.
* Psychological harm.
* Physical harm.
* Social harm.

Human consequences should not be reduced to financial metrics alone.

---

### 78. Business Impact

Business impact analysis can examine:

* Revenue loss.
* Operational disruption.
* Productivity loss.
* Customer churn.
* Remediation cost.
* Contractual penalties.
* Business interruption.

This connects AI risk analysis to enterprise risk management.

---

### 79. Financial Impact

Where appropriate, financial consequence can be estimated.

A simplified model is:

```text
Expected Loss
=
Event Probability
×
Financial Impact
```

However, this should only be used when the underlying assumptions are sufficiently reliable.

---

### 80. Regulatory Impact

AI risks can produce regulatory consequences.

Analysis may consider:

* Regulatory investigation.
* Corrective orders.
* Fines.
* Reporting obligations.
* Restrictions on processing.
* Suspension of an AI system.

The actual consequence depends on the applicable legal and regulatory environment.

---

### 81. Legal Impact

Legal consequences may include:

* Litigation.
* Contract disputes.
* Liability claims.
* Intellectual-property disputes.
* Employment claims.
* Privacy claims.

Legal analysis should normally involve qualified legal professionals where appropriate.

---

### 82. Reputational Impact

Reputation can be affected by:

* AI failures.
* Customer harm.
* Data exposure.
* Bias.
* Lack of transparency.
* Misleading AI-generated information.

Reputational impact can be difficult to quantify and may therefore require qualitative analysis.

---

### 83. Operational Impact

Operational analysis should consider:

* Service downtime.
* Manual workload.
* Process disruption.
* Reduced productivity.
* Incident-response effort.
* Recovery time.

Example:

```text
AI Failure
    ↓
Manual Processing
    ↓
Higher Workload
    ↓
Service Delay
```

---

### 84. Security Impact

Security analysis can use the traditional:

```text
Confidentiality
Integrity
Availability
```

But AI systems may require additional considerations such as:

* Model integrity.
* Output integrity.
* Prompt integrity.
* Data provenance.
* Tool authorization.
* AI service availability.

---

### 85. Privacy Impact

AI privacy risk analysis can examine:

* Collection.
* Processing.
* Use.
* Disclosure.
* Retention.
* Access.
* Deletion.
* Secondary use.

The analysis should follow the actual data lifecycle.

---

### 86. Model Integrity

Model integrity asks:

> **Can the model or its supporting artifacts be manipulated in a way that changes behavior?**

Potential scenarios include:

* Unauthorized model modification.
* Malicious training data.
* Compromised model artifact.
* Incorrect model deployment.

A compromised model may produce persistent incorrect behavior.

---

### 87. Data Integrity

AI decisions depend heavily on data integrity.

Example:

```text
Corrupted Data
      ↓
Model Input
      ↓
Incorrect Output
      ↓
Incorrect Decision
```

Data integrity therefore becomes a key analytical factor.

---

### 88. Data Provenance

Risk analysis should consider whether the organization knows:

* Where data came from.
* Who supplied it.
* How it was transformed.
* Whether it is trustworthy.
* Whether it remains accurate.

Poor provenance can increase uncertainty around AI outputs.

---

### 89. Output Reliability

Analyze how reliable the AI output is for the intended purpose.

Questions include:

* What is the expected error rate?
* What types of errors occur?
* Are errors systematic?
* Are errors detectable?
* Can users validate the output?

The acceptable reliability threshold depends on the use case.

---

### 90. Hallucination Analysis

For generative AI, incorrect generated information can create a risk.

Analyze:

* Frequency.
* Severity.
* Detectability.
* User reliance.
* Automation level.
* Consequences.

A hallucination in a creative writing assistant differs significantly from one used in a critical business process.

---

### 91. Uncertainty Propagation

Uncertainty in one component can propagate through the system.

Example:

```text
Uncertain Data
      ↓
Uncertain Model Output
      ↓
Uncertain Human Decision
      ↓
Uncertain Business Outcome
```

Risk analysis should identify where uncertainty increases downstream.

---

### 92. Confidence and Calibration

An AI system may express high confidence even when it is incorrect.

This creates a potential risk:

```text
Incorrect Output
      +
High Apparent Confidence
      ↓
Greater Human Trust
      ↓
Greater Potential Consequence
```

The relationship between model confidence and actual correctness can therefore be relevant.

---

### 93. Detectability

Detectability asks:

> **How easily can the organization recognize that the risk event has occurred?**

A simple scale might be:

| Level  | Detectability                 |
| ------ | ----------------------------- |
| High   | Quickly detected              |
| Medium | Detectable with investigation |
| Low    | Difficult to detect           |

For some risk methodologies, lower detectability corresponds to higher exposure.

---

### 94. Controllability

Controllability asks:

> **How effectively can the organization influence or contain the risk?**

Factors include:

* Ability to disable the AI.
* Ability to restrict access.
* Ability to modify configuration.
* Ability to reverse decisions.
* Availability of manual fallback.

Low controllability can increase concern even when likelihood is moderate.

---

### 95. Recoverability

Recoverability asks:

> **How effectively can the organization restore normal operations after the event?**

Consider:

* Backup availability.
* Recovery time.
* Data restoration.
* Model restoration.
* Manual alternatives.
* Vendor support.

Poor recoverability can increase the overall consequence.

---

### 96. Resilience

AI resilience refers to the ability of the AI-enabled process to continue operating safely despite failures.

Analyze:

```text
Failure
  ↓
Detection
  ↓
Containment
  ↓
Fallback
  ↓
Recovery
```

A resilient system can reduce the duration and magnitude of consequences.

---

### 97. Control Effectiveness as an Analytical Factor

Existing controls should be considered during risk analysis.

Examples:

* Access control.
* Input filtering.
* Output filtering.
* Human review.
* Monitoring.
* Rate limiting.
* Segmentation.
* Logging.

The analyst should assess whether the controls are:

* Designed appropriately.
* Implemented.
* Operating effectively.
* Relevant to the scenario.

---

### 98. Preventive vs Detective Controls

Controls can influence risk differently.

**Preventive:**

> Stops the event from occurring.

**Detective:**

> Identifies the event after or while it occurs.

Example:

```text
Authorization
→ Preventive

Monitoring
→ Detective
```

Risk analysis should not treat all controls as equivalent.

---

### 99. Compensating Controls

A compensating control may reduce exposure when the preferred control is unavailable.

Example:

```text
Automated Authorization
      ↓
Not Available
      ↓
Manual Approval
```

The analysis should consider whether the compensating control provides adequate protection.

---

### 100. Control Dependency

A control may itself depend on another system.

Example:

```text
AI Authorization
      ↓
Identity Provider
      ↓
Cloud Infrastructure
```

If the dependency fails, the effectiveness of the primary control may also decrease.

---

### 101. Control Failure Analysis

Analyze the possibility that controls fail.

Example:

```text
Security Filter
      ↓
Failure
      ↓
Unsafe Output
      ↓
No Secondary Review
      ↓
Customer Exposure
```

This is especially important when the AI architecture relies heavily on automated controls.

---

### 102. Defense-in-Depth Analysis

Where multiple controls exist, analyze their combined protection.

Example:

```text
Authentication
      ↓
Authorization
      ↓
Input Validation
      ↓
Model Guardrails
      ↓
Output Filtering
      ↓
Human Review
      ↓
Monitoring
```

The analysis should determine what happens if one or more layers fail.

---

### 103. Single-Control Dependency

A particularly important condition is reliance on one critical control.

Example:

```text
AI Agent
   ↓
One Authorization Layer
   ↓
Sensitive Database
```

If that single control fails, the blast radius may be large.

This should be reflected in the risk analysis.

---

### 104. Residual Exposure During Analysis

Risk analysis may distinguish between:

**Inherent exposure**

The exposure without considering existing controls.

**Current/controlled exposure**

The exposure considering existing controls.

A simplified model:

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Existing Controls
      ↓
Current Risk Exposure
```

The exact terminology should follow the organization's methodology.

---

### 105. Risk Analysis Matrix

A common qualitative matrix combines likelihood and consequence.

Example:

| Likelihood \ Consequence |  Minor | Moderate |    Major |   Severe | Critical |
| ------------------------ | -----: | -------: | -------: | -------: | -------: |
| Rare                     |    Low |      Low |   Medium |   Medium |     High |
| Unlikely                 |    Low |      Low |   Medium |     High |     High |
| Possible                 |    Low |   Medium |     High |     High | Critical |
| Likely                   | Medium |     High |     High | Critical | Critical |
| Almost Certain           |   High |     High | Critical | Critical | Critical |

The actual matrix should be approved by the organization's risk framework.

---

### 106. Why the Matrix Is Not the Analysis

A risk matrix is an **output mechanism**, not the complete analytical process.

For example:

```text
Likelihood = Possible
Impact = Major
```

does not explain:

* Why likelihood is possible.
* Why impact is major.
* What evidence supports the conclusion.
* What assumptions were used.
* How uncertainty affects the result.

The analytical narrative remains important.

---

### 107. Analytical Narrative

A strong risk analysis might state:

> "The likelihood is assessed as Possible because the AI interface is externally accessible, similar attack patterns have been demonstrated in testing, and the system processes untrusted user input. Existing input filtering reduces but does not eliminate the opportunity for exploitation."

This is much stronger than:

> "Likelihood: 3."

---

### 108. Scenario Comparison

When multiple scenarios exist, compare them.

| Scenario                  | Likelihood | Consequence | Exposure |
| ------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | -------- |
| Minor output error        | Likely     | Minor       | Limited  |
| Repeated incorrect advice | Possible   | Major       | Broad    |
| Autonomous harmful action | Unlikely   | Critical    | Broad    |

This allows decision-makers to understand where the major exposure exists.

---

### 109. Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis asks:

> **What happens to the risk assessment if a key assumption changes?**

Example:

```text
Current:
10,000 users

Scenario:
100,000 users

Result:
Potential consequence increases significantly
```

This is useful for rapidly changing AI systems.

---

### 110. Threshold Analysis

Identify conditions that materially change risk.

Example:

```text
Read-only access
     ↓
Lower exposure

Write access
     ↓
Higher exposure

Autonomous financial transaction
     ↓
Materially higher consequence
```

These thresholds can become important governance triggers.

---

### 111. Scenario Stress Testing

Stress testing examines how the AI system behaves under unfavorable conditions.

Examples:

* Extreme input volume.
* Malicious input.
* Data corruption.
* Model degradation.
* Provider outage.
* Loss of human oversight.
* Control failure.

The purpose is to determine whether the original risk analysis remains valid under stress.

---

### 112. Dependency Stress Testing

Consider simultaneous failures.

Example:

```text
AI Provider Outage
+
Identity Provider Outage
+
Manual Fallback Unavailable
```

The combined consequence may be much greater than any single failure.

---

### 113. Risk Correlation Analysis

Where multiple risks share causes or dependencies, assess whether they are correlated.

Example:

```text
Same AI Provider
      ↓
System A
System B
System C
      ↓
Common Failure
      ↓
Multiple Business Impacts
```

This prevents underestimating portfolio-level exposure.

---

### 114. Aggregated Risk Analysis

Individual risks should sometimes be aggregated.

For example:

```text
AI-R-001 → Data Exposure
AI-R-002 → Model Failure
AI-R-003 → Provider Failure
AI-R-004 → Human Overreliance
       ↓
Enterprise AI Risk Theme
       ↓
Significant AI Exposure
```

Aggregation allows management to see systemic risk.

---

### 115. Risk Analysis Under Uncertainty

When evidence is limited, the analyst should explicitly document:

* What is known.
* What is estimated.
* What is unknown.
* What assumptions are being made.
* What evidence is missing.
* How uncertainty affects the result.

Example:

> "Likelihood is assessed as Possible with Medium confidence because no comparable internal incident history exists and the assessment relies primarily on testing and expert judgment."

---

### 116. Analytical Confidence

A risk assessment can include confidence separately from the risk rating.

For example:

```text
Likelihood: Possible
Consequence: Severe
Overall Risk: High
Confidence: Medium
```

This tells management:

> "The potential risk is high, but the evidence supporting the exact assessment is not yet complete."

---

### 117. Conservative Analysis

Where uncertainty is significant, an organization may adopt conservative assumptions.

For example:

> If the maximum credible consequence cannot be reliably excluded, the organization may analyze that scenario separately rather than assuming the consequence is negligible.

Conservative analysis should be governed by an approved methodology rather than arbitrary pessimism.

---

### 118. Avoiding Overestimation

Risk analysis should remain evidence-based.

Avoid statements such as:

> "AI is unpredictable, therefore the risk is critical."

Instead:

> "The system demonstrates inconsistent behavior under defined test conditions, and the output is used in a high-impact decision process; therefore, the consequence and uncertainty require further analysis."

---

### 119. Avoiding Underestimation

Similarly, avoid:

> "There have been no incidents, therefore the likelihood is low."

Absence of incidents may simply mean:

* No incidents occurred.
* Incidents were not detected.
* Incidents were not reported.
* The system is too new.
* Monitoring is insufficient.

Historical absence should therefore be interpreted carefully.

---

### 120. Evidence Hierarchy

Evidence can be considered at different levels.

```text
Strongest
↓
Direct Testing
Observed Incidents
Operational Metrics
Relevant Historical Data
Expert Assessment
Documented Assumptions
Theoretical Possibility
↓
Weakest
```

The exact hierarchy varies by organization, but the principle is important:

> Evidence quality should influence confidence in the analysis.

---

### 121. AI Risk Analysis Record — Advanced Example

```text
Risk ID:
AI-R-021

Risk:
Unauthorized AI agent action

Cause:
Excessive tool permissions

Threat:
Malicious prompt manipulation

Risk Event:
Agent executes unauthorized CRM modification

Likelihood:
Possible

Consequence:
Major

Exposure:
50,000 customer records

Detection:
Medium

Reversibility:
Partial

Control Effectiveness:
Moderate

Confidence:
Medium

Evidence:
Red-team testing and architecture review

Key Assumption:
Current agent permissions remain unchanged
```

This represents a defensible analytical record.

---

### 122. GRC Review Questions

A GRC reviewer should ask:

1. What evidence supports the likelihood?
2. What evidence supports the consequence?
3. Are the assumptions documented?
4. Is uncertainty recognized?
5. Are dependencies included?
6. Are human factors considered?
7. Is the blast radius understood?
8. Is the risk affected by scale?
9. Are existing controls appropriately considered?
10. Could multiple risks be correlated?

These questions help challenge weak assessments.

---

### 123. Part 2 Core Principle

> **AI risk analysis should examine the drivers, conditions, evidence, uncertainty, likelihood, consequences, exposure, dependencies, and control environment that determine the characteristics of an identified AI risk.**

A mature analyst does not simply calculate:

```text
Risk = Likelihood × Impact
```

The mature approach is:

```text
Risk Scenario
      ↓
Understand Causes
      ↓
Understand Exposure
      ↓
Assess Likelihood
      ↓
Assess Consequences
      ↓
Consider Controls
      ↓
Consider Uncertainty
      ↓
Analyze Dependencies
      ↓
Determine Risk Characteristics
```

The resulting analysis should be **evidence-based, transparent, proportionate, and reproducible**.

# 06.04 — AI Risk Analysis

## Part 3 — Practical AI Risk Analysis, Scenario Modeling, and GRC Application

### 124. From Risk Statement to Analytical Scenario

A risk statement is often too broad to analyze directly.

For example:

> "The organization's generative AI system may expose confidential information."

A stronger analytical scenario is:

> "An employee submits a prompt containing a request for customer information, and the AI assistant retrieves confidential records from an integrated enterprise data source that the employee is not authorized to access."

The second statement provides identifiable:

* Actor.
* Trigger.
* AI component.
* Data source.
* Failure condition.
* Risk event.
* Potential consequence.

This makes the risk suitable for structured analysis.

---

### 125. Scenario Decomposition

A practical technique is to decompose the scenario:

```text id="0d1z7v"
Threat / Trigger
      ↓
Precondition
      ↓
AI Processing
      ↓
Control Failure
      ↓
Risk Event
      ↓
Immediate Consequence
      ↓
Downstream Consequence
```

Example:

```text id="h7q6d3"
Malicious User Input
      ↓
Untrusted Content Accepted
      ↓
AI Processes Content
      ↓
Guardrail Fails
      ↓
Unauthorized Tool Invocation
      ↓
Sensitive Data Retrieved
      ↓
Customer / Regulatory Impact
```

This structure makes the analysis more defensible.

---

### 126. Cause–Event–Consequence Model

A simple GRC model is:

```text id="qg5g4t"
CAUSE
Why could it happen?
        ↓
EVENT
What actually happens?
        ↓
CONSEQUENCE
What happens afterward?
```

Example:

```text id="e1j3l0"
Cause:
Excessive AI permissions

        ↓

Event:
AI agent modifies records without authorization

        ↓

Consequence:
Data integrity and operational impact
```

This prevents the analyst from confusing vulnerabilities with actual risk events.

---

### 127. Multiple Causes

A risk may have several contributing causes.

Example:

```text id="zj5bqn"
Weak Authorization
       +
Excessive AI Permissions
       +
Insufficient Testing
       +
Poor Monitoring
       ↓
Unauthorized AI Action
```

The analysis should identify the most important causes rather than treating them as one generic weakness.

---

### 128. Root Cause vs Contributing Cause

These should be distinguished.

**Root cause:**

> The AI agent was designed with excessive privileges.

**Contributing cause:**

> Permissions were not reviewed after a model or workflow change.

Both matter, but they have different implications for analysis and management.

---

### 129. Proximate Cause

The proximate cause is the immediate condition leading to the event.

Example:

```text id="z4r6ak"
Root Cause:
Poor AI access architecture

Contributing Cause:
Infrequent permission review

Proximate Cause:
Agent receives authorization to access restricted record
```

This distinction helps explain how a risk materializes.

---

### 130. Risk Path Analysis

A risk path describes the sequence through which a scenario develops.

Example:

```text id="o8kj8r"
External Input
      ↓
Prompt Processing
      ↓
Retrieval
      ↓
Model Reasoning
      ↓
Tool Selection
      ↓
API Invocation
      ↓
Database Access
      ↓
Unauthorized Result
```

Each stage can introduce:

* Failure.
* Manipulation.
* Uncertainty.
* Control dependency.

---

### 131. Attack Path Analysis

For security-related scenarios, the analyst can model the attack path.

```text id="h0s0xq"
Attacker
  ↓
AI Interface
  ↓
Prompt Manipulation
  ↓
Model Behavior
  ↓
Tool Invocation
  ↓
Privilege Boundary
  ↓
Target System
```

The analyst should determine which conditions must be satisfied for the attack to succeed.

---

### 132. Failure Path Analysis

Not all AI risks involve malicious actors.

A failure path may look like:

```text id="r8kq3c"
Poor Training Data
      ↓
Model Error
      ↓
Incorrect Classification
      ↓
Automated Decision
      ↓
Customer Impact
```

This is important because AI risk management must consider both:

* Malicious scenarios.
* Non-malicious failures.

---

### 133. Human Error Path

Human behavior can also create a risk path.

Example:

```text id="2k4qf0"
Employee Trusts AI Output
      ↓
No Verification
      ↓
Incorrect Information Accepted
      ↓
Business Decision
      ↓
Operational Impact
```

The analyst should consider actual human behavior rather than assuming perfect compliance with procedures.

---

### 134. Third-Party Failure Path

AI systems often depend on external providers.

Example:

```text id="5p6g6w"
Third-Party Model Provider
        ↓
Service Degradation
        ↓
AI Application Failure
        ↓
Business Process Disruption
```

The analysis should consider whether the organization has:

* Alternative providers.
* Manual fallback.
* Service-level commitments.
* Recovery procedures.

---

### 135. Dependency Mapping

A practical GRC analysis can create a dependency map:

```text id="8b9qps"
AI Application
      │
 ┌────┼─────┐
 ↓    ↓     ↓
Model Data  IAM
Provider Sources
      │
      ↓
Business Process
```

This helps identify systemic risks that may not be obvious from the AI application itself.

---

### 136. Common Cause Analysis

Multiple AI systems may share a common dependency.

Example:

```text id="s6y1o2"
                    AI Provider
                   /     |     \
                  ↓      ↓      ↓
                AI-01  AI-02  AI-03
                  \      |      /
                   Enterprise
```

A provider failure could therefore create correlated risks across multiple systems.

---

### 137. Scenario Variations

A single risk may have several variants.

Example:

**Scenario 1**

AI produces an incorrect answer.

**Scenario 2**

AI repeatedly produces incorrect answers.

**Scenario 3**

AI automatically acts on the incorrect answer.

**Scenario 4**

AI automatically acts on the incorrect answer at enterprise scale.

The underlying weakness may be similar, but the consequences differ substantially.

---

### 138. Scenario Escalation

Risk can escalate through successive stages:

```text id="f57d84"
AI Error
   ↓
Human Accepts Error
   ↓
Automated Action
   ↓
Large-Scale Processing
   ↓
Delayed Detection
   ↓
Significant Impact
```

The analyst should determine which escalation conditions are realistic.

---

### 139. Worst Credible Scenario

The worst credible scenario is not necessarily the most catastrophic scenario imaginable.

For example:

> "The AI system destroys the entire global economy."

is generally not useful as a normal operational risk scenario.

A more credible scenario might be:

> "An autonomous AI agent with production write access incorrectly modifies a large number of customer records before monitoring detects the activity."

The second scenario can be investigated, tested, and managed.

---

### 140. Best-Case Scenario

Best-case analysis can help establish the lower boundary.

Example:

```text id="6k0qgh"
AI Error
   ↓
Immediate Human Detection
   ↓
No External Action
   ↓
No Material Impact
```

This demonstrates how effective intervention can limit consequences.

---

### 141. Expected Scenario

The expected scenario represents the outcome considered most plausible based on available evidence.

Example:

```text id="k4z4zv"
AI Error
   ↓
Detected by Monitoring
   ↓
Manual Correction
   ↓
Limited Operational Impact
```

The expected case should be based on evidence rather than optimism.

---

### 142. Scenario Range

For uncertain risks, use a range:

```text id="e3g9ms"
Best Case
     │
     ├──────── Expected Case
     │
     └──────────────── Worst Credible Case
```

This is particularly useful where AI behavior varies significantly.

---

### 143. Likelihood Decomposition

Instead of treating likelihood as one unexplained number, break it down.

For example:

```text id="q5k0nq"
Likelihood of Exposure
        =
Probability of Trigger
×
Probability of Successful Exploitation
×
Probability of Control Failure
```

This is a conceptual model, not necessarily a formal mathematical requirement.

It helps identify where uncertainty exists.

---

### 144. Conditional Probability

AI risk events may depend on conditions.

Example:

> Unauthorized AI action may be unlikely under normal operation but significantly more plausible if the attacker can influence retrieved content and the AI agent has unrestricted tool access.

Therefore:

```text id="o7cv2d"
Normal Conditions
      ↓
Lower Likelihood

Attack Preconditions Present
      ↓
Higher Likelihood
```

Risk analysis should account for these conditional states.

---

### 145. Frequency-Based Analysis

Where operational data exists, frequency can support analysis.

Example:

```text id="m2o6ud"
1,000,000 AI interactions
        ↓
2,000 detected errors
        ↓
Observed error frequency = 0.2%
```

This can provide useful evidence, but the analyst must determine whether the observed population is representative of future operations.

---

### 146. Incident-Based Analysis

Past incidents can provide evidence.

Consider:

* Number of incidents.
* Incident severity.
* Recurrence.
* Root causes.
* Detection time.
* Recovery time.

A repeated pattern may indicate that the risk is more plausible than previously assumed.

---

### 147. Near-Miss Analysis

Near misses are also valuable.

Example:

> An AI agent almost executed an unauthorized transaction, but a secondary authorization control blocked it.

Although no harm occurred, the event provides evidence that:

* The scenario is technically plausible.
* Existing controls were tested.
* The attack path exists.
* Control effectiveness may need review.

---

### 148. Testing as Evidence

Testing can provide direct evidence of risk characteristics.

Examples:

* Prompt-injection testing.
* Adversarial testing.
* Model robustness testing.
* Data leakage testing.
* Access-control testing.
* Agent authorization testing.
* Failover testing.

Test results should be incorporated into the risk analysis.

---

### 149. Test Result Interpretation

Suppose:

```text id="wz4j0m"
10,000 test cases
      ↓
100 failures
```

The analyst should not automatically conclude:

> "Risk = 1%."

Instead, ask:

* Were the tests representative?
* Were they production-like?
* What constitutes failure?
* Were controls enabled?
* Were failures equally severe?
* Can the results be generalized?

---

### 150. Model Performance Analysis

For predictive AI, performance metrics may be central to risk analysis.

Possible measures include:

* Accuracy.
* Precision.
* Recall.
* F1 score.
* False-positive rate.
* False-negative rate.
* Calibration.

The relevant metric depends on what type of error creates the greatest risk.

---

### 151. False Positives

A false positive occurs when the system incorrectly identifies something as positive.

Example:

```text id="v1x0te"
Legitimate Transaction
        ↓
AI Classifies as Fraud
        ↓
Transaction Blocked
```

The consequence could include:

* Customer inconvenience.
* Operational workload.
* Revenue loss.

---

### 152. False Negatives

A false negative occurs when the system fails to identify something that should have been detected.

Example:

```text id="hj7tqv"
Fraudulent Transaction
        ↓
AI Classifies as Legitimate
        ↓
Transaction Approved
```

Depending on the use case, false negatives may create substantially greater consequences than false positives.

---

### 153. Error Asymmetry

Not all AI errors have equal consequences.

For example:

```text id="5xqk8k"
False Positive
→ Customer inconvenience

False Negative
→ Financial fraud
```

The risk analysis should therefore examine **error type**, not simply overall error rate.

---

### 154. Distribution of Errors

An overall accuracy figure can conceal important problems.

Example:

> Model accuracy = 98%

This may appear strong.

But if the 2% error rate is concentrated in a particular customer population, region, transaction type, or critical process, the risk may be materially higher.

---

### 155. Segment Analysis

Risk analysis should consider whether performance varies across relevant groups or operating conditions.

Examples:

* Customer segment.
* Geographic region.
* Language.
* Product type.
* Transaction value.
* Input type.

This can identify hidden concentrations of risk.

---

### 156. Outlier Analysis

AI systems may perform poorly on unusual inputs.

Example:

```text id="z3dfgc"
Typical Input
     ↓
Good Performance

Rare / Unusual Input
     ↓
Poor Performance
```

Risk analysis should determine whether those unusual inputs are relevant to the organization's actual environment.

---

### 157. Edge Cases

Edge cases are unusual situations that may expose weaknesses.

Examples:

* Extremely large inputs.
* Missing information.
* Conflicting data.
* Unexpected user behavior.
* Novel scenarios.
* Unusual languages.
* System degradation.

Edge cases should be considered when they can create material consequences.

---

### 158. Distribution Shift

AI behavior may change when production data differs from training data.

```text id="v36tks"
Training Distribution
       ↓
Production Distribution
       ↓
Significant Difference
       ↓
Potential Performance Degradation
```

This is particularly important for long-lived AI deployments.

---

### 159. Temporal Analysis

Risk can change over time.

### 188. The Difference Between Calculation and Analysis

This distinction is fundamental.

**Calculation:**

```text
Likelihood = 4
Impact = 5
Risk = 20
```

**Analysis:**

```text
Likelihood is rated Likely because:
- the system is externally accessible;
- testing demonstrated the attack path;
- the affected interface processes untrusted input;
- the primary preventive control is only partially effective.

Impact is rated Severe because:
- the AI can access confidential information;
- exposure could involve thousands of records;
- disclosure cannot reliably be reversed.
```

The second is what makes the assessment useful for governance.

---

### 189. Analytical Rigor

A rigorous AI risk analysis should be:

**Traceable**

The conclusion can be traced to evidence.

**Transparent**

Assumptions are visible.

**Consistent**

Similar risks are analyzed using comparable criteria.

**Proportionate**

The depth of analysis matches the significance of the risk.

**Evidence-based**

Available empirical evidence is used where possible.

**Contextual**

The analysis reflects the actual AI deployment.

**Reproducible**

Another qualified analyst can understand the reasoning.

---

### 190. Core Principle

> **AI risk analysis is not simply the assignment of likelihood and impact scores. It is the disciplined examination of how an AI risk could materialize, the conditions that influence its occurrence, the magnitude and scope of its consequences, the effectiveness of existing controls, and the uncertainty surrounding the assessment.**

A mature GRC analyst moves from:

```text id="h8l2n7"
"What is the risk score?"
```

to:

```text id="2k9s5r"
"What is the scenario?"
        ↓
"Why could it happen?"
        ↓
"What evidence do we have?"
        ↓
"What conditions affect likelihood?"
        ↓
"What could happen?"
        ↓
"How large could the exposure become?"
        ↓
"What controls influence the outcome?"
        ↓
"What remains uncertain?"
        ↓
"How defensible is our conclusion?"
```

That is the foundation of **evidence-based AI risk analysis**.

## Part 4 — Enterprise Application, Documentation, Review, and Interview Mastery

### 191. Enterprise AI Risk Analysis

At enterprise level, AI risk analysis should not be performed only at the model level.

The analyst should understand the complete environment:

```text
AI Model
   ↓
AI Application
   ↓
Data
   ↓
Users
   ↓
Integrations
   ↓
Business Process
   ↓
Business Outcome
```

A model may be technically reliable while the **overall AI-enabled process** remains high risk because of poor access controls, excessive autonomy, weak human oversight, or inappropriate use.

---

### 192. System-Level Risk Analysis

The unit of analysis should be clearly defined.

Possible levels include:

* Model.
* AI component.
* Application.
* Business process.
* AI use case.
* Enterprise AI service.

For example:

> The organization should not assess only the language model if the model is connected to a customer database and can execute transactions.

The risk belongs to the **AI-enabled system and its operating context**, not necessarily to the model alone.

---

### 193. Use-Case Context

The same model can have very different risk profiles depending on its purpose.

Consider the same generative AI model used for:

**Use case A**

> Drafting internal meeting notes.

**Use case B**

> Supporting medical decisions.

**Use case C**

> Approving financial transactions.

The underlying technology may be similar, but:

```text
Purpose
+
Decision Criticality
+
Autonomy
+
Potential Consequence
```

can produce materially different risk characteristics.

---

### 194. Intended Use

The analyst should document the intended use of the AI system.

Questions include:

* What is the AI designed to do?
* Who is authorized to use it?
* What decisions does it support?
* What data does it process?
* What actions can it perform?
* What are users expected to do with its output?

Risk analysis should be performed against the **actual intended operating context**.

---

### 195. Reasonably Foreseeable Misuse

AI risk analysis should also consider foreseeable misuse.

Example:

```text
Intended:
AI summarizes documents

Foreseeable Misuse:
AI summary used as the sole basis
for a high-impact decision
```

The analyst should distinguish between:

* Impossible misuse.
* Unlikely misuse.
* Reasonably foreseeable misuse.

Foreseeable misuse can materially affect the risk profile.

---

### 196. Scope Definition

Before analysis begins, define:

* System boundary.
* Organizational boundary.
* Geographic boundary.
* Data boundary.
* Assessment period.
* Users included.
* Dependencies included.
* Exclusions.

Example:

> "The assessment covers the production AI assistant, its retrieval database, identity service, model provider, users in Europe, and all customer data processed during the assessment period."

Clear scope prevents gaps and duplication.

---

### 197. Risk Analysis Boundary

The analyst should establish where responsibility begins and ends.

For example:

```text

     ↓

     ↓
Third-Party Model
     ↓

```

The organization may not control every component, but it may still carry risk arising from those dependencies.

**Provider-controlled elements**

**Organization-controlled elements.**

Example:

| Area                      | Provider | Organization |

| Application configuration |          | ✓            |
| User access               |          | ✓            |
| Data supplied             |          | ✓            |
| Model availability        | ✓        |              |
| Business process          |          | ✓            |

This helps prevent responsibility gaps.

* Architecture documentation.
* Data-processing information.

The analyst should distinguish between **verified evidence** and unsupported vendor claims.

### 204. Risk Analysis Narrative


### 212. Avoiding Single-Dimension Analysis

A common weakness is:

> "There is no significant financial loss, therefore the AI risk is low."

This can be incorrect.

An AI system could produce:

* Significant privacy harm.
* Discrimination.
* Safety consequences.
* Regulatory consequences.

Therefore, financial impact alone should not determine the overall risk.

### 213. Multi-Criteria Analysis

A more mature approach evaluates multiple dimensions.

Conceptually:

```text

                    │
       ┌────────────┼────────────┐

   Security      Privacy       Human
       ↓            ↓            ↓
   Financial    Legal       Operational
       └────────────┼────────────┘
                    ↓
             Overall Analysis
```

The weighting and aggregation method should be defined by the organization.

### 214. Risk Scoring Transparency

If a numerical score is used, the calculation should be transparent.

Example:

```text

Consequence = 5

Score = 4 × 5
      = 20
```

But the record should also explain **why** those values were selected.

The calculation provides consistency; the narrative provides justification.

---

### 215. Avoiding Score Manipulation

Risk scores should not be reduced simply to make a project easier to approve.

For example:

> "Let's rate the consequence as moderate so the system can proceed."

This undermines the risk-management process.

Ratings should reflect evidence and approved criteria.

### 233. Common AI Risk Analysis Mistakes

### Mistake 1 — Scoring Before Understanding

> Assigning a score before defining the scenario.

### Mistake 2 — Treating Model Accuracy as Overall Risk

> A highly accurate model can still be deployed insecurely.

### Mistake 3 — Ignoring Human Factors

> Assuming users always verify AI outputs.

### Mistake 4 — Ignoring Scale

> Evaluating one AI interaction instead of millions of interactions.

### Mistake 5 — Ignoring Dependencies

> Assessing the AI application without its external providers and integrations.

---

### 234. Additional Common Mistakes

### Mistake 6 — Ignoring Uncertainty

> Presenting estimates as facts.

### Mistake 7 — Overusing Worst Cases

> Treating unrealistic catastrophes as normal risk scenarios.

### Mistake 8 — Relying Only on Historical Incidents

> Assuming no previous incident means low likelihood.

### Mistake 9 — Treating Controls as Automatically Effective

> Assuming a documented control works simply because it exists.

### Mistake 10 — Failing to Reassess

> Continuing to use an old assessment after major AI changes.

### 236. Practical Interview Explanation

If asked:

> **"How do you perform AI risk analysis?"**

A strong answer is:

> "I start by defining the AI risk scenario and separating the causes, risk event, and consequences. I then analyze the factors that influence likelihood, such as exposure, threat capability, system vulnerabilities, user population, autonomy, permissions, dependencies, and control effectiveness. I assess consequences across security, privacy, operational, financial, legal, human, and reputational dimensions. I also document evidence, assumptions, uncertainty, detectability, reversibility, and potential blast radius. Finally, I document the rationale behind the likelihood and consequence ratings so that the assessment is traceable, reviewable, and reproducible."

---

### 237. Short Interview Version

If the interviewer wants a concise answer:

> **"AI risk analysis is about understanding how a risk can materialize, how likely it is, what could happen, and how much uncertainty exists. I analyze the scenario, causes, exposure, likelihood, consequences, controls, dependencies, human factors, and evidence, then document the rationale behind the assessment rather than relying only on a numerical risk score."**

---

### 238. Senior-Level Interview Version

For a senior GRC position:

> **"I treat AI risk analysis as evidence-based scenario analysis rather than simple scoring. I establish the AI use-case context and risk scenario, analyze causal and dependency paths, evaluate likelihood and consequence across multiple dimensions, and consider scale, autonomy, human oversight, detectability, reversibility, control effectiveness, and uncertainty. I then document assumptions and supporting evidence, challenge the assessment through independent review, and ensure the analysis can be traced to the resulting risk decision."**

### 241. Final Principle

> **The objective of AI risk analysis is not merely to calculate a risk score. It is to develop a defensible understanding of the AI risk scenario, including its causes, likelihood, consequences, exposure, dependencies, controls, uncertainty, and supporting evidence.**

A strong AI GRC professional should therefore be able to move from:

**"There is an AI risk."**

to:

**"This is the scenario, these are the conditions that could cause it, this is the evidence supporting its likelihood, these are the potential consequences and affected stakeholders, these controls influence the exposure, these assumptions create uncertainty, and this is why we reached our analytical conclusion."**
