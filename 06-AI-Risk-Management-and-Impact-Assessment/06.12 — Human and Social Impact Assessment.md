# 06.12 — Human and Social Impact Assessment

## Part 1 — Foundations of Human and Social Impact Assessment

### 01. Introduction

Human and social impact assessment examines how an AI system may affect **people, groups, communities, relationships, behavior, participation, well-being, and society**.

While an AI system may be technically secure and operationally effective, it can still produce undesirable human or social consequences.

A useful starting model is:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Human Interaction
    ↓
Individual / Group
    ↓
Behavior, Opportunity, Well-being
    ↓
Social Consequences
```

The objective is to understand these effects systematically rather than discovering them only after deployment.

---

### 02. What Is Human Impact?

**Human impact** refers to the effect an AI system may have on individuals.

Potential effects include:

* Decision-making.
* Autonomy.
* Employment.
* Access to services.
* Financial circumstances.
* Safety.
* Well-being.
* Privacy.
* Dignity.
* Ability to participate.
* Access to information.
* Personal opportunities.

The exact impacts depend on the AI use case.

---

### 03. What Is Social Impact?

**Social impact** refers to effects that extend beyond individual users and influence groups, communities, institutions, relationships, or broader social conditions.

Examples include:

* Changes in employment patterns.
* Changes in access to services.
* Changes in public trust.
* Information distribution.
* Social participation.
* Community relationships.
* Institutional trust.
* Concentration of decision-making power.

Social impact may therefore emerge even when no single individual experiences severe harm.

---

### 04. Human Impact Versus Social Impact

The two concepts are closely related but operate at different levels.

```text
Individual
   ↓
Human Impact

Group / Community
   ↓
Social Impact
```

For example:

> An AI system incorrectly rejects one applicant.

This is primarily an individual impact.

If the system systematically reduces opportunities for a particular population, the concern becomes broader:

> **A social or group-level impact may exist.**

---

### 05. Why Human and Social Impact Matters

AI systems increasingly influence activities that affect people's daily lives.

Examples include:

* Employment.
* Education.
* Financial services.
* Healthcare.
* Customer services.
* Public services.
* Security.
* Content recommendation.
* Workplace management.

The more closely AI interacts with human decisions and social systems, the more important human and social impact assessment becomes.

---

### 06. Human-Centered Perspective

A human-centered assessment starts with the affected person rather than the technology.

Instead of asking:

> "What does the model do?"

ask:

> **"What happens to a person because the model does this?"**

For example:

```text
Model Classification
       ↓
Business Decision
       ↓
Individual Outcome
       ↓
Human Consequence
```

This shifts the assessment from technical functionality to real-world effects.

---

### 07. The Person Behind the Data

AI systems often process people as:

* Records.
* Profiles.
* Transactions.
* Attributes.
* Scores.
* Predictions.
* Categories.

The impact assessment must recognize that these data representations correspond to **real people**.

For example:

```text
Customer Record
      ↓
AI Score
      ↓
Business Classification
      ↓
Real Customer
      ↓
Real-World Outcome
```

The score itself is not the final impact.

The impact occurs when the score influences what happens to the person.

---

### 08. Human Agency

**Human agency** refers broadly to a person's ability to make meaningful choices and act according to their own interests and judgment.

AI can potentially influence agency by:

* Recommending choices.
* Filtering information.
* Ranking options.
* Automating decisions.
* Predicting behavior.
* Restricting available choices.

The assessment should consider whether AI is supporting human choice or increasingly determining it.

---

### 09. Human Autonomy

Autonomy concerns a person's ability to make decisions without inappropriate manipulation or control.

An AI system may affect autonomy when it:

* Strongly influences decisions.
* Personalizes persuasive content.
* Restricts available options.
* Makes decisions on behalf of people.
* Creates dependency on automated recommendations.

The assessment should consider the degree of influence rather than assuming that AI is neutral.

---

### 10. Human Dignity

Human dignity concerns the way people are treated as individuals with inherent value.

AI systems can potentially affect dignity through:

* Dehumanizing automated interactions.
* Excessive surveillance.
* Intrusive profiling.
* Unfair treatment.
* Public classification.
* Persistent labeling.
* Lack of meaningful recourse.

The assessment should consider whether the use of AI could undermine respectful treatment.

---

### 11. Human Well-Being

Human impact may include effects on:

* Physical well-being.
* Psychological well-being.
* Economic well-being.
* Professional well-being.
* Social well-being.

For example, an AI workplace-monitoring system could affect employee stress and workplace relationships even if the system performs exactly as designed.

---

### 12. Economic Impact on Individuals

AI can affect people's economic circumstances.

Examples include:

* Employment opportunities.
* Compensation.
* Access to credit.
* Insurance-related outcomes.
* Business opportunities.
* Customer pricing.
* Access to financial services.

The assessment should identify whether AI outputs could materially affect someone's economic position.

---

### 13. Employment Impact

AI may change:

* Job responsibilities.
* Required skills.
* Workforce demand.
* Performance evaluation.
* Recruitment.
* Promotion.
* Work allocation.
* Employee monitoring.

Potential positive impacts include:

> Automation of repetitive tasks and improved productivity.

Potential negative impacts include:

> Reduced employment opportunities or increased workplace surveillance.

Both should be considered.

---

### 14. Human Impact in the Workplace

Consider:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Employee Evaluation
    ↓
Performance Score
    ↓
Manager Decision
    ↓
Employee Consequence
```

Potential consequences may include:

* Performance-related action.
* Promotion or non-promotion.
* Work allocation.
* Compensation effects.
* Employee stress.
* Loss of trust.

The assessment should examine how AI outputs influence these outcomes.

---

### 15. Access to Opportunity

AI systems can influence access to opportunities.

Examples:

* Employment.
* Education.
* Financial services.
* Housing-related processes.
* Customer programs.
* Professional opportunities.

The assessment should ask:

> **Could AI reduce someone's opportunity because of an incorrect or inappropriate output?**

---

### 16. Access to Essential Services

Some AI systems may influence access to services that are particularly important to people's lives.

Examples include:

* Financial services.
* Healthcare-related services.
* Education.
* Public services.
* Essential customer services.

The consequences of an incorrect decision may be significantly greater in these contexts.

---

### 17. Human Dependence on AI

AI can gradually become part of normal human decision-making.

For example:

```text
Initial
Human → AI as Tool

Later
Human + AI → Joint Decision

Eventually
Human → Relies Heavily on AI
```

The assessment should consider whether increasing dependence changes human behavior or reduces independent judgment.

---

### 18. Automation Bias

Automation bias occurs when people place excessive trust in automated outputs.

Example:

> "The system recommended it, so it must be correct."

This can create human impacts even when a human technically remains responsible for the final decision.

The assessment should therefore consider the **behavioral effect of AI recommendations**.

---

### 19. Human Oversight

Human oversight can reduce potential impact when it is meaningful.

Relevant questions include:

* Can the human understand the output?
* Can the human challenge it?
* Can the human override it?
* Does the human have enough information?
* Is there enough time to review?
* Is intervention realistically possible?

Human involvement should not automatically be treated as an effective safeguard.

---

### 20. Human-in-the-Loop

A human-in-the-loop process generally places a person directly within the decision pathway.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Decision
```

The assessment should determine whether the human reviewer has genuine decision-making authority.

---

### 21. Human-on-the-Loop

A human-on-the-loop model places humans in a supervisory role.

Example:

```text
AI
 ↓
Automated Decisions
 ↓
Human Monitoring
 ↓
Intervention When Necessary
```

This can be appropriate for some systems, but the assessment should consider whether intervention can happen quickly enough.

---

### 22. Human-Out-of-the-Loop

A fully automated process may operate without routine human intervention.

Example:

```text
Input
 ↓
AI
 ↓
Decision
 ↓
Action
```

Where consequences are significant, this can materially increase the potential human impact.

The assessment should consider the consequences of incorrect automation.

---

### 23. Social Relationships

AI can influence how people interact with:

* Employers.
* Customers.
* Teachers.
* Service providers.
* Institutions.
* Communities.

For example, replacing human customer-service interactions with AI may improve efficiency but may also change how customers experience the organization.

---

### 24. Human Connection

Some AI applications may reduce human interaction.

Potential benefits:

* Faster service.
* 24/7 availability.
* Increased accessibility.

Potential concerns:

* Reduced personal interaction.
* Difficulty escalating complex cases.
* Customer frustration.
* Loss of trust.

The assessment should consider whether human interaction is important to the use case.

---

### 25. Social Trust

AI systems can influence trust in:

* Organizations.
* Institutions.
* Information.
* Decisions.
* Public services.
* Businesses.

For example, unexplained AI decisions may cause affected people to distrust the organization even when the system's overall accuracy is high.

---

### 26. Trust and Explainability

People may reasonably ask:

> "Why did the AI produce this result?"

The appropriate level of explanation depends on the use case.

An impact assessment should consider whether insufficient transparency could create:

* Confusion.
* Distrust.
* Inability to challenge outcomes.
* Reduced accountability.

---

### 27. Social Participation

AI can influence people's ability to participate in society.

Potential areas include:

* Access to information.
* Employment.
* Education.
* Public services.
* Communication.
* Digital platforms.

An AI system that unintentionally excludes certain populations may create broader social consequences.

---

### 28. Inclusion

An inclusive AI system should consider the diversity of people who may interact with or be affected by it.

Potential considerations include:

* Different user capabilities.
* Language.
* Accessibility.
* Digital literacy.
* Cultural context.
* Different usage environments.

The objective is to identify whether some populations may experience materially different effects.

---

### 29. Accessibility

AI systems should be assessed for their potential effect on people with different accessibility needs.

Examples:

* AI interfaces.
* Voice systems.
* Automated customer service.
* Document processing.
* Accessibility assistants.

Potential impact can occur when the AI system makes an important service harder to access.

---

### 30. Digital Divide

AI adoption can create differences between people who can effectively use AI-enabled services and those who cannot.

For example:

```text
AI-Enabled Service
       ↓
Digital Access Required
       ↓
Some Users Excluded
```

The assessment should consider whether AI deployment creates or increases barriers to participation.

---

### 31. Language and Communication

AI systems may perform differently across languages, dialects, or communication styles.

Potential impacts include:

* Misunderstanding.
* Reduced service quality.
* Incorrect classification.
* Exclusion.
* Poor accessibility.

Language-related effects can therefore become human and social impacts.

---

### 32. Cultural Context

AI outputs may be interpreted differently across cultural contexts.

An AI system deployed internationally should consider whether:

* Language assumptions are appropriate.
* Social norms differ.
* Communication styles differ.
* Certain outputs could be misunderstood.

The impact assessment should consider the actual deployment environment.

---

### 33. Community-Level Impact

Some AI systems may affect entire communities.

Examples:

* AI-driven public services.
* Workforce automation.
* Large-scale recommendation systems.
* Community surveillance.
* Resource allocation.

The assessment should consider whether effects extend beyond individual users.

---

### 34. Social Cohesion

AI may influence relationships between groups or within communities.

Potential effects could include:

* Increased polarization.
* Reduced trust.
* Social exclusion.
* Unequal access.
* Conflicts caused by AI-generated information.

The assessment should determine whether such effects are reasonably foreseeable for the particular use case.

---

### 35. Information Environment

AI can influence what information people see, receive, or prioritize.

Examples:

* Recommendation systems.
* Search systems.
* Generative AI.
* Personalized content.
* Automated news summaries.

Potential human impacts include:

* Misinformation.
* Confusion.
* Reduced exposure to alternative viewpoints.
* Overreliance on AI-generated content.

The relevance depends heavily on the system's purpose and context.

---

### 36. AI-Generated Information

AI-generated information may appear authoritative even when incorrect.

This creates a potential impact pathway:

```text
AI Generates Information
        ↓
User Trusts Output
        ↓
User Acts on Information
        ↓
Real-World Consequence
```

The assessment should therefore consider not just whether AI can make mistakes, but whether users are likely to rely on those mistakes.

---

### 37. Social Influence

Some AI systems are specifically designed to influence behavior.

Examples include:

* Recommendation engines.
* Personalized advertising.
* Engagement optimization.
* Automated persuasion.

The assessment should consider whether the AI's influence is:

* Expected.
* Transparent.
* Proportionate.
* Consistent with the intended purpose.

---

### 38. Behavioral Effects

AI may change how people behave.

Examples:

* Employees may change work habits because of AI monitoring.
* Customers may change purchasing behavior because of recommendations.
* Users may become dependent on AI assistance.
* People may alter decisions based on AI-generated predictions.

Behavioral changes can become part of the impact assessment.

---

### 39. Psychological Effects

Depending on context, AI may contribute to:

* Stress.
* Anxiety.
* Frustration.
* Loss of confidence.
* Perceived surveillance.
* Reduced autonomy.

The assessment should avoid unsupported speculation but should consider credible consequences relevant to the use case.

---

### 40. Surveillance

AI-enabled monitoring can increase the scale and sophistication of surveillance.

Examples:

* Workplace monitoring.
* Customer behavior analysis.
* Video analytics.
* Automated activity detection.

Potential human impacts include:

* Reduced privacy.
* Chilling effects.
* Reduced sense of autonomy.
* Workplace stress.

---

### 41. Chilling Effects

A **chilling effect** occurs when people change their behavior because they believe they are being monitored, evaluated, or observed.

For example:

```text
AI Monitoring
      ↓
Employee Knows Activity Is Analyzed
      ↓
Behavior Changes
      ↓
Reduced Willingness to Act / Speak Freely
```

Whether this is relevant depends on the context and deployment.

---

### 42. Social Equity

Impact assessment should consider whether AI benefits and burdens are distributed fairly.

For example:

```text
AI Benefits
     ↓
Who receives them?

AI Burdens
     ↓
Who bears them?
```

The organization should avoid assuming that overall system performance automatically means equitable outcomes.

---

### 43. Distribution of Benefits

Positive impact may also be uneven.

For example:

> AI may significantly improve service for digitally sophisticated customers while providing little benefit to people with limited digital access.

This creates a distributional question:

> **Who benefits from AI?**

---

### 44. Distribution of Harm

Similarly:

> Who experiences the negative consequences?

An AI system may have acceptable average performance while certain groups experience substantially greater adverse effects.

Therefore, impact assessment should consider distribution rather than only aggregate results.

---

### 45. Vulnerable Populations

Certain populations may be more susceptible to adverse consequences.

Examples may include:

* Children.
* Elderly people.
* People with limited digital literacy.
* People dependent on essential services.
* People with limited ability to challenge decisions.

The assessment should determine whether additional safeguards are appropriate.

---

### 46. Dependency and Power Imbalances

Human and social impacts can become more significant where one party has substantially greater power.

Examples:

```text
Employer → Employee
Institution → Individual
Service Provider → Customer
Platform → User
```

If an AI system is used by the more powerful party to evaluate or control the less powerful party, the potential impact may warrant additional scrutiny.

---

### 47. Power Asymmetry

A person may technically have a choice but practically have little ability to refuse.

For example:

> An employee may be uncomfortable with AI monitoring but may not realistically be able to avoid the monitored workplace.

The assessment should consider the **practical context**, not just formal choices.

---

### 48. Human Choice

A mature assessment asks:

> **"Does the affected person have meaningful alternatives?"**

Consider:

* Can they refuse?
* Can they opt out?
* Can they appeal?
* Can they request human review?
* Can they access an alternative process?

The availability of meaningful alternatives can influence the severity of impact.

---

### 49. Contestability

Where an AI output has meaningful consequences, affected individuals may need a way to challenge the outcome.

A basic pathway is:

```text
AI Decision
    ↓
Affected Person
    ↓
Challenge / Appeal
    ↓
Human Review
    ↓
Correction
```

The assessment should consider whether such mechanisms are appropriate and practical.

---

### 50. Impact on Children

AI systems involving children may require heightened consideration because children may:

* Have less ability to understand AI.
* Have less ability to challenge outcomes.
* Be more vulnerable to influence.
* Have different developmental needs.

The assessment should consider the particular characteristics of the use case.

---

### 51. Social Externalities

An AI system may create effects beyond the organization and its direct users.

For example:

> A system may improve operational efficiency while creating broader workforce disruption.

These effects can be considered **externalities**.

The assessment should identify material external effects where reasonably foreseeable.

---

### 52. Positive Social Impact

Human and social impact assessment should recognize potential benefits.

Examples include:

* Improved accessibility.
* Expanded access to information.
* Better service availability.
* Reduced administrative barriers.
* Improved safety.
* Increased productivity.
* New opportunities.
* Support for underserved populations.

The objective is not to assume AI is harmful.

The objective is to understand its overall effect.

---

### 53. Negative Social Impact

Potential negative effects may include:

* Exclusion.
* Loss of trust.
* Reduced employment opportunities.
* Increased surveillance.
* Social polarization.
* Reduced autonomy.
* Unequal access.
* Information distortion.

The relevance depends on the specific AI application.

---

### 54. Intended Versus Unintended Impact

Impact assessment should distinguish:

**Intended impact**

> The organization expects AI to improve customer-service efficiency.

**Unintended impact**

> Customers find AI-only service difficult to navigate and experience reduced access to human assistance.

Both should be evaluated.

---

### 55. Direct Versus Indirect Impact

**Direct:**

> AI incorrectly rejects a customer.

**Indirect:**

> The customer loses trust in the organization and changes providers.

Indirect effects may be harder to identify but can still be significant.

---

### 56. Short-Term Versus Long-Term Impact

Some impacts occur immediately.

Others develop over time.

Example:

```text
Short-Term
AI reduces administrative workload.

Long-Term
Employees become increasingly dependent on AI
and lose certain manual skills.
```

The assessment should consider the appropriate time horizon for the use case.

---

### 57. Reversibility

A critical question is:

> **Can the human or social impact be reversed?**

For example:

**Highly reversible**

> Incorrect AI-generated recommendation corrected before action.

**Partially reversible**

> Incorrect customer decision corrected after several days.

**Difficult to reverse**

> Lost employment opportunity.

**Potentially irreversible**

> Certain lasting personal or social consequences.

Reversibility can materially affect impact significance.

---

### 58. Scale of Impact

Consider:

> How many people could be affected?

```text
1 Person
   ↓
100 People
   ↓
10,000 People
   ↓
1 Million People
```

A moderate impact affecting millions of people may require greater attention than a severe impact limited to a very small population.

---

### 59. Duration of Impact

Consider whether the effect is:

* Temporary.
* Recurring.
* Long-term.
* Persistent.

For example:

> A temporary service delay is different from a persistent AI-generated profile that continues to influence future decisions.

---

### 60. Frequency

Impact assessment should also consider how often the impact could occur.

```text
Rare × High Impact
```

is different from:

```text
Frequent × Moderate Impact
```

Both may require attention for different reasons.

---

### 61. Human and Social Impact Chain

A useful mental model is:

```text
AI Capability
      ↓
AI Output
      ↓
Human Interpretation
      ↓
Decision / Behavior
      ↓
Individual Effect
      ↓
Group Effect
      ↓
Social Effect
```

This helps the assessor move beyond technical analysis.

---

### 62. Example — AI Recruitment

Consider an AI recruitment-screening system.

```text
Applicant Data
      ↓
AI Screening
      ↓
Candidate Ranking
      ↓
Recruiter Review
      ↓
Employment Decision
      ↓
Applicant
```

Potential human impact:

> A qualified applicant loses an employment opportunity.

Potential social impact:

> If similar outcomes systematically affect a population, broader employment inequality or exclusion may emerge.

---

### 63. Example — AI Customer Service

```text
Customer
   ↓
AI Assistant
   ↓
Recommendation
   ↓
Customer Action
```

Potential human impact:

> Customer receives incorrect information.

Potential organizational impact:

> Complaint or financial remediation.

Potential social impact may be limited unless the system operates at large scale or affects broader populations.

---

### 64. Example — Workplace Monitoring

```text
AI Monitoring
      ↓
Employee Activity Analysis
      ↓
Performance Assessment
      ↓
Manager Action
```

Potential human impacts:

* Stress.
* Perceived surveillance.
* Reduced autonomy.
* Employment consequences.

The assessment should consider both intended productivity benefits and potential human consequences.

---

### 65. Example — AI Education Assistant

An AI system assists students with learning.

Potential positive impacts:

* Personalized support.
* Increased availability.
* Faster feedback.

Potential negative impacts:

* Incorrect information.
* Student dependency.
* Reduced independent learning.
* Unequal access.

The assessment should examine the complete educational context.

---

### 66. Example — AI Public-Service Assistant

An AI assistant helps citizens navigate a public service.

Potential positive impact:

> Faster access to information.

Potential negative impact:

> Incorrect guidance causes a person to miss an important deadline.

The impact assessment should consider:

* Accuracy.
* Accessibility.
* Human escalation.
* Ability to correct errors.
* Consequence of missed actions.

---

### 67. Human and Social Impact Questions

A practical assessment should ask:

**People**

* Who may be affected?
* What decisions affect them?
* What could they lose or gain?

**Autonomy**

* Does AI influence meaningful choices?
* Can people exercise independent judgment?

**Access**

* Could AI create barriers?
* Can affected people obtain alternatives?

**Trust**

* Could AI reduce confidence in the organization or institution?

**Society**

* Could impacts extend to groups or communities?

---

### 68. Core Assessment Dimensions

A useful framework is:

```text
Human Impact
├── Autonomy
├── Dignity
├── Well-being
├── Opportunity
├── Economic Effect
├── Access
└── Safety

Social Impact
├── Inclusion
├── Equity
├── Trust
├── Participation
├── Community Effects
├── Social Relationships
└── Broader Externalities
```

Not every category applies to every AI system.

---

### 69. Impact Assessment Should Be Contextual

There is no universal human-impact score that applies equally to every AI system.

The assessor should consider:

* Purpose.
* Population.
* Environment.
* Decision significance.
* AI influence.
* Scale.
* Existing safeguards.

Context determines significance.

---

### 70. Proportionality

The assessment should be proportional to the potential consequences.

For example:

```text
Low Human Consequence
       ↓
Basic Assessment

Moderate Consequence
       ↓
Standard Assessment

Significant Human Consequence
       ↓
Enhanced Assessment
```

The organization should define its own thresholds.

---

### 71. Evidence-Based Assessment

Human and social impacts should be supported by appropriate evidence.

Potential evidence includes:

* User research.
* Stakeholder interviews.
* Complaints.
* Surveys.
* Testing results.
* Operational data.
* Outcome analysis.
* Accessibility testing.
* Incident records.
* Subject-matter expertise.

Evidence helps distinguish credible concerns from unsupported assumptions.

---

### 72. User Research

Where appropriate, organizations can engage actual users or affected stakeholders.

Questions may include:

* How do users interact with the system?
* What problems do they experience?
* Do they understand AI involvement?
* Do they trust the output?
* Can they obtain human assistance?
* Can they correct errors?

This can reveal impacts invisible to system developers.

---

### 73. Importance of Real-World Context

An AI system may perform well in testing but produce different effects in practice.

For example:

```text
Testing
   ↓
High Accuracy

Real World
   ↓
Users misunderstand outputs
   ↓
Unexpected Human Impact
```

Therefore, human and social assessment should consider actual use conditions.

---

### 75. GRC Role

The GRC professional helps ensure that human and social effects are not overlooked during AI deployment.

The role may include:

* Establishing methodology.
* Facilitating stakeholder assessment.
* Challenging assumptions.
* Reviewing evidence.
* Connecting impacts to controls.
* Escalating significant concerns.
* Supporting management decisions.
* Maintaining governance records.

The GRC professional does not need to personally determine every social consequence.

The responsibility is to ensure that the organization has a **credible process for identifying and addressing them**.

---

### 76. Common Mistake — Focusing Only on Technical Accuracy

A system may have high accuracy and still create undesirable human outcomes.

For example:

> The AI consistently predicts employee productivity but the use of the prediction creates excessive surveillance pressure.

Technical performance does not automatically equal acceptable human impact.

---

### 77. Common Mistake — Assuming Efficiency Equals Benefit

An organization may say:

> "The AI saves 40% of processing time."

That demonstrates efficiency.

It does not automatically prove:

> "The AI creates a positive human outcome."

The assessment should separately evaluate the effect on people.

---

### 78. Common Mistake — Ignoring Indirect Effects

An assessment may focus only on:

> "What does the AI output?"

instead of:

> "What happens after the output is used?"

The second question is more useful for human-impact analysis.

---

### 79. Common Mistake — Ignoring Distribution

An organization may report:

> "Overall accuracy is 95%."

But the assessment should also ask:

> "Who experiences the remaining 5%?"

Aggregate statistics can hide concentrated impacts.

---

### 80. Common Mistake — Treating Human Oversight as Automatic Protection

A human reviewer does not eliminate human impact.

The reviewer may:

* Trust AI excessively.
* Lack sufficient information.
* Lack authority to override.
* Be under time pressure.
* Fail to recognize AI errors.

Human oversight should therefore be evaluated for effectiveness.

---

### 81. Common Mistake — Ignoring Power Relationships

An AI system used by an employer to evaluate employees is different from an AI system used by employees for personal productivity.

The relationship between the parties can change the significance of the impact.

---

### 82. Common Mistake — Ignoring Accessibility

An AI system may work technically but still be difficult for some users to access.

Accessibility should therefore be considered as part of human impact where relevant.

---

### 83. Human and Social Impact Anchor

For practical GRC work, remember:

```text
WHO?
   ↓
WHAT HAPPENS TO THEM?
   ↓
HOW DOES AI CAUSE OR INFLUENCE IT?
   ↓
HOW SERIOUS?
   ↓
HOW MANY?
   ↓
HOW LONG?
   ↓
CAN IT BE REVERSED?
   ↓
CAN PEOPLE CHALLENGE IT?
   ↓
WHO BEARS THE BENEFIT?
   ↓
WHO BEARS THE BURDEN?
```

---

### 84. Part 1 Summary

Human and social impact assessment examines the consequences AI can have on individuals, groups, communities, and society.

The key principles are:

1. Start with the affected person.
2. Understand the AI use case.
3. Identify direct stakeholders.
4. Identify indirect stakeholders.
5. Consider human agency.
6. Consider autonomy.

### Core Anchor

> **AI Use Case → Affected People → Human Effects → Group Effects → Social Effects → Severity → Scale → Duration → Reversibility → Agency → Access → Trust → Safeguards**

The central GRC principle is:

> **"Human and social impact assessment asks not only whether an AI system works as designed, but what its use means for the people and communities affected by it. A mature assessment identifies who may be affected, understands how AI influences their choices and outcomes, considers both benefits and harms, examines how impacts are distributed, evaluates severity and reversibility, and ensures that significant human and social consequences are visible to accountable decision-makers."**

# 06.12 — Human and Social Impact Assessment

## Part 2 — Human and Social Impact Assessment Methodology

### 85. Purpose of the Assessment Methodology

A human and social impact assessment should provide a **repeatable method** for determining how an AI system may affect people and society.

The methodology should allow the organization to move from:

```text
AI Use Case
    ↓
Affected Stakeholders
    ↓
Potential Impacts
    ↓
Impact Evidence
    ↓
Impact Significance
    ↓
Safeguards
    ↓
Governance Decision
```

The purpose is not simply to produce a document.

The purpose is to create a defensible process for understanding consequences before and during AI use.

---

## 86. Step 1 — Define the AI System

The assessment begins by establishing exactly what is being assessed.

Document:

* AI system name.
* Business owner.
* Technical owner.
* Purpose.
* Main functions.
* Users.
* Deployment environment.
* AI capabilities.
* Inputs.
* Outputs.
* Connected systems.
* Intended operating scope.

A vague system description makes the rest of the assessment unreliable.

For example:

> "AI platform used by HR"

is insufficient.

A stronger description would identify:

> An AI-enabled recruitment screening capability that analyzes applicant information and produces candidate-ranking recommendations for recruiter review.

---

## 87. Step 2 — Define the Intended Purpose

The organization should clearly state:

> **Why is the AI being used?**

Examples:

* Customer-service assistance.
* Document classification.
* Employee productivity.
* Fraud detection.
* Recruitment support.
* Forecasting.
* Recommendation.
* Content generation.

Purpose matters because the same technology can have radically different human consequences depending on how it is used.

---

## 88. Step 3 — Identify the Actual Use

The assessment should examine how the system is **actually used**, not merely how it was originally designed.

Consider:

* Who uses it?
* How often?
* For what decisions?
* What happens to its outputs?
* Are outputs automatically acted upon?
* Are users applying it beyond the approved purpose?

This helps identify differences between:

```text
Intended Use
      ≠
Actual Use
```

---

## 89. Step 4 — Define the Assessment Boundary

The assessment should establish what is inside and outside the assessment.

Possible boundaries include:

* AI model.
* Application.
* Business process.
* Human decision-makers.
* Downstream systems.
* External users.
* Third-party providers.

A narrow technical boundary can miss important real-world effects.

---

## 90. Step 5 — Map the Decision Pathway

One of the most important activities is mapping how AI output reaches a real-world outcome.

Example:

```text
Data
 ↓
AI Model
 ↓
Prediction
 ↓
Human Review
 ↓
Business Decision
 ↓
Individual
 ↓
Outcome
```

The assessment should examine every meaningful step.

---

## 91. Why the Decision Pathway Matters

The model may only generate a recommendation.

However, the recommendation may influence:

* Hiring.
* Approval.
* Rejection.
* Prioritization.
* Pricing.
* Access.
* Monitoring.

Therefore:

> **Impact is determined partly by what happens after the AI output is generated.**

---

## 92. Step 6 — Identify Affected Stakeholders

Stakeholder identification should go beyond direct users.

Consider:

### Direct users

People interacting with the AI system.

### Decision-makers

People using AI outputs to make decisions.

### Subjects of decisions

People about whom AI makes predictions or recommendations.

### Indirectly affected people

People affected by downstream consequences.

### Groups and communities

Populations that may experience collective effects.

---

## 93. Stakeholder Mapping

A simple stakeholder map can be:

```text
                  AI SYSTEM
                      │
        ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
        ↓             ↓             ↓
      Users       Decision-Makers   Subjects
                                      │
                                      ↓
                              Indirectly Affected
                                      │
                                      ↓
                                  Community
```

This helps prevent stakeholder blind spots.

---

## 94. Step 7 — Identify Stakeholder Interests

For each stakeholder, determine:

* What matters to them?
* What could they gain?
* What could they lose?
* What decisions affect them?
* What control do they have?
* What ability do they have to challenge outcomes?

This converts stakeholder identification into meaningful impact analysis.

---

## 95. Stakeholder Power and Influence

Not every stakeholder has equal ability to influence the AI system.

Consider:

```text
Organization
     ↓
High decision authority

Individual
     ↓
Limited ability to challenge
```

Power imbalance can increase the significance of certain impacts.

---

## 96. Step 8 — Identify Potential Positive Impacts

The assessment should deliberately identify benefits.

Examples:

* Improved access.
* Faster service.
* Better personalization.
* Reduced administrative burden.
* Improved safety.
* Increased availability.
* Improved accessibility.
* Greater consistency.

Positive impact should be supported by evidence where possible.

---

## 97. Step 9 — Identify Potential Negative Impacts

Identify plausible adverse effects.

Examples:

* Loss of opportunity.
* Reduced autonomy.
* Incorrect decisions.
* Exclusion.
* Increased surveillance.
* Loss of trust.
* Economic harm.
* Reduced access.
* Psychological stress.

The assessment should focus on **credible scenarios**, not unlimited hypothetical possibilities.

---

## 98. Step 10 — Develop Impact Scenarios

Rather than writing:

> "AI may negatively affect people."

develop a specific scenario.

Example:

> The AI incorrectly ranks a qualified applicant below other candidates, resulting in the applicant not progressing to interview.

This is more useful because it identifies a concrete impact pathway.

---

## 99. Impact Scenario Structure

A useful structure is:

```text
Trigger
   ↓
AI Behavior
   ↓
Human / System Response
   ↓
Affected Stakeholder
   ↓
Immediate Consequence
   ↓
Potential Longer-Term Consequence
```

This creates traceability between AI behavior and human impact.

---

## 100. Step 11 — Identify Causal Links

The assessor should ask:

> **How exactly does the AI contribute to the impact?**

Possible relationships include:

### Direct

```text
AI Output → Human Outcome
```

### Indirect

```text
AI Output
   ↓
Human Decision
   ↓
Business Action
   ↓
Human Outcome
```

### Systemic

```text
AI
 ↓
Repeated Decisions
 ↓
Population-Level Pattern
 ↓
Broader Social Effect
```

---

## 101. Step 12 — Determine Impact Type

Classify the impact where useful.

Possible categories:

* Autonomy.
* Dignity.
* Safety.
* Well-being.
* Economic.
* Employment.
* Access.
* Inclusion.
* Social trust.
* Participation.
* Community.
* Information environment.

Multiple categories can apply to one scenario.

---

## 102. Step 13 — Assess Severity

Severity describes **how serious the consequence could be**.

A simple scale might be:

| Level           | Description                                               |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 — Low         | Minor inconvenience or limited effect                     |
| 2 — Moderate    | Meaningful but manageable consequence                     |
| 3 — Significant | Material effect requiring intervention                    |
| 4 — Severe      | Major effect on an individual or group                    |
| 5 — Critical    | Extremely serious or potentially irreversible consequence |

Organizations should define their own criteria.

---

## 103. Severity Should Be Contextual

Severity should not be determined by the AI technology alone.

For example:

> An incorrect restaurant recommendation may be inconvenient.

An incorrect AI recommendation affecting employment may be substantially more consequential.

The difference is the **human context**.

---

## 104. Step 14 — Assess Scale

Determine how many people could potentially experience the impact.

Consider:

* Number of users.
* Number of decisions.
* Number of affected individuals.
* Geographic scope.
* Frequency of use.
* Population coverage.

Example:

```text
One incorrect outcome
        ↓
Individual impact

10,000 similar outcomes
        ↓
Potential population-level concern
```

---

## 105. Step 15 — Assess Duration

Determine how long the impact may persist.

Possible categories:

* Temporary.
* Short-term.
* Recurring.
* Long-term.
* Persistent.

For example:

> A temporary service interruption is different from a decision that permanently affects a person's opportunity.

---

## 106. Step 16 — Assess Reversibility

Ask:

> **Can the consequence be corrected?**

Consider:

* Can the decision be reversed?
* Can records be corrected?
* Can the individual be compensated?
* Can the person regain the lost opportunity?
* Can the social effect realistically be undone?

Reversibility is especially important for consequential decisions.

---

## 107. Step 17 — Assess Detectability

Determine how quickly the organization would discover that an impact occurred.

Example:

```text
Immediate Detection
      ↓
Rapid Intervention
      ↓
Potentially Reduced Harm
```

versus:

```text
Impact occurs
      ↓
Months pass
      ↓
Problem discovered
      ↓
Large accumulated effect
```

Poor detectability can increase practical impact.

---

## 108. Step 18 — Assess Frequency

Consider how often the scenario could occur.

For example:

* Rare.
* Occasional.
* Regular.
* Frequent.
* Continuous.

A moderate impact occurring thousands of times may deserve significant attention.

---

## 109. Step 19 — Assess Reach

Reach concerns how broadly the consequences can extend.

Consider:

* Individual.
* Team.
* Department.
* Organization.
* Customer population.
* Community.
* Society.

This is particularly important for highly scalable AI systems.

---

## 110. Step 20 — Assess Autonomy

Determine how independently AI can influence outcomes.

A basic model:

```text
Level 1
AI provides information

Level 2
AI recommends

Level 3
AI influences decision

Level 4
AI initiates action

Level 5
AI autonomously executes action
```

Greater autonomy may increase the need for stronger safeguards depending on context.

---

## 111. Step 21 — Assess Human Oversight

Evaluate whether human involvement is meaningful.

Ask:

* Who reviews the output?
* What do they review?
* What information do they have?
* Can they reject the recommendation?
* Can they override the system?
* How quickly can they intervene?
* Are overrides monitored?

This determines whether human oversight actually reduces impact.

---

## 112. Step 22 — Assess Human Ability to Challenge

Consider whether affected people can:

* Ask questions.
* Request review.
* Correct information.
* Appeal decisions.
* Reach a human.
* Obtain remediation.

A formal appeal mechanism may not be sufficient if it is practically inaccessible.

---

## 113. Step 23 — Assess Distribution of Impact

Ask:

> **Who experiences the benefit and who experiences the burden?**

A useful table:

| Stakeholder  | Potential Benefit | Potential Burden          |
| ------------ | ----------------- | ------------------------- |
| Organization | Efficiency        | Governance exposure       |
| Employees    | Productivity      | Monitoring pressure       |
| Customers    | Faster service    | Incorrect recommendations |
| Community    | Improved access   | Potential exclusion       |

This provides a balanced assessment.

---

## 114. Step 24 — Assess Vulnerability

Determine whether some affected populations may be more susceptible to harm.

Consider:

* Dependency on the service.
* Ability to understand AI.
* Ability to challenge decisions.
* Ability to obtain alternatives.
* Ability to recover from errors.

The assessment should be evidence-based and proportionate.

---

## 115. Step 25 — Consider Power Imbalances

Identify relationships such as:

```text
Employer → Employee
Institution → Individual
Provider → Customer
Platform → User
```

Where one party has significantly greater authority, an AI-driven decision may have greater practical consequences for the other party.

---

## 116. Step 26 — Assess Choice and Alternatives

Ask:

> **What happens if the affected person does not accept the AI-enabled process?**

Possible situations:

### Meaningful alternative

A person can easily use another process.

### Limited alternative

A manual process exists but is difficult to access.

### No meaningful alternative

The AI-enabled process is effectively mandatory.

This distinction can materially affect impact.

---

## 117. Step 27 — Assess Transparency

Determine what information should be available to stakeholders.

Potential information includes:

* AI involvement.
* Purpose.
* Role of AI.
* Nature of the output.
* Human oversight.
* Available challenge mechanism.

The appropriate level depends on the context.

---

## 118. Step 28 — Assess Explainability Needs

Not every AI system requires the same level of explanation.

The assessment should ask:

> **What explanation is necessary for the affected person to understand, challenge, or respond to the outcome?**

Higher-consequence decisions may require stronger explanation and review mechanisms.

---

## 119. Step 29 — Assess Social Effects

Move beyond individual consequences.

Ask:

* Could this affect a group?
* Could repeated outcomes create a pattern?
* Could it affect community trust?
* Could it change participation?
* Could it influence social behavior?
* Could it create unequal access?

This establishes the social component of the assessment.

---

## 120. Step 30 — Assess Cumulative Effects

Consider whether repeated exposure could produce greater effects.

For example:

```text
Single AI Interaction
       ↓
Minimal impact

Repeated AI Interactions
       ↓
Accumulated effect

Long-term exposure
       ↓
Potentially significant consequence
```

This can be relevant to recommendation, monitoring, profiling, and personalization systems.

---

## 121. Step 31 — Identify Existing Safeguards

Document safeguards already implemented.

Examples:

* Human review.
* Access controls.
* User training.
* Monitoring.
* Escalation.
* Appeal mechanisms.
* Output restrictions.
* Manual fallback.
* Rate limits.
* Testing.
* Quality assurance.

Do not treat the existence of a safeguard as proof of effectiveness.

---

## 122. Step 32 — Evaluate Safeguard Effectiveness

For each safeguard, ask:

1. Does it operate?
2. Is it consistently applied?
3. Does it address the relevant impact?
4. Can it fail?
5. How quickly does it work?
6. Can it contain the consequence?

Example:

> Human review exists.

Further questions:

> Is every decision reviewed?

> Can reviewers override the AI?

> Do they have enough time?

> Are overrides possible?

> Is review effectiveness measured?

---

## 123. Step 33 — Identify Control Gaps

A control gap exists when existing measures do not adequately address the identified impact.

Example:

```text
Impact:
Incorrect AI recommendation

Existing Control:
Human review

Gap:
No monitoring of whether reviewers
actually detect incorrect recommendations
```

The gap is not necessarily absence of a control.

It may be **insufficient control effectiveness**.

---

## 124. Step 34 — Define Additional Measures

Potential measures include:

* Additional human review.
* Improved user training.
* Better escalation.
* Additional testing.
* Improved accessibility.
* Stronger transparency.
* Monitoring of outcomes.
* Restricted use cases.
* Reduced AI autonomy.
* Improved remediation.

Controls should directly address the identified impact.

---

## 125. Step 35 — Assign Ownership

Every significant action should have an owner.

Possible owners:

* AI business owner.
* Product owner.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Compliance.
* HR.
* Risk.
* Vendor management.
* Executive sponsor.

Ownership prevents findings from becoming purely theoretical.

---

## 126. Step 36 — Document Evidence

Evidence should support important conclusions.

Examples:

* User research.
* Testing.
* Complaints.
* Incident records.
* Outcome data.
* Stakeholder interviews.
* Accessibility testing.
* Process documentation.
* Vendor documentation.

A defensible assessment should distinguish:

```text
Evidence
vs.
Assumption
vs.
Professional Judgment
```

---

## 127. Step 37 — Document Uncertainty

Where evidence is incomplete, document the uncertainty.

For example:

> "The organization currently lacks sufficient evidence to determine whether the AI produces materially different outcomes across all user populations."

This is stronger than pretending the uncertainty does not exist.

---

## 128. Step 38 — Determine Impact Significance

Bring the assessment together.

A simplified conceptual model is:

```text
Impact Significance
        =
Severity
+ Scale
+ Duration
+ Irreversibility
+ Reach
+ Vulnerability
+ Autonomy
```

This is a conceptual framework rather than a mandatory mathematical formula.

---

## 129. Do Not Automatically Add Scores

Human and social impact cannot always be reduced to arithmetic.

For example:

> A low-frequency impact may still be unacceptable if the consequence is extremely serious and difficult to reverse.

Therefore, quantitative scoring should support—not replace—professional judgment.

---

## 130. Step 39 — Determine Required Governance Response

Possible outcomes include:

```text
Low Concern
    ↓
Proceed

Moderate Concern
    ↓
Proceed with Controls

Significant Concern
    ↓
Enhanced Review / Conditions

Unacceptable Concern
    ↓
Redesign / Restrict / Do Not Proceed
```

The organization should define decision thresholds.

---

## 131. Step 40 — Obtain Appropriate Approval

The person conducting the assessment should not necessarily be the person accepting the resulting exposure.

A sound governance model separates:

```text
Assessment
    ↓
Recommendation
    ↓
Decision
    ↓
Accountability
```

Appropriate management authority should make consequential decisions.

---

## 132. Assessment Record

A practical assessment record might look like:

| Area            | Finding                                      |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| AI Use Case     | Recruitment screening                        |
| Stakeholders    | Applicants, recruiters, HR                   |
| Impact          | Employment opportunity                       |
| Scenario        | Qualified applicant incorrectly ranked lower |
| Severity        | Significant                                  |

| Control Gap     | Limited outcome monitoring                   |
| Action          | Implement outcome monitoring                 |

---

## 133. Stakeholder Consultation

For significant systems, consultation may improve assessment quality.

Relevant stakeholders may include:

* Business users.
* Employees.
* Customers.
* Subject-matter experts.
* Accessibility specialists.
* Risk professionals.
* Privacy professionals.
* Legal.
* Security.
* Compliance.

Different stakeholders see different consequences.

---

## 134. Avoid Token Consultation

Consultation should not become:

> "We asked stakeholders whether they had concerns."

A stronger approach records:

* Who was consulted.
* What concerns were raised.
* What evidence was provided.
* What changes were made.
* Which concerns were rejected.
* Why they were rejected.

This creates an auditable decision trail.

---

## 135. Impact Assessment Workshop

A practical workshop can use the following sequence:

```text
1. Explain AI use case
2. Map process
3. Identify stakeholders
4. Identify positive effects
5. Identify negative effects
6. Develop scenarios
7. Assess severity
8. Assess scale
9. Identify existing safeguards
10. Identify gaps
11. Define actions
12. Determine governance decision
```

---

## 136. Facilitating the Workshop

The GRC facilitator should challenge statements such as:

> "The AI is only advisory."

Follow-up:

> "How much influence does the recommendation actually have?"

Or:

> "There is always a human reviewer."

Follow-up:

> "Can that reviewer realistically override the recommendation?"

The purpose is to expose assumptions.

---

## 137. Evidence Versus Assumptions

Consider:

> "Users will always independently verify AI output."

This is an assumption unless supported by evidence.

A better assessment asks:

> "What evidence demonstrates that users independently verify AI output?"

This distinction improves assessment credibility.

---

## 138. Testing Human Behavior

Where human reliance is important, organizations may test:

* Whether users notice AI errors.
* Whether users challenge recommendations.
* Whether users over-rely on AI.
* Whether users understand confidence limitations.
* Whether users use escalation procedures.

Human behavior can materially influence actual impact.

---

## 139. Impact Assessment as a Lifecycle Activity

The assessment should not necessarily occur only once.

A useful lifecycle is:

```text
Design
  ↓
Initial Assessment
  ↓
Testing
  ↓
Deployment
  ↓
Monitoring
  ↓
Material Change
  ↓
Reassessment
```

The assessment should remain aligned with actual system use.

---

## 140. Reassessment Triggers

Examples include:

* New AI capability.
* New model.
* New data.
* New user group.
* New geography.
* New business purpose.
* Increased automation.
* Increased scale.
* Significant incident.
* Material stakeholder concern.
* Significant vendor change.

These triggers should be defined by the organization.

---

## 141. Change in Scale

Scale is especially important.

Example:

```text
Pilot
100 users
    ↓
Low observed impact

Production
1,000,000 users
    ↓
Potentially different impact profile
```

A successful pilot does not automatically prove that large-scale deployment has the same impact.

---

## 142. Change in Population

An AI system may initially serve employees and later be offered to customers.

That change can introduce:

* New stakeholders.
* New expectations.
* New consequences.
* New accessibility requirements.
* New support needs.

The assessment should therefore consider population changes.

---

## 143. Change in Decision Authority

Another significant change occurs when AI moves from:

> Information support

to:

> Recommendation

and then:

> Automated action.

The impact profile can increase as AI receives greater authority.

---

## 144. Change in Business Purpose

A system originally assessed for:

> Internal productivity

may later be used for:

> Employee evaluation.

That is not merely a configuration change.

It may fundamentally change the human impact.

---

## 145. Impact Assessment Metrics

Organizations can monitor whether their assessment process is functioning.

Potential metrics include:

* Percentage of AI systems assessed.
* Percentage reassessed after material changes.
* Number of significant findings.
* Number of overdue actions.
* Number of stakeholder concerns.
* Number of AI-related complaints.
* Number of impact-related incidents.
* Percentage of actions completed.

Metrics should support governance rather than become an administrative exercise.

---

## 146. Leading Indicators

Leading indicators may include:

* Increasing AI use outside approved purposes.
* Rising user complaints.
* Increasing override rates.
* Declining human-review quality.
* New stakeholder concerns.
* Increased AI autonomy.

These may indicate that the impact profile is changing.

---

## 147. Lagging Indicators

Lagging indicators may include:

* Confirmed adverse outcomes.
* Complaints.
* Regulatory findings.
* Remediation cases.
* AI incidents.
* Customer losses.

A mature program uses both leading and lagging indicators.

---

## 148. Documentation Traceability

A strong assessment should allow an auditor or reviewer to trace:

```text
Impact
 ↓
Scenario
 ↓
Evidence
 ↓
Safeguard
 ↓
Control Gap
 ↓
Action
 ↓
Owner
 ↓
Decision
```

This creates accountability.

---

## 149. Quality Review Questions

Before finalizing the assessment, ask:

* Is the AI use case clearly described?
* Is the actual use understood?
* Are affected stakeholders identified?
* Are positive and negative effects documented?
* Are impact scenarios specific?
* Is severity justified?
* Is scale considered?
* Is duration considered?
* Is reversibility considered?
* Is human oversight evaluated?
* Are power imbalances considered?
* Are alternatives available?
* Are social effects considered?
* Is evidence documented?
* Are assumptions identified?
* Are controls tested for effectiveness?
* Are actions assigned?
* Is the governance decision documented?

## 150. Practical GRC Example

### Scenario

An organization deploys AI to prioritize customer-service cases.

### AI Function

The system assigns priority scores.

### Decision Pathway

```text
      ↓

      ↓

      ↓

```

### Potential Human Impact

A customer may experience delayed assistance because of an incorrect AI classification.

* How accurate is the classification?
* Who is affected?
* Can customers challenge prioritization?
* Is human review available?
* How quickly are errors detected?
* Can queue decisions be corrected?
* Are outcomes monitored across populations?

This demonstrates how the methodology moves from technical function to human consequence.

## Part 3 — Governance, Controls, Evidence, and Decision-Making

### 154. Governance Objective

Human and social impact assessment becomes valuable to GRC when its findings are connected to **governance, accountability, controls, evidence, and management decisions**.

The governance objective is to ensure that:

> **Material effects on people and society are identified, documented, controlled, monitored, and escalated to the appropriate decision-makers.**

A useful governance chain is:

```text
Human / Social Impact
        ↓
Risk or Concern
        ↓
Control Requirement
        ↓
Evidence
        ↓
Monitoring
        ↓
Governance Decision
        ↓
Accountability
```

---

## 155. Accountability Structure

A mature organization should clearly distinguish between:

* **System ownership**
* **Business accountability**
* **Risk oversight**
* **Control ownership**
* **Technical responsibility**
* **Human-impact expertise**
* **Final decision authority**

The GRC function should not automatically become the owner of every human-impact issue.

Its role is often to establish the governance mechanism and challenge whether the responsible owners have appropriately addressed the identified impacts.

---

## 156. Three Lines Perspective

Human and social impact assessment can be understood through a Three Lines model.

### First line

Business and operational teams:

* Own the AI use case.
* Operate the controls.
* Manage day-to-day consequences.
* Escalate issues.

### Second line

Risk, compliance, privacy, security, AI governance, and related functions:

* Establish frameworks.
* Challenge assessments.
* Define requirements.
* Monitor risk.
* Provide oversight.

### Third line

Internal audit:

* Independently evaluates governance.
* Reviews control effectiveness.
* Tests whether the framework operates as intended.

This separation supports accountability and independent challenge.

---

## 157. Policy Requirements

An organization can establish an AI governance policy requiring human and social impact assessment when certain criteria are met.

For example:

```text
AI System
   ↓
Does it materially affect people?
   ↓
YES
   ↓
Impact Assessment Required
```

The policy should establish:

* Applicability.
* Roles.
* Assessment requirements.
* Approval thresholds.
* Documentation.
* Review frequency.
* Escalation.
* Exception handling.

---

## 158. Applicability Criteria

Not every AI system requires the same level of assessment.

Possible triggers include:

* Significant human decisions.
* Employment-related decisions.
* Access to important services.
* High-volume public-facing use.
* Significant autonomy.
* Vulnerable populations.
* Large-scale deployment.
* Significant monitoring.
* Material social consequences.

The organization should define its own thresholds.

---

## 159. Risk-Based Governance

A mature framework should avoid treating every AI system identically.

A simple model is:

```text
Lower Impact
     ↓
Standard Assessment

Higher Impact
     ↓
Enhanced Assessment

Very High Impact
     ↓
Executive / Specialized Review
```

This allows resources to be concentrated where consequences are greatest.

---

## 160. Impact Assessment as a Governance Gate

The assessment can become a formal decision gate.

For example:

```text
AI Development
      ↓
Impact Assessment
      ↓
Risk Review
      ↓
Approval
      ↓
Deployment
```

Deployment should be conditional where material issues remain unresolved.

---

## 161. Conditional Approval

An organization may approve an AI system subject to conditions.

Example:

> Deployment is approved provided that human review, user escalation, outcome monitoring, and documented remediation are implemented before production release.

This creates a direct relationship between assessment findings and deployment authorization.

---

## 162. Non-Approval

The organization should also have the ability to conclude:

> **Do not deploy.**

This may be appropriate where:

* Impacts are unacceptable.
* Controls are inadequate.
* The purpose cannot be justified.
* Consequences cannot reasonably be mitigated.
* Required evidence is unavailable.
* Governance requirements cannot be satisfied.

A mature governance framework must allow negative decisions.

---

## 163. Control Design

Controls should be designed against specific impact scenarios.

Example:

**Impact**

> AI recommendation incorrectly denies a customer opportunity.

**Control**

> Mandatory human review before denial.

**Evidence**

> Review records demonstrating human approval.

This provides traceability:

```text
Impact
 ↓
Control
 ↓
Evidence
```

---

## 164. Preventive Controls

Preventive controls attempt to stop undesirable impacts before they occur.

Examples:

* Restricted AI use cases.
* Human approval.
* Input validation.
* Access restrictions.
* User eligibility rules.
* Output restrictions.
* Mandatory review.
* Controlled deployment.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation
      ↓
Mandatory Human Approval
      ↓
Customer Action
```

---

## 165. Detective Controls

Detective controls identify impacts or control failures after or while they occur.

Examples:

* Outcome monitoring.
* Complaint monitoring.
* Exception reporting.
* Human override analysis.
* Incident monitoring.
* Periodic testing.

Example:

```text
AI Decisions
     ↓
Monitoring
     ↓
Unusual Outcome Detected
     ↓
Investigation
```

---

## 166. Corrective Controls

Corrective controls respond after an impact or failure is identified.

Examples:

* Reversing decisions.
* Correcting records.
* Reprocessing cases.
* Providing human review.
* Remediation.
* Compensation where appropriate.
* Model or process changes.

Corrective controls are particularly important when impacts are reversible.

---

## 167. Compensating Controls

Sometimes the preferred control cannot be implemented.

A compensating control may provide an alternative protection.

Example:

> If real-time model explainability is unavailable, the organization may provide mandatory human review and an alternative manual decision process for consequential cases.

Compensating controls should not automatically be treated as equivalent.

Their effectiveness must be evaluated.

---

## 168. Human Oversight as a Control

Human oversight can be a control, but it should have clearly defined operating requirements.

Document:

* Who performs review.
* What is reviewed.
* When review occurs.
* What authority the reviewer has.
* What constitutes escalation.
* How decisions are documented.
* How review quality is measured.

---

## 169. Meaningful Human Oversight

A useful test is:

```text
Can the reviewer understand?
        ↓
Can the reviewer challenge?
        ↓
Can the reviewer override?
        ↓
Can the reviewer intervene in time?
        ↓
Is the reviewer accountable?
```

If the answer is consistently "no," human involvement may be largely procedural rather than substantive.

---

## 170. Human Override

An override mechanism should be practical.

Questions include:

* Is override technically possible?
* Is it permitted by policy?
* Are employees encouraged to use it?
* Are overrides recorded?
* Are unusual override patterns investigated?

A control that technically exists but is practically unusable is weak.

---

## 171. Escalation Controls

An AI system should have defined escalation pathways where appropriate.

Example:

```text
AI Output
   ↓
Normal Case → Standard Process

Exception
   ↓
Human Review

Serious Concern
   ↓
Specialist Review

Critical Concern
   ↓
Management Escalation
```

The thresholds should be documented.

---

## 172. Appeal and Contestability

Where individuals may be materially affected, governance may require mechanisms to:

* Challenge decisions.
* Request review.
* Correct information.
* Obtain human intervention.
* Seek remediation.

The mechanism should be accessible and understandable to the affected population.

---

## 173. Remediation Governance

Remediation should be treated as an accountable process.

A remediation workflow may be:

```text
Impact Identified
      ↓
Affected Person / Group
      ↓
Case Review
      ↓
Correction
      ↓
Remediation
      ↓
Root Cause Analysis
      ↓
Control Improvement
```

The organization should consider both the individual case and the systemic cause.

---

## 174. Individual Remediation Versus Systemic Remediation

These are different.

### Individual remediation

Correct one person's outcome.

### Systemic remediation

Address the reason similar impacts may occur repeatedly.

Example:

```text
10 Incorrect Decisions
        ↓
Correct 10 Cases
        +
Investigate Why They Occurred
```

A mature GRC program should consider both.

---

## 175. Evidence Hierarchy

Evidence can vary in quality.

For example:

```text
Strong
    ↓
Observed production outcomes
Validated testing
Independent review
Structured stakeholder research

Moderate
    ↓
Operational records
Surveys
Expert assessment

Weak
    ↓
Assumptions
Anecdotal statements
Unverified expectations
```

This does not mean weak evidence is useless.

It means conclusions should reflect evidence quality.

## 176. Evidence Traceability

Every significant conclusion should ideally have a traceable basis.

Example:

```text

Owner:

Status:

```

This makes the assessment auditable.

## 179. Impact Statement

A strong impact statement should be specific.

Weak:

> "AI could harm customers."

Stronger:

> "Incorrect AI prioritization may delay service for affected customers, particularly where automated prioritization is not routinely reviewed."

The second statement provides something that can actually be governed.

## 180. Impact-to-Control Mapping

A practical control matrix might look like:

| Impact             | Cause                    | Control             | Evidence           | Owner      |
| ------------------ | ------------------------ | ------------------- | ------------------ | ---------- |
| Incorrect decision | AI error                 | Human review        | Review logs        | Operations |
| Exclusion          | Accessibility limitation | Alternative process | Accessibility test | Product    |
| Reduced autonomy   | Excessive automation     | Override mechanism  | Override records   | Business   |
| Loss of trust      | Poor transparency        | User notification   | Approved notice    | Compliance |

This is one of the most useful GRC artifacts.

## 181. Control Effectiveness

Control effectiveness should consider:

### Design effectiveness

> Is the control capable of addressing the impact?

### Operating effectiveness

> Does the control actually operate as designed?

Example:

> Human review is required.

Design question:

> Would human review detect the problem?

Operating question:

> Are reviewers actually performing effective reviews?

* Sample AI decisions.

* Verify remediation.
* Test accessibility.
* Compare expected and actual workflows.
* Interview reviewers.

Testing should provide evidence that controls operate in practice.

## 185. Override Rate

An unusually high or low override rate can be informative.

For example:

```text

Could indicate high accuracy
OR
```

The metric must therefore be interpreted in context.

Complaints can be an important source of human-impact evidence.

* Repeated AI errors.

* Unfair outcomes.
* Accessibility problems.
* Poor explanations.
* Inability to appeal.

Complaints can reveal impacts not detected through technical monitoring.

## 188. Incident Management

A material human-impact event may need to enter the organization's incident-management process.

Example:

> An AI system repeatedly makes incorrect decisions affecting customers.

The organization should determine:

* Whether it qualifies as an AI incident.
* Who must be notified.
* Whether deployment should be restricted.
* Whether affected people require remediation.
* Whether root-cause analysis is necessary.

## 189. AI Incident Versus Human-Impact Issue

Not every impact necessarily constitutes an incident.

A potential issue may be identified during assessment before deployment.

An incident is generally associated with an actual event requiring response under organizational criteria.

The organization should define its own classification thresholds.

## 192. Risk Acceptance

Where material human or social exposure remains, acceptance should be performed by an appropriately authorized decision-maker.

The acceptance record should identify:

* What controls exist.

* Who accepted it.
* When it was accepted.

## 206. Governance Red Flags

A GRC professional should pay particular attention when hearing:

> "It's only an AI recommendation."

Potential response:

> How much influence does the recommendation have on the final decision?

---

> "There is a human in the loop."

Ask:

> Can that person meaningfully challenge and override the AI?

---

> "The model is 95% accurate."

Ask:

> What happens to the people represented by the remaining 5%?

---

> "There haven't been any complaints."

Ask:

> Do affected people have a practical way to complain?

---

> "The vendor manages the model."

Ask:

> Who remains accountable for our use of the AI?

## 207. Governance Maturity Model

A useful maturity model is:

### Level 1 — Ad hoc

* No consistent assessment.
* Individual teams make decisions.
* Limited documentation.

### Level 2 — Defined

* Basic methodology exists.
* Roles are documented.
* Assessments are performed.

### Level 3 — Managed

* Assessments connect to controls.
* Evidence is collected.
* Monitoring exists.

### Level 4 — Integrated

* Impact assessment is embedded into lifecycle governance.
* Decisions use impact information.
* Metrics are reported.

### Level 5 — Optimized

* Continuous monitoring.
* Outcome-based governance.
* Advanced stakeholder engagement.
* Lessons learned improve the framework.

## 208. What Good Governance Looks Like

A mature organization can answer:

**Who is affected?**

> Identified.

**How could they be affected?**

> Documented through specific scenarios.

**What evidence supports the assessment?**

> Traceable.

**What controls address the impacts?**

> Mapped.

**Are controls effective?**

> Tested.

**Who owns the remaining exposure?**

> Explicitly assigned.

**Who makes the final decision?**

> Authorized governance authority.

**How will we know if the impact changes?**

> Monitoring and reassessment triggers exist.

## 209. GRC Interview Answer

If asked:

> **"How would you govern human and social impacts from AI?"**

A strong answer is:

> "I would first establish clear accountability and require a proportionate impact assessment based on the AI use case and affected population. I would map the decision pathway, identify human and social impact scenarios, assess severity, scale, duration, reversibility, autonomy, vulnerability, and distribution, and then map each material impact to preventive, detective, and corrective controls. I would require evidence for significant conclusions, validate that human oversight is meaningful rather than nominal, establish escalation and remediation mechanisms, and assign accountable owners. Finally, I would connect the assessment to deployment approval, monitoring, incident management, change management, and periodic reassessment so that human and social impacts remain governed throughout the AI lifecycle."

# 06.12 — Human and Social Impact Assessment

## Part 4 — Advanced Application, Scenarios, and Interview Mastery

### 213. Advanced Perspective

At an advanced GRC level, human and social impact assessment should not be treated as a checklist asking whether an AI system is "fair" or "safe."

The real question is:

> **How does the AI system change the distribution of decisions, opportunities, power, autonomy, benefits, and burdens among the people it affects?**

This requires connecting the AI system to its broader operating environment.

```text
AI Capability
      ↓
Business Process
      ↓
Human Behavior
      ↓
Individual Outcomes
      ↓
Group-Level Patterns
      ↓
Social Consequences
      ↓
Governance Response
```

---

## 214. The Difference Between Model Risk and Human Impact

A model can be technically reliable while the overall AI use case still produces undesirable human outcomes.

For example:

```text
Model
95% Accurate
      ↓
Business Process
      ↓
AI Recommendation
      ↓
Human Decision
      ↓
Employment Outcome
```

The model's accuracy answers one question.

The impact assessment asks another:

> **What happens to people when the organization uses that model in this particular decision process?**

This distinction is fundamental for AI GRC.

---

## 215. Context Changes Impact

The same AI capability can create different levels of impact depending on context.

Consider an image-classification model.

### Low-impact context

Identifying objects in photographs.

### Higher-impact context

Assisting security personnel.

### Potentially significant context

Supporting decisions about whether a person receives access to an important facility or service.

The underlying technology may be similar.

The **human consequence is different**.

Therefore:

> **Impact is contextual, not purely technological.**

## 216. The Consequence Chain

A useful advanced framework is:

```text
TECHNOLOGY
    ↓
USE
    ↓
DECISION
    ↓
PERSON
    ↓
CONSEQUENCE
    ↓
RECOVERY
```

For each stage, ask:

### Technology

What does the AI actually do?

### Use

How is it used?

### Decision

What decisions depend on its output?

### Person

Who is affected?

### Consequence

What happens to them?

### Recovery

Can the consequence be corrected?

## 217. Decision Dependency

One of the strongest indicators of potential impact is **how dependent a decision is on AI output**.

Consider:

```text
AI provides optional information
        ↓
Low dependency

AI provides recommendation
        ↓
Moderate dependency

AI strongly influences decision
        ↓
Higher dependency

AI automatically determines outcome
        ↓
Very high dependency
```

This does not mean automation is automatically unacceptable.

It means the level of dependency should influence the assessment.

## 218. AI Influence Versus AI Authority

These concepts should be distinguished.

### AI influence

AI affects a human decision.

### AI authority

AI effectively determines what happens.

Example:

> A recruiter receives an AI ranking.

The AI may have influence.

If the organization automatically rejects everyone below a threshold, the AI effectively has much greater authority.

This distinction is important in governance.

---

## 219. Human Oversight Stress Test

Do not simply record:

> "Human oversight exists."

Perform a stress test.

Ask:

1. Does the human see the AI output?
2. Does the human understand its limitations?
3. Does the human have enough time?
4. Can the human disagree?
5. Can the human override?
6. Is overriding permitted?
7. Is overriding discouraged?
8. Is override activity monitored?
9. Can the human obtain additional evidence?
10. Can the decision be reversed afterward?

The answers determine whether human oversight is meaningful.

---

## 220. Automation Bias Scenario

Imagine:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Recruiter
       ↓
"AI probably knows better."
       ↓
Recommendation accepted
```

Even with a human decision-maker, the AI may effectively determine the outcome.

Therefore:

> **Human presence does not necessarily equal human control.**

---

## 221. Rubber-Stamp Human Review

A particularly weak control occurs when humans merely approve AI recommendations.

Example:

```text
AI → Recommendation
       ↓
Human → Approve
       ↓
Decision
```

If the human rarely challenges the AI, governance should investigate whether the review process is genuinely independent.

## 222. Impact Amplification

AI can amplify the consequences of existing processes because it can operate:

* Faster.
* At greater scale.
* More consistently.
* Continuously.
* Across large populations.

This creates an important principle:

> **Automation can amplify both benefits and weaknesses.**

A flawed process operating manually may affect hundreds of people.

The same process automated through AI may affect millions.

---

## 223. Scale as an Impact Multiplier

Conceptually:

```text
Impact per Decision × Number of Decisions
             ↓
Potential Aggregate Effect
```

This is not necessarily a formal risk equation.

It is a way of recognizing that scale matters.

## 224. Feedback Loops

AI can create feedback loops.

Example:

```text
Historical Decisions
      ↓
AI Training / Inputs
      ↓
AI Prediction
      ↓
New Decisions
      ↓
New Data
      ↓
Future AI Decisions
```

If undesirable patterns enter this loop, repeated decisions can reinforce them.

Human and social assessment should therefore consider whether the AI system participates in a self-reinforcing process.

---

## 225. Organizational Feedback Loop

A similar loop can occur operationally:

```text

      ↓
Employee Trust
      ↓
Reduced Human Challenge
      ↓
More AI Reliance
      ↓
Greater AI Authority
      ↓
Even Less Human Challenge
```

This can gradually change the organization's decision culture.

## 226. Dependency Risk

Long-term dependence on AI may create human and organizational effects.

Potential examples:

* Loss of human expertise.
* Reduced independent judgment.
* Reduced ability to operate without AI.
* Increased operational dependency.
* Reduced resilience.

The relevance depends on the AI system and business context.

---

## 227. Human Skill Atrophy

If AI consistently performs a task previously performed by humans, human capability may decline over time.

For example:

```text
AI Performs Task
      ↓
Human Performs Less Frequently
      ↓
Skill Declines
      ↓
AI Failure Occurs
      ↓
Human Recovery Capability Reduced
```

This is especially relevant for critical operational functions.

---

## 228. Organizational Resilience

An impact assessment can therefore ask:

> **If the AI system becomes unavailable or unreliable, can humans still perform the essential function?**

Relevant controls may include:

* Manual fallback.
* Training.
* Business continuity.
* Alternative systems.
* Periodic human practice.

---

## 229. Power Concentration

AI can influence who has decision-making power.

For example:

```text
Before AI
Many employees exercise judgment

After AI
Centralized AI system determines recommendations
```

The organization should consider whether AI unintentionally concentrates decision-making authority.

---

## 230. Information Asymmetry

AI can create information differences between parties.

For example:

```text
Organization
  ↓
Has AI prediction about customer

Customer
  ↓
Does not know how prediction was produced
```

This may affect the individual's ability to understand or challenge decisions.

The assessment should consider whether this information asymmetry creates material concerns.

## 231. Contestability as a Governance Principle

A powerful advanced question is:

> **Can an affected person meaningfully contest an AI-influenced outcome?**

A strong contestability mechanism may include:

```text
Notice
  ↓
Explanation
  ↓
Challenge
  ↓
Human Review
  ↓
Correction
  ↓
Remediation
```

Each step should be practical rather than merely theoretical.

---

## 232. Accessibility of Contestability

A technically available appeal process may still be ineffective if:

* It is difficult to find.
* It requires excessive effort.
* It is available only in one language.
* It requires digital access.
* It takes too long.
* Users do not understand the process.

Therefore:

> **Contestability must be evaluated from the perspective of the affected person.**

## 233. Cumulative Human Impact

A system may have a small effect each time but a significant cumulative effect.

Example:

```text
One Recommendation
       ↓
Minor influence

Thousands of Recommendations
       ↓
Behavioral pattern

Long-term exposure
       ↓
Potentially significant social effect
```

This is particularly relevant to personalization and recommendation systems.

## 234. Population-Level Effects

Individual decisions may aggregate into population-level outcomes.

For example:

```text

      ↓

      ↓
Group-Level Difference
      ↓
Population-Level Effect
```

The organization should therefore consider whether repeated use could produce systematic consequences.

## 235. Distribution of Benefits and Burdens

### Burden

> Who bears the risks or disadvantages?

These groups may not be the same.

Example:

```text
→ Gains efficiency

Customers
→ Receive faster service

Certain users
→ Experience greater difficulty accessing support
```

Overall efficiency does not automatically eliminate distributional concerns.

## 237. Vulnerability Analysis

Vulnerability should not simply mean:

> "This group is vulnerable."

The assessment should explain **why the AI system creates greater consequences for them**.

For example:

```text

   ↓

   ↓
High dependency on service
   ↓
Greater consequence from error
```

This provides a stronger analytical basis.

## 238. Power-Imbalance Analysis

Ask:

> Who can make decisions?

> Who must live with those decisions?

> Who can challenge them?

> Who controls the AI?

This can reveal power asymmetries that technical testing would not identify.

## 239. Social Trust as an Outcome

```text
 ↓
```

## 240. Trust Recovery

Organizations may need:

* Transparency.

* Human engagement.
* Corrective action.
* Remediation.
* Demonstrated control improvement.

Trust can therefore become part of the remediation strategy.

## 256. Worst-Reasonable-Case Analysis

## 257. Reasonable Foreseeability

* Intended use.

* Operating context.
* Available evidence.
* Expert knowledge.

The assessment should avoid turning into unlimited speculation.

## 258. Unknown Unknowns

* Is it sufficiently staffed?

* Has it been tested?

## 264. Impact Assessment and Business Continuity

* Human consequences of AI failure.
* Human consequences of continuing unsafe AI operation.

The correct decision may be to fail safely into a manual process.

## 265. Documentation of Judgment

Professional judgment should be documented when material decisions are made.

## 267. Executive-Level Question

A senior executive may ask:

> **"Why should we care about this if the AI improves efficiency?"**

A strong GRC response:

> "Efficiency is one dimension of value. Human and social impact assessment ensures that we also understand who benefits, who bears the burden, what consequences could arise, how severe and reversible they are, and whether our controls are sufficient. The objective is not to prevent useful AI adoption but to ensure that efficiency gains are achieved within an acceptable and accountable impact profile."

## 268. Interview Question — Methodology

### Question

> "Walk me through your human and social impact assessment."

### Answer

> "I begin by defining the AI system, its intended purpose, actual use, and affected population. I then map the decision pathway from AI input and output through human or automated decisions to the final real-world outcome. From there, I identify credible positive and negative impact scenarios and assess severity, scale, duration, reversibility, autonomy, vulnerability, power imbalance, distribution of benefits and burdens, and the ability of affected individuals to understand or challenge outcomes. I then evaluate existing preventive, detective, and corrective controls, validate meaningful human oversight, identify evidence and gaps, assign accountable owners, and translate the findings into an appropriate governance decision. Finally, I establish monitoring and reassessment triggers so the assessment remains aligned with actual use."

---

## 269. Interview Question — Human Oversight

### Question

> "How do you determine whether human oversight is effective?"

### Answer

> "I don't treat the presence of a human reviewer as sufficient. I assess whether the reviewer has the information, competence, time, authority, and practical ability to challenge and override the AI output. I also look at actual behavior, such as override rates, review quality, escalation patterns, and evidence from sampled decisions. The key distinction is between human involvement and meaningful human control."

---

## 270. Interview Question — Social Impact

### Question

> "What is the difference between human and social impact?"

### Answer

> "Human impact focuses primarily on consequences for individuals, such as autonomy, dignity, well-being, employment, access, or economic opportunity. Social impact looks at broader effects on groups, communities, relationships, participation, trust, inclusion, and societal patterns. The two are connected because repeated individual outcomes can aggregate into broader social effects."

---

## 271. Interview Question — Technical Accuracy

### Question

> "If the AI model is highly accurate, why do you still need a human impact assessment?"

### Answer

> "Because technical accuracy measures model performance, not whether the overall use of the AI produces acceptable human outcomes. A highly accurate model can still be used in a process that creates excessive surveillance, reduces autonomy, creates unequal access, or produces difficult-to-reverse consequences. I therefore assess the complete pathway from AI capability through organizational use to real-world human outcomes."

---

## 272. Interview Question — Vulnerable Groups

### Question

> "How would you address potentially vulnerable populations?"

### Answer

> "I would first identify whether particular populations are more exposed to the consequences because of dependency, limited ability to understand or challenge decisions, limited alternatives, or greater consequences from error. I would then assess whether the existing controls are sufficient and whether additional safeguards, human review, alternative access channels, or enhanced monitoring are appropriate."

---

## 273. Interview Question — Unacceptable Impact

### Question

> "What would you do if the impact assessment identified an unacceptable human impact?"

### Answer

> "I would not treat that as simply another open risk. I would escalate it according to the governance framework, identify whether the use case can be redesigned or restricted, evaluate whether stronger safeguards could reduce the impact to an acceptable level, and ensure that deployment does not proceed without the appropriate authorized decision. Where the impact cannot reasonably be controlled or justified, stopping or restricting the use case should remain a governance option."

---

## 274. Interview Question — Vendor AI

### Question

> "What if the AI is provided by a third-party vendor?"

### Answer

> "The vendor relationship does not eliminate the organization's responsibility for how the AI is used. I would assess the intended use, affected population, vendor documentation, known limitations, contractual obligations, change management, incident notification, and available evidence. I would also identify controls that remain under our direct control because vendor assurance alone does not address every human or social consequence of our deployment context."

---

## 275. Interview Question — Monitoring

### Question

> "How do you know whether human impact changes after deployment?"

### Answer

> "I establish outcome-based monitoring tied to the original impact scenarios. Depending on the use case, that could include complaints, appeals, overrides, adverse outcomes, service delays, accessibility issues, or other relevant indicators. I also establish reassessment triggers such as changes in model, purpose, population, scale, autonomy, or significant incidents. This allows governance to respond to real-world evidence rather than relying solely on the initial assessment."

---

## 276. Interview Question — GRC Role

### Question

> "What is the role of GRC in human and social impact assessment?"

### Answer

> "GRC provides the governance structure, methodology, challenge, oversight, and traceability. I would ensure the assessment is proportionate, evidence-based, linked to controls, properly owned, and connected to approval and monitoring processes. I would not assume the GRC function should make every business decision; instead, I would ensure that accountable decision-makers have sufficient information to make and document those decisions."

## 289. Advanced Principle — Outcome Over Intention

```text
      ↓
```

```text
    Real-world outcomes

12. REASSESS
    When circumstances materially change
```

## 297. Final Interview Answer

If an interviewer asks:

> **"How would you approach human and social impact assessment for an AI system?"**

A polished senior-level answer is:

> "I approach it from the real-world outcome rather than only the technical characteristics of the model. I first define the AI system, its intended and actual use, and the population affected. I then map the complete pathway from AI input and output through human or automated decision-making to the final outcome. I identify credible positive and negative scenarios and assess severity, scale, duration, reversibility, AI influence, autonomy, vulnerability, power imbalance, and the distribution of benefits and burdens. I then evaluate whether human oversight and contestability are genuinely effective, rather than simply present on paper. From a GRC perspective, I map material impacts to preventive, detective, and corrective controls, validate those controls with evidence, assign accountable owners, and establish monitoring and escalation thresholds. Finally, I connect the assessment to the governance decision—whether to approve, approve with conditions, restrict, redesign, or stop the use case—and ensure that material changes or emerging evidence trigger reassessment."

> **Human and Social Impact Assessment is the disciplined evaluation of how an AI system affects individuals, groups, communities, and society across its real-world lifecycle.
