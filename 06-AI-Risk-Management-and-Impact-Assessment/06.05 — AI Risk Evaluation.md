# 06.05 — AI Risk Evaluation

## Part 1 — Foundations, Purpose, Principles, and Decision Context

### 242. Introduction to AI Risk Evaluation

AI risk evaluation is the process of **comparing analyzed AI risks against established organizational risk criteria to determine their significance and support a risk decision**.

Risk analysis asks:

> **"What is the nature and magnitude of the AI risk?"**

Risk evaluation asks:

> **"Is this level of AI risk acceptable within the organization's defined criteria?"**

This distinction is fundamental.

```text
AI Risk Identification
        ↓
AI Risk Analysis
        ↓
AI Risk Evaluation
        ↓
Risk Decision
```

Risk evaluation therefore converts analytical information into a **governance judgment**.

---

### 243. Analysis vs Evaluation

The two activities should not be confused.

**Risk analysis** determines characteristics such as:

* Likelihood.
* Consequence.
* Exposure.
* Control effectiveness.
* Uncertainty.
* Detectability.
* Reversibility.

**Risk evaluation** determines the significance of those characteristics relative to organizational criteria.

For example:

> Analysis: Likelihood = Likely; Consequence = Major.

> Evaluation: The resulting risk exceeds the organization's acceptable risk threshold and therefore requires management attention before deployment.

---

### 244. Why AI Risk Evaluation Matters

AI systems can create risks that organizations may not be accustomed to evaluating.

Examples include:

* AI-generated misinformation.
* Model bias.
* Autonomous actions.
* Privacy impacts.
* Security vulnerabilities.
* Discriminatory outcomes.
* Loss of human oversight.
* AI supply-chain dependencies.
* Large-scale automated errors.

Risk evaluation determines whether these risks are:

* Acceptable.
* Acceptable with conditions.
* Require additional controls.
* Require escalation.
* Require a different treatment decision.
* Potentially unacceptable.

---

### 245. Evaluation Is Context-Dependent

A risk does not have an absolute meaning outside its context.

Consider:

> An AI system has a 2% error rate.

Whether that is acceptable depends on its use.

For:

> Internal brainstorming

2% error may be relatively tolerable.

For:

> Safety-critical automated control

2% error could be unacceptable.

Therefore:

```text
Risk Characteristics
        +
Business Context
        +
Risk Criteria
        ↓
Risk Evaluation
```

---

### 246. Organizational Risk Criteria

Risk evaluation requires predefined criteria.

These criteria establish what the organization considers:

* Low risk.
* Moderate risk.
* High risk.
* Critical risk.
* Acceptable risk.
* Unacceptable risk.

For example:

| Risk Score | Classification | Evaluation                  |
| ---------: | -------------- | --------------------------- |
|        1–4 | Low            | Generally acceptable        |
|        5–9 | Moderate       | Management attention        |
|      10–16 | High           | Treatment normally required |
|      17–25 | Critical       | Executive escalation        |

The exact thresholds should be established by the organization's risk methodology.

---

### 247. Risk Appetite

**Risk appetite** describes the amount and type of risk an organization is generally willing to pursue or retain in pursuit of its objectives.

For AI governance, risk appetite may differ across areas.

Example:

```text
AI Innovation
     ↓
Higher Risk Appetite

Customer Privacy
     ↓
Low Risk Appetite

Safety-Critical AI
     ↓
Very Low Risk Appetite
```

An organization may be willing to tolerate greater uncertainty in experimentation than in production systems affecting customers.

---

### 248. Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance defines the acceptable degree of variation around the organization's risk appetite or objectives.

For example:

> The organization may tolerate minor AI output errors in an internal productivity tool but have zero tolerance for unauthorized disclosure of certain highly sensitive information.

Risk tolerance therefore helps establish more specific evaluation boundaries.

---

### 249. Risk Capacity

Risk capacity is different from appetite.

It represents the **maximum amount of risk an organization can absorb without threatening its ability to continue operating or achieve its objectives**.

For example:

```text
Risk Appetite
      ↓
What we are willing to accept

Risk Capacity
      ↓
What we can actually withstand
```

An organization may have a relatively high appetite for AI innovation but limited capacity to absorb a major regulatory or operational failure.

---

### 250. Risk Appetite vs Risk Tolerance vs Risk Capacity

| Concept        | Core Question                                     |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk Appetite  | How much risk are we willing to pursue or accept? |
| Risk Tolerance | How much variation is acceptable?                 |
| Risk Capacity  | How much risk can we withstand?                   |

These concepts help provide context for AI risk evaluation.

---

### 251. AI Risk Criteria

AI-specific evaluation criteria may consider more than conventional enterprise risk scores.

Possible criteria include:

* Safety.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Fairness.
* Human oversight.
* Transparency.
* Accountability.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Operational resilience.
* Financial impact.
* Reputational impact.

The organization should determine which criteria are relevant to its AI environment.

---

### 252. Multi-Dimensional AI Evaluation

An AI system could have:

| Dimension    | Assessment |
| ------------ | ---------- |
| Security     | High       |
| Privacy      | High       |
| Financial    | Moderate   |
| Operational  | Moderate   |
| Human impact | High       |
| Reputation   | Moderate   |

A single aggregate score may hide important information.

Therefore, evaluation should preserve material dimensions even when the organization ultimately produces one overall risk rating.

---

### 253. Evaluation Against Objectives

Risk evaluation should consider the organization's objectives.

For example:

> An AI deployment may improve customer-service efficiency by 30% but introduce a significant privacy risk.

The evaluation asks whether the benefit and risk are consistent with organizational objectives and risk criteria.

This creates a balanced governance perspective:

```text
Business Benefit
       ↕
AI Risk
       ↓
Organizational Decision
```

---

### 254. Risk Evaluation Is Not Risk Elimination

The objective is not necessarily to eliminate every risk.

AI systems inherently involve uncertainty and imperfect outcomes.

The objective is to determine:

> **Which risks are acceptable, which require treatment, and which cannot be accepted under the organization's criteria.**

This distinction is essential for practical AI governance.

---

### 255. Inherent Risk and Residual Risk

Evaluation may consider both:

**Inherent risk**

> Risk before considering existing controls.

**Residual risk**

> Risk remaining after considering controls.

Example:

```text
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Existing Controls
     ↓
Residual Risk
     ↓
Evaluation
```

A system may have:

> High inherent risk

but:

> Moderate residual risk.

Whether moderate residual risk is acceptable depends on organizational criteria.

---

### 256. Control Effectiveness and Evaluation

The presence of a control does not automatically make a risk acceptable.

For example:

> Human review exists.

The evaluator should consider:

* Is it consistently performed?
* Is the reviewer qualified?
* Can the reviewer reject the AI output?
* Does the reviewer have sufficient information?
* How quickly can intervention occur?
* Is the control independently tested?

Only then can its effect on residual risk be appropriately considered.

---

### 257. Uncertainty in Evaluation

AI risk evaluation often involves uncertainty.

Sources include:

* Limited historical data.
* New models.
* Emerging threats.
* Changing user behavior.
* Model evolution.
* Limited testing.
* Incomplete vendor information.

The organization should avoid treating uncertain estimates as precise facts.

---

### 258. Confidence in Evaluation

A useful concept is **confidence**.

Example:

```text
Risk Rating: High
Confidence: High
```

means the organization has strong evidence supporting the assessment.

Another example:

```text
Risk Rating: Moderate
Confidence: Low
```

means the rating is less certain and additional evidence may be necessary.

Confidence does not automatically change the risk score, but it can influence the decision about whether additional analysis or controls are needed.

---

### 259. Evaluation Under Uncertainty

Suppose two AI systems both receive a "Moderate" risk rating.

**System A**

> Strong evidence, extensive testing, stable operating history.

**System B**

> Limited testing, new model, uncertain behavior.

The nominal risk rating is the same, but the governance response may differ because the uncertainty is different.

---

### 260. Risk Evaluation and Precaution

Where potential consequences are severe and evidence is limited, organizations may adopt a more cautious approach.

For example:

```text
High Potential Consequence
+
Low Evidence
+
High Uncertainty
        ↓
Greater Need for Caution
```

The exact decision should follow organizational governance and applicable requirements.

---

### 261. Risk Evaluation Criteria Should Be Established Beforehand

Evaluation becomes less reliable if criteria are changed simply to accommodate a desired outcome.

For example, an organization should not decide:

> "This AI project is strategically important, so let's redefine high risk as acceptable."

Instead, criteria should normally be established independently of individual project outcomes.

---

### 262. Consistency Across AI Systems

A mature AI governance program applies consistent evaluation principles.

For example:

```text
AI Use Case A → Evaluation Criteria
AI Use Case B → Same Core Framework
AI Use Case C → Same Core Framework
```

The specific thresholds may vary by risk domain, but the underlying methodology should remain understandable and controlled.

---

### 263. Proportionality

Risk evaluation should be proportionate to the significance of the AI use case.

A low-impact internal AI assistant may require a relatively simple evaluation.

A high-impact autonomous AI system may require:

* Detailed analysis.
* Multiple specialists.
* Independent review.
* Strong evidence.
* Executive approval.
* Formal documentation.

The level of governance should correspond to the level of potential risk.

---

### 264. Materiality

Materiality asks whether a risk is significant enough to affect:

* Business objectives.
* Customers.
* Employees.
* Stakeholders.
* Regulatory obligations.
* Security posture.
* Privacy.
* Financial performance.

A small technical issue may be immaterial in one context but material in another.

---

### 265. AI Risk Evaluation and Criticality

AI systems can be classified by business criticality.

Example:

| AI System                  | Criticality |
| -------------------------- | ----------- |
| Internal writing assistant | Low         |
| Customer chatbot           | Medium      |
| Fraud detection            | High        |
| Financial decision support | High        |
| Safety-control AI          | Critical    |

Criticality can influence the evaluation criteria and required governance response.

---

### 266. AI System Classification

A mature organization may establish AI categories such as:

```text
Category 1
Low-impact productivity

Category 2
Business-support AI

Category 3
Customer-impacting AI

Category 4
High-impact decision AI

Category 5
Critical / safety-sensitive AI
```

The category can influence how risk is evaluated.

---

### 267. Use-Case Risk vs Technology Risk

An important distinction is:

> **Technology risk** concerns the characteristics of the AI technology.

> **Use-case risk** concerns what the organization does with that technology.

For example, a highly capable model may be relatively low risk when used to draft internal documents but high risk when used to make consequential decisions.

Therefore, evaluation should consider **purpose and context**, not technology alone.

---

### 268. AI Risk Evaluation and Stakeholders

Different stakeholders may have different evaluation perspectives.

**Business owner**

> Is the risk acceptable for the business objective?

**Security**

> Is the security exposure acceptable?

**Privacy**

> Is the privacy risk acceptable?

**Legal / Compliance**

> Is the risk consistent with applicable obligations?

**AI Governance**

> Is the use case consistent with organizational AI policy?

The final evaluation should reflect the organization's established governance model.

---

### 269. Stakeholder Disagreement

Disagreement is not necessarily a failure.

For example:

```text
Business → Moderate
Security → High
Privacy → High
Legal → High
```

This may indicate that the risk is multi-dimensional.

The organization should resolve the disagreement through defined governance rather than simply averaging the opinions.

---

### 270. Escalation

An AI risk may require escalation when:

* It exceeds risk thresholds.
* It exceeds risk appetite.
* It affects critical business processes.
* It has significant human consequences.
* It creates major regulatory exposure.
* There is substantial uncertainty.
* The risk owner lacks authority to accept it.

Escalation should follow the organization's governance structure.

---

### 271. Risk Owner

The **risk owner** is accountable for managing the risk within the organization's governance framework.

The risk owner should understand:

* The risk scenario.
* Risk rating.
* Risk criteria.
* Treatment requirements.
* Residual risk.
* Decision authority.

The analyst supports the evaluation but should not automatically assume ownership of the business risk.

---

### 272. Decision Authority

Different risk levels may require different approval levels.

Example:

```text
Low
→ Business Owner

Moderate
→ Department Management

High
→ Executive Management

Critical
→ Senior Risk Committee / Executive Authority
```

The exact structure depends on the organization's governance model.

---

### 273. Risk Acceptance Threshold

An organization may establish a threshold above which formal risk acceptance is required.

Example:

```text
Residual Risk ≤ Threshold
        ↓
May be acceptable

Residual Risk > Threshold
        ↓
Treatment / Escalation Required
```

This creates consistency in decision-making.

---

### 274. Risk Evaluation Output

The output of risk evaluation may be:

**Accept**

> Risk falls within approved criteria.

**Accept with conditions**

> Risk is tolerable provided specified controls or conditions remain in place.

**Treat**

> Additional controls are required.

**Escalate**

> Risk exceeds the authority or appetite of the current decision-maker.

**Reject / Do not proceed**

> Risk is outside acceptable boundaries.

---

### 275. Conditional Acceptance

Conditional acceptance is particularly useful for AI deployments.

Example:

> The AI system may proceed only if:

* Human review remains mandatory.
* Production write access remains disabled.
* Monitoring is implemented.
* High-risk use cases are restricted.
* Periodic reassessment is performed.

This connects risk evaluation directly to governance conditions.

---

### 276. Risk Evaluation as a Gate

AI governance can use evaluation as a decision gate:

```text
AI Proposal
     ↓
Risk Analysis
     ↓
Risk Evaluation
     ↓
┌────┴─────────────┐
↓                  ↓
Within Criteria    Outside Criteria
↓                  ↓
Proceed            Treat / Escalate
```

This creates a structured mechanism for AI deployment decisions.

---

### 277. Evaluation and Business Value

Risk evaluation should not ignore expected benefits.

For example:

> AI automation may reduce processing time by 70% but introduce moderate operational risk.

Management may determine that the risk is acceptable because the benefits are substantial and the residual risk remains within appetite.

This does not mean benefits "cancel out" risk mathematically.

Instead, risk is evaluated within the organization's decision-making context.

---

### 278. Risk-Benefit Consideration

A mature governance process considers:

```text
Expected Benefit
       +
Risk Characteristics
       +
Risk Appetite
       +
Risk Capacity
       +
Legal / Regulatory Constraints
       ↓
Management Decision
```

Certain risks may remain unacceptable regardless of business benefit because of legal, ethical, safety, or organizational constraints.

---

### 279. AI Risk Evaluation and Compliance

Compliance requirements can establish hard boundaries.

For example, an organization may determine that a particular AI practice:

* Cannot proceed.
* Requires additional safeguards.
* Requires specific documentation.
* Requires human oversight.
* Requires a particular approval.

Risk evaluation therefore must account for applicable mandatory requirements rather than treating everything as a discretionary business decision.

---

### 280. Evaluation Should Be Defensible

A strong evaluation should answer:

> **Why was this risk considered acceptable or unacceptable?**

The record should contain:

* Risk analysis.
* Evaluation criteria.
* Threshold.
* Applicable appetite/tolerance.
* Evidence.
* Assumptions.
* Decision.
* Decision authority.
* Conditions.
* Date.

This produces an auditable decision trail.

---

### 281. Evaluation Record Example

```text
Risk ID: AI-R-001

Risk:
Unauthorized disclosure through AI assistant.

Analysis:
Likelihood = Likely
Consequence = Major
Residual Risk = High

Evaluation Criteria:
High risks require treatment or formal escalation.

Risk Appetite:
Low tolerance for unauthorized disclosure of confidential data.

Evaluation:
Outside normal risk appetite.

Decision:
Additional controls required before production.

Decision Authority:
Designated Risk Owner / Management.
```

---

### 282. Core Principle of AI Risk Evaluation

The central principle is:

> **Risk analysis describes the risk; risk evaluation determines its significance against defined criteria and supports the decision about whether that risk can be accepted within the organization's governance boundaries.**

A useful mental model is:

```text
ANALYSIS
"What is the risk?"
        ↓
EVALUATION
"How significant is it against our criteria?"
        ↓
DECISION
"What should the organization do about it?"
```

This distinction should remain clear throughout the AI risk-management process.

# 06.05 — AI Risk Evaluation

## Part 2 — AI Risk Evaluation Criteria, Thresholds, Decision Models, and Governance

### 283. Risk Evaluation Criteria

AI risk evaluation requires **defined criteria against which analyzed risks can be compared**.

Without criteria, two risk analysts may reach completely different conclusions from the same analysis.

For example:

> Analyst A considers a likelihood of "Likely" and consequence of "Major" acceptable.

> Analyst B considers the same combination unacceptable.

A defined evaluation framework establishes consistency.

```text
AI Risk Analysis
       ↓
Defined Risk Criteria
       ↓
Comparison
       ↓
Risk Evaluation
       ↓
Decision
```

---

### 284. Components of Evaluation Criteria

An organization's AI risk evaluation criteria may define:

* Likelihood thresholds.
* Consequence thresholds.
* Overall risk thresholds.
* Risk appetite.
* Risk tolerance.
* Risk capacity.
* Mandatory escalation points.
* Mandatory treatment conditions.
* Prohibited conditions.
* Approval authority.

These criteria should be documented before they are applied to individual AI use cases.

---

### 285. Likelihood Thresholds

Likelihood categories can be defined consistently.

Example:

| Rating | Description    |
| -----: | -------------- |
|      1 | Rare           |
|      2 | Unlikely       |
|      3 | Possible       |
|      4 | Likely         |
|      5 | Almost Certain |

However, the organization should define what each category actually means.

For example:

> **Possible:** The scenario has credible conditions for occurrence, but available evidence does not indicate that it is expected to occur frequently.

The definitions should be appropriate to the organization's risk methodology.

---

### 286. Consequence Thresholds

Consequence can similarly be classified.

| Rating | Description   |
| -----: | ------------- |
|      1 | Insignificant |
|      2 | Minor         |
|      3 | Moderate      |
|      4 | Major         |
|      5 | Severe        |

Again, the descriptions should be tied to measurable or observable organizational consequences.

---

### 287. AI-Specific Consequence Dimensions

AI evaluation may require several consequence dimensions.

Possible dimensions include:

```text
Security
Privacy
Human
Safety
Financial
Operational
Legal
Regulatory
Reputational
Strategic
```

This is important because an AI event can have low financial impact but significant human or privacy consequences.

---

### 288. Consequence Matrix

An organization could establish criteria such as:

| Dimension  | Minor            | Moderate              | Major                | Severe              |
| ---------- | ---------------- | --------------------- | -------------------- | ------------------- |
| Financial  | Limited loss     | Material loss         | Significant loss     | Critical loss       |
| Privacy    | Limited exposure | Moderate exposure     | Significant exposure | Extensive exposure  |
| Operations | Minor disruption | Noticeable disruption | Major disruption     | Critical disruption |
| Human      | Minimal effect   | Moderate effect       | Significant harm     | Severe harm         |

The exact thresholds should be tailored to the organization.

---

### 289. Risk Matrix

A basic risk matrix combines likelihood and consequence.

```text
                 CONSEQUENCE
              1    2    3    4    5
           ┌────┬────┬────┬────┬────┐
        5  │    │    │    │    │    │
           ├────┼────┼────┼────┼────┤
L       4  │    │    │    │    │    │
I          ├────┼────┼────┼────┼────┤
K       3  │    │    │    │    │    │
E          ├────┼────┼────┼────┼────┤
L       2  │    │    │    │    │    │
I          ├────┼────┼────┼────┼────┤
H       1  │    │    │    │    │    │
O          └────┴────┴────┴────┴────┘
O
D
```

The matrix is only useful when the organization has clearly defined what the resulting categories mean.

---

### 290. Numerical Risk Scoring

A simple methodology may calculate:

> **Risk Score = Likelihood × Consequence**

Example:

```text
Likelihood = 4
Consequence = 5

Risk Score = 4 × 5
           = 20
```

The score can then be mapped to organizational risk categories.

However, the score should support—not replace—the underlying analysis.

---

### 291. Limitations of Multiplication

A numerical score can hide important differences.

Consider:

```text
Likelihood = 5
Impact = 2

Score = 10
```

and:

```text
Likelihood = 2
Impact = 5

Score = 10
```

Both produce the same score, but they represent very different risk situations.

The evaluation should therefore retain the individual dimensions.

---

### 292. Risk Rating vs Risk Score

These terms should be distinguished.

**Risk score**

> A numerical representation derived from defined criteria.

**Risk rating**

> A categorized interpretation such as Low, Moderate, High, or Critical.

Example:

```text
Score = 20
      ↓
Risk Rating = Critical
```

The mapping should be defined by the organization's methodology.

---

### 293. Threshold-Based Evaluation

Risk evaluation can use thresholds.

Example:

```text
1–4    → Acceptable
5–9    → Monitor / Management Attention
10–16  → Treatment Required
17–25  → Escalation / Critical Treatment
```

These numbers are illustrative.

An organization should establish its own approved thresholds.

---

### 294. Hard Thresholds

Some AI risks may have hard thresholds.

Example:

> Any AI system that can independently execute high-value financial transactions requires executive approval.

This is different from a numerical risk score.

The condition itself triggers governance action.

---

### 295. Qualitative Thresholds

Not every evaluation needs numerical scoring.

Example:

> Any AI use case involving highly sensitive personal data requires privacy review before deployment.

This is a qualitative governance criterion.

---

### 296. Quantitative and Qualitative Evaluation

A mature framework can use both.

```text
Quantitative
   ↓
Risk Score / Metrics

Qualitative
   ↓
Context / Expert Judgment / Mandatory Criteria
```

The two approaches can complement each other.

---

### 297. Expert Judgment

AI risk evaluation frequently requires professional judgment.

This may be necessary when:

* Historical data is limited.
* The technology is new.
* Consequences are difficult to quantify.
* AI behavior is uncertain.
* Regulatory expectations are evolving.

Expert judgment should be documented rather than presented as unquestionable fact.

---

### 298. Structured Expert Judgment

A structured approach can ask experts to independently assess:

* Likelihood.
* Consequence.
* Confidence.
* Key assumptions.

The results can then be compared and discussed.

This reduces the influence of the most senior or vocal participant.

---

### 299. Avoiding Groupthink

AI risk evaluation can be influenced by organizational pressure.

For example:

> "The project is already approved by senior management."

This should not automatically influence the risk rating.

The evaluation should remain based on:

* Evidence.
* Criteria.
* Context.
* Governance requirements.

---

### 300. Independence of Evaluation

Where practical, the person evaluating risk should have sufficient independence from the project team.

This is particularly important for:

* High-risk AI.
* Customer-impacting systems.
* Safety-sensitive systems.
* Regulatory-sensitive use cases.

Independence improves objectivity.

---

### 301. Risk Appetite Statement

Organizations may define statements such as:

> "The organization has low appetite for AI risks that could result in unauthorized disclosure of highly sensitive information."

This becomes an important reference during evaluation.

---

### 302. AI Risk Appetite by Domain

Risk appetite may vary.

Example:

| Risk Domain               | Appetite      |
| ------------------------- | ------------- |
| Internal productivity     | Moderate      |
| Security compromise       | Low           |
| Sensitive data exposure   | Very Low      |
| Regulatory non-compliance | Very Low      |
| AI experimentation        | Moderate      |
| Safety-critical AI        | Extremely Low |

This illustrates why one enterprise-wide appetite statement may not be sufficient.

---

### 303. Risk Tolerance Boundaries

Tolerance can be expressed as boundaries.

Example:

> An AI model may produce a small number of low-impact errors, but error rates above a defined threshold require investigation and reassessment.

Conceptually:

```text
Normal Range
───────────────
     │
     ↓
Tolerance Boundary
     │
     ↓
Escalation
```

---

### 304. Risk Capacity and AI

Risk capacity is especially relevant when AI incidents could create systemic effects.

For example:

> A small organization may have limited ability to absorb a major AI-related regulatory penalty or prolonged service outage.

Therefore:

```text
Potential AI Impact
       ↓
Organizational Capacity
       ↓
Evaluation
```

The same event can therefore have different organizational significance.

---

### 305. Risk Evaluation Under Regulatory Constraints

Some AI risks may be subject to legal or regulatory requirements.

In such cases:

```text
Risk Score
     +
Mandatory Requirement
     ↓
Evaluation
```

A low numerical score does not necessarily make a prohibited or non-compliant activity acceptable.

---

### 306. Non-Negotiable Criteria

Some conditions can override normal scoring.

Examples:

* Prohibited activity.
* Mandatory legal requirement.
* Safety requirement.
* Critical security control requirement.
* Contractual restriction.

This prevents an organization from using risk scoring to justify something that is otherwise not permitted.

---

### 307. AI Risk Evaluation and Human Rights

Certain AI use cases may create significant impacts on individuals.

Examples:

* Employment decisions.
* Access to essential services.
* Credit decisions.
* Law enforcement.
* Education.
* Healthcare.

Evaluation should therefore consider whether the risk has implications beyond traditional financial or operational measures.

---

### 308. Stakeholder Impact

Risk evaluation should consider affected stakeholders.

Possible groups include:

* Customers.
* Employees.
* Suppliers.
* Partners.
* Vulnerable populations.
* Regulators.
* Communities.

A risk may be low from the organization's internal perspective but significant for affected individuals.

---

### 309. Distribution of Impact

The average impact can hide concentrated harm.

Example:

> An AI system performs well across 98% of users but creates significantly worse outcomes for a small population.

The evaluation should consider whether that concentration is material.

---

### 310. Scale and Volume

AI creates the possibility of rapid and large-scale propagation.

Compare:

```text
Human Process
10 decisions/hour
```

with:

```text
AI Process
10,000 decisions/hour
```

A small error probability can become significant at large scale.

Therefore, evaluation should consider **volume and automation rate**.

---

### 311. Blast Radius

Blast radius represents the potential scope of an AI failure.

Possible factors include:

* Number of users affected.
* Number of records affected.
* Number of transactions affected.
* Number of systems affected.
* Geographic scope.
* Duration.
* Number of stakeholders.

Example:

```text
Single User
    ↓
Department
    ↓
Business Unit
    ↓
Enterprise
    ↓
External Ecosystem
```

Greater blast radius can materially influence evaluation.

---

### 312. Speed of Propagation

An AI failure can propagate rapidly.

Example:

```text
Incorrect AI Decision
        ↓
Automated Workflow
        ↓
10,000 Transactions
        ↓
Large-Scale Impact
```

Fast propagation reduces the opportunity for human intervention.

---

### 313. Detectability

Evaluation should consider how easily the organization can detect the risk event.

Example:

**High detectability**

> Every AI action is logged and monitored in real time.

**Low detectability**

> Errors may remain unnoticed until a customer reports them.

Lower detectability may increase practical risk.

---

### 314. Reversibility

Ask:

> Can the consequences be reversed?

Examples:

**Highly reversible**

> Incorrect internal draft is corrected before use.

**Partially reversible**

> Incorrect customer record is corrected after processing.

**Poorly reversible**

> Confidential information is publicly disclosed.

Reversibility can materially affect consequence evaluation.

---

### 315. Duration

Risk evaluation should consider how long consequences may persist.

```text
Short Duration
     ↓
Temporary Impact

Long Duration
     ↓
Persistent Impact
```

For example, an incorrect internal recommendation may be quickly corrected, while leaked personal information may remain outside organizational control indefinitely.

---

### 316. Containment Capability

The organization should evaluate its ability to contain an AI incident.

Questions include:

* Can the AI service be disabled?
* Can permissions be revoked?
* Can transactions be stopped?
* Can data access be isolated?
* Can the model be rolled back?
* Can affected records be restored?

Strong containment capability may reduce potential consequences.

---

### 317. Recovery Capability

Recovery should also be considered.

Example:

```text
AI Failure
   ↓
Detection
   ↓
Containment
   ↓
Recovery
   ↓
Return to Normal Operation
```

A system with strong recovery capabilities may have lower operational consequences than an otherwise identical system.

---

### 318. Dependency Concentration

Evaluation should consider concentration risk.

Example:

```text
             One AI Provider
             /      |      \
           AI-01   AI-02   AI-03
```

If multiple critical AI services depend on one provider, a single provider failure may have enterprise-wide consequences.

---

### 319. Cascading Risk

AI risks may propagate into other systems.

Example:

```text
AI Error
   ↓
Incorrect Database Update
   ↓
Incorrect Business Process
   ↓
Incorrect Customer Communication
   ↓
Reputational Impact
```

The evaluation should consider the complete chain rather than only the initial AI error.

---

### 320. Interconnected Risk

Multiple risks may reinforce each other.

Example:

```text
Model Error
     +
Poor Access Control
     +
High Automation
     +
Weak Monitoring
     ↓
Significant AI Risk
```

The combination may create a risk greater than any individual issue considered independently.

---

### 321. Risk Aggregation

Organizations may need to evaluate multiple AI risks together.

Example:

| AI Risk            | Rating   |
| ------------------ | -------- |
| Data leakage       | High     |
| Model drift        | Moderate |
| Provider outage    | Moderate |
| Excessive autonomy | High     |

Individually these may appear manageable, but together they may indicate a broader weakness in the organization's AI environment.

---

### 322. Portfolio-Level Evaluation

At enterprise level, evaluate:

```text
AI-01
AI-02
AI-03
AI-04
  ↓
Enterprise AI Risk Portfolio
```

This can identify:

* Common dependencies.
* Common control gaps.
* Concentrated exposure.
* Repeated risk themes.
* Systemic weaknesses.

---

### 323. Risk Concentration

Suppose 80% of enterprise AI applications use the same external provider.

A provider outage may therefore represent a portfolio-level risk even if every individual application has acceptable operational risk.

This demonstrates why evaluation should sometimes occur at both:

* System level.
* Enterprise level.

---

### 324. Emerging AI Risks

AI risk evaluation should account for emerging risks where historical evidence is limited.

Examples:

* New attack techniques.
* New model capabilities.
* New autonomous behaviors.
* New regulatory expectations.
* Novel misuse patterns.

Limited history should not automatically be interpreted as low risk.

---

### 325. Uncertainty Treatment

When uncertainty is high, the organization may:

* Require additional testing.
* Require stronger controls.
* Limit deployment.
* Reduce autonomy.
* Restrict users.
* Establish additional monitoring.
* Require management approval.

This creates a practical response to uncertainty without pretending it does not exist.

---

### 326. Confidence-Based Escalation

An organization can define a principle such as:

> High-risk assessment + low confidence → additional validation.

For example:

```text
High Risk
   +
Low Confidence
   ↓
Additional Evidence
```

This is particularly useful for new AI technologies.

---

### 327. Risk Evaluation and Pilot Deployment

A pilot can reduce uncertainty.

Example:

```text
Limited Pilot
      ↓
Operational Evidence
      ↓
Revised Analysis
      ↓
Risk Evaluation
      ↓
Production Decision
```

A controlled pilot can therefore serve as an evidence-generation mechanism.

---

### 328. Risk Evaluation and Deployment Conditions

Evaluation may result in specific deployment conditions.

For example:

```text
Proceed only if:
✓ Human approval required
✓ AI permissions restricted
✓ Monitoring enabled
✓ Transaction limits applied
✓ Incident response tested
```

This turns risk evaluation into an actionable governance decision.

---

### 329. Risk-Based Conditions

Conditions should be linked to the risk drivers.

Example:

**Risk driver:**

> AI can perform unrestricted transactions.

**Condition:**

> Introduce transaction limits and approval thresholds.

**Risk driver:**

> AI can access sensitive information.

**Condition:**

> Implement fine-grained authorization.

This provides traceability from:

```text
Risk
 ↓
Evaluation
 ↓
Condition
```

---

### 330. Risk Evaluation Decision Tree

A simple decision tree can be:

```text
Is the risk within approved criteria?
            │
       ┌────┴────┐
      YES        NO
       │          │
       ↓          ↓
   Accept     Can risk be
              reduced?
                  │
             ┌────┴────┐
            YES        NO
             │          │
             ↓          ↓
          Treat      Escalate /
                    Do Not Proceed
```

This provides a simple governance mechanism.

---

### 331. Conditional Approval

Conditional approval may be appropriate where:

* The risk is understood.
* The residual risk is manageable.
* Specific controls are mandatory.
* Conditions can be monitored.

Example:

> Approved for limited production provided that human review remains enabled and no autonomous write access is granted.

---

### 332. Approval with Monitoring

Some risks may be accepted subject to continuous monitoring.

Example:

```text
Accept
  ↓
Monitor KRI
  ↓
Threshold Breach?
  ↓
Re-evaluate
```

This is useful for dynamic AI environments.

---

### 333. Risk Evaluation Documentation

A complete evaluation record should capture:

```text
Risk ID
Risk Scenario
Risk Analysis
Risk Criteria
Risk Rating
Risk Appetite
Risk Tolerance
Risk Capacity
Uncertainty
Confidence
Evaluation Decision
Conditions
Escalation
Decision Authority
Date
```

This provides an auditable record of the governance decision.

---

### 334. Decision Rationale

The rationale should explain **why** the decision was made.

Weak:

> "Risk accepted."

Strong:

> "Residual risk is assessed as Moderate and remains within the approved appetite for internal productivity applications. Existing controls provide sufficient protection, and monitoring is established for material changes."

The second statement is much more defensible.

---

### 335. Documentation of Dissent

If qualified stakeholders disagree, material dissent should be documented.

Example:

```text
Business → Acceptable
Security → High Concern
Privacy → Acceptable with Conditions
```

Recording significant disagreement helps management understand the uncertainty surrounding the decision.

---

### 336. Evaluation Review

A risk evaluation should be reviewed when material circumstances change.

Triggers can include:

* New AI model.
* New functionality.
* Increased autonomy.
* New data source.
* New user population.
* Security incident.
* Material model-performance change.
* New dependency.
* Regulatory change.
* Significant change in business process.

---

### 337. Core Evaluation Workflow

A practical workflow is:

```text
1. Receive Risk Analysis
          ↓
2. Confirm Scope and Context
          ↓
3. Apply Risk Criteria
          ↓
4. Compare Against Appetite / Tolerance
          ↓
5. Consider Mandatory Constraints
          ↓
6. Consider Uncertainty and Confidence
          ↓
7. Consider Scale and Stakeholder Impact
          ↓
8. Determine Risk Significance
          ↓
9. Determine Decision / Escalation
          ↓
10. Document Rationale
```

---

### 338. Final Principle of Part 2

The central principle is:

> **AI risk evaluation requires more than calculating a score. It requires comparing analyzed risk characteristics against defined organizational criteria, risk appetite, tolerance, capacity, mandatory constraints, and contextual factors to determine whether the risk is acceptable and what level of governance response is required.**

A mature evaluator asks:

```text
What is the analyzed risk?
        ↓
What criteria apply?
        ↓
Does it exceed appetite or tolerance?
        ↓
Are there mandatory constraints?
        ↓
How significant is the uncertainty?
        ↓
Who is affected?
        ↓
How large and fast could the impact be?
        ↓
What decision authority applies?
        ↓
Is the risk acceptable?
        ↓
Under what conditions?
```

This establishes the foundation for making **consistent, transparent, and defensible AI risk decisions**.

# 06.05 — AI Risk Evaluation

## Part 3 — Advanced Evaluation Methods, Scenario Comparison, Aggregation, and Decision Quality

### 339. Advanced AI Risk Evaluation

At an advanced level, AI risk evaluation is not simply a matter of placing risks into a red, amber, or green category.

A mature evaluation process considers:

* The analyzed risk.
* Organizational objectives.
* Risk appetite.
* Risk tolerance.
* Legal and regulatory constraints.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Uncertainty.
* Confidence in the evidence.
* Scale.
* Speed of propagation.
* Reversibility.
* Control effectiveness.
* Business criticality.
* Interdependencies.

The objective is to determine the **significance of the risk in its real operating context**.

---

### 340. Scenario-Based Evaluation

Instead of evaluating only a generic risk statement, evaluate realistic scenarios.

Example:

> "AI may produce inaccurate output."

This is too broad.

A stronger scenario is:

> "The AI customer-support assistant generates an incorrect response to a customer regarding a contractual obligation, and the response is automatically delivered without human review."

Now the evaluator can examine:

```text id="6c6a6q"
AI Error
   ↓
Customer Receives Error
   ↓
Incorrect Action
   ↓
Potential Financial / Legal / Reputation Impact
```

Scenario specificity improves evaluation quality.

---

### 341. Best-Case, Expected-Case, and Worst-Case

For complex AI systems, evaluation may consider multiple scenarios.

```text id="1g7n9e"
Best Case
    ↓
Expected Case
    ↓
Worst Credible Case
```

The objective is not to assume the worst possible outcome.

Instead, evaluate **credible scenarios supported by evidence and context**.

---

### 342. Worst Credible Case

The worst credible case is different from the worst imaginable case.

Example:

**Worst imaginable:**

> AI causes complete global financial collapse.

**Worst credible:**

> AI fraud-detection failure causes a significant number of fraudulent transactions to pass through before detection.

Risk evaluation should focus on credible scenarios.

---

### 343. Scenario Comparison

Multiple scenarios can be compared.

| Scenario                     | Likelihood | Consequence | Detectability | Evaluation |
| ---------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | ------------- | ---------- |
| Minor AI error               | Likely     | Minor       | High          | Acceptable |
| Data leakage                 | Possible   | Major       | Medium        | Treatment  |
| Autonomous transaction error | Unlikely   | Severe      | Low           | Escalation |

This prevents a single generalized rating from hiding materially different outcomes.

---

### 344. Scenario Weighting

Where appropriate, organizations may consider scenario probabilities.

For example:

```text id="h2xg4n"
Scenario A:
70% likelihood × Moderate impact

Scenario B:
25% likelihood × Major impact

Scenario C:
5% likelihood × Severe impact
```

This can help management understand the distribution of possible outcomes.

However, probability-based calculations should not replace mandatory risk criteria or hard constraints.

---

### 345. Expected Loss

For financial risks, organizations may use expected-loss concepts.

Conceptually:

> **Expected Loss = Probability × Financial Consequence**

Example:

```text id="7m8n2p"
Probability = 10%
Potential Loss = €500,000

Expected Loss = €50,000
```

This can support financial decision-making.

However, not all AI impacts can or should be reduced to monetary value.

---

### 346. Non-Financial Consequences

Some AI consequences may be difficult or inappropriate to monetize.

Examples:

* Discrimination.
* Loss of privacy.
* Human harm.
* Loss of trust.
* Fundamental-rights impacts.
* Safety consequences.

These should remain explicit evaluation dimensions.

---

### 347. Scenario Sensitivity

Sensitivity analysis asks:

> **"What happens to the evaluation if an important assumption changes?"**

Example:

```text id="n9o6u0"
Assumption:
Human review catches 90% of AI errors.

If review effectiveness falls to 60%
        ↓
Residual risk increases
        ↓
Evaluation may change
```

This identifies assumptions that materially influence the decision.

---

### 348. Sensitivity Analysis

A simple sensitivity table might look like:

| Assumption                 | Base      | Alternative | Evaluation Effect       |
| -------------------------- | --------- | ----------- | ----------------------- |
| Human review effectiveness | 90%       | 60%         | Risk increases          |
| User population            | 5,000     | 50,000      | Blast radius increases  |
| AI autonomy                | Limited   | High        | Consequence increases   |
| Monitoring                 | Real-time | Daily       | Detectability decreases |

This helps decision-makers understand what matters most.

---

### 349. Threshold Sensitivity

Sometimes a small change can cross an evaluation threshold.

Example:

```text id="w1l5zh"
Risk Score = 9
→ Moderate

Risk Score = 10
→ High
```

If a small change causes a large governance consequence, the evaluator should understand why.

This can also identify situations where the scoring model is overly sensitive.

---

### 350. Qualitative Override

An organization may permit a qualitative override where numerical scoring does not adequately represent the risk.

Example:

> The calculated score is Moderate, but the use case involves a critical safety function and therefore requires enhanced governance.

An override should be:

* Authorized.
* Documented.
* Justified.
* Traceable.

It should not be used casually.

---

### 351. Mandatory Escalation Criteria

Some conditions can automatically trigger escalation.

Examples:

```text id="w0i2sa"
Critical AI System
        OR
Severe Human Impact
        OR
Major Regulatory Exposure
        OR
Uncontrolled Sensitive Data Access
        OR
Unacceptable Residual Risk
        ↓
Mandatory Escalation
```

This provides stronger governance than relying only on a calculated score.

---

### 352. Risk Evaluation Exceptions

Sometimes a business unit requests an exception.

For example:

> "The project cannot meet the standard control requirement before the planned launch."

The exception process should capture:

* Reason.
* Risk.
* Compensating controls.
* Duration.
* Risk owner.
* Approval authority.
* Expiration date.
* Review date.

An exception should not silently change the risk criteria.

---

### 353. Compensating Controls

If a preferred control cannot be implemented, another control may reduce the exposure.

Example:

**Preferred control:**

> Automated authorization enforcement.

**Temporary compensating control:**

> Manual approval of every AI-generated transaction.

The evaluator should determine whether the alternative provides sufficient protection.

---

### 354. Evaluation of Control Dependencies

A control may itself depend on another system.

Example:

```text id="h1o2e7"
AI Monitoring
      ↓
SIEM
      ↓
Identity System
      ↓
Cloud Provider
```

If the monitoring system depends on several services, their availability and reliability can influence the effectiveness of the control.

---

### 355. Control Failure Scenario

Evaluation should sometimes consider:

> **"What happens if the primary control fails?"**

Example:

```text id="qf1t8m"
Primary Control
     ↓
Fails
     ↓
Secondary Control
     ↓
Detection
     ↓
Containment
```

This helps determine whether the AI risk is resilient to control failure.

---

### 356. Defense in Depth

AI systems should not depend on one control where consequences are significant.

Example:

```text id="c3xg2f"
Authentication
      ↓
Authorization
      ↓
Data Filtering
      ↓
Output Validation
      ↓
Human Review
      ↓
Monitoring
```

Multiple independent controls can reduce the likelihood or consequences of an AI risk.

---

### 357. Control Independence

Controls that depend on the same underlying component may not provide true independence.

For example:

```text id="1qk3v5"
Control A
Control B
Control C
     ↓
Same Failed Service
```

If all three controls fail when one service fails, the organization may have **apparent defense in depth** rather than genuine defense in depth.

This can influence evaluation.

---

### 358. Correlated Controls

AI systems may have multiple controls that fail for the same reason.

Example:

> Three monitoring mechanisms all rely on the same logging pipeline.

If the logging pipeline fails:

```text id="t5w4ub"
Logging Failure
      ↓
Control A ✗
Control B ✗
Control C ✗
```

The evaluator should account for this dependency.

---

### 359. AI Risk Interdependencies

Risks themselves can be interconnected.

Example:

```text id="7c4gqf"
Poor Data Quality
       ↓
Model Error
       ↓
Incorrect Decision
       ↓
Customer Impact
```

Another chain might be:

```text id="d1q1gk"
Excessive Permissions
       ↓
Prompt Injection
       ↓
Unauthorized Tool Use
       ↓
Data Disclosure
```

Evaluating each risk independently may underestimate the overall exposure.

---

### 360. Risk Bow-Tie Perspective

A useful conceptual model is:

```text id="7wujn4"
CAUSES
  ↓
RISK EVENT
  ↓
CONSEQUENCES
```

Controls can be placed on both sides:

```text id="x1o7lj"
Preventive Controls
       ↓
CAUSES
       ↓
RISK EVENT
       ↓
CONSEQUENCES
       ↓
Detective / Corrective Controls
```

This helps the evaluator understand where controls influence the risk.

---

### 361. Preventive Controls

Preventive controls attempt to stop the risk event.

Examples:

* Access control.
* Input validation.
* Data restrictions.
* Tool permissions.
* Network segmentation.
* Model restrictions.
* Human approval.

---

### 362. Detective Controls

Detective controls identify a risk event after or while it occurs.

Examples:

* AI activity monitoring.
* Security alerts.
* Anomaly detection.
* Audit logs.
* Output monitoring.
* User reports.

---

### 363. Corrective Controls

Corrective controls reduce consequences after an event.

Examples:

* Account suspension.
* AI service shutdown.
* Transaction reversal.
* Data restoration.
* Incident response.
* Model rollback.

A complete evaluation considers the effectiveness of all three categories.

---

### 364. AI Risk Evaluation and Control Coverage

The evaluator should ask:

> "Does the control address the actual risk driver?"

Example:

**Risk:**

> AI accesses confidential data without authorization.

**Control:**

> Employee security-awareness training.

Training may be useful, but it does not directly enforce authorization.

A stronger control may be:

> Fine-grained access control enforced at the data and application layers.

---

### 365. Control Effectiveness Assessment

Control effectiveness may be classified as:

```text id="r0g0q4"
Effective
Partially Effective
Ineffective
Unknown
```

"Unknown" is important.

An undocumented or untested control should not automatically be treated as effective.

---

### 366. Evidence Quality

Evidence supporting an evaluation can vary.

**High-quality evidence**

* Production testing.
* Independent assessment.
* Security testing.
* Verified logs.
* Historical incident data.

**Lower-confidence evidence**

* Vendor statements.
* Unvalidated assumptions.
* Limited testing.
* Informal stakeholder opinions.

The evaluator should consider evidence quality.

---

### 367. Evidence Triangulation

For significant risks, multiple evidence sources can be compared.

Example:

```text id="w1r8s7"
Architecture Review
       +
Penetration Testing
       +
Production Logs
       +
Incident Records
       ↓
Higher Confidence
```

Triangulation reduces dependence on a single source.

---

### 368. Risk Evaluation Confidence

Confidence can be explicitly recorded.

Example:

| Risk            | Rating   | Confidence |
| --------------- | -------- | ---------- |
| Data leakage    | High     | High       |
| Model drift     | Moderate | Medium     |
| Emerging attack | Moderate | Low        |

This allows management to distinguish between:

> **High risk with strong evidence**

and:

> **High risk based on uncertain assumptions.**

---

### 369. Decision Robustness

A good evaluation should remain reasonably stable when minor assumptions change.

If a small assumption change moves the risk from:

> Acceptable → Critical

the decision may require further analysis.

This is an important indicator of decision robustness.

---

### 370. Robust vs Fragile Evaluation

**Robust evaluation**

> Small changes in assumptions do not materially change the decision.

**Fragile evaluation**

> Small changes in assumptions significantly change the decision.

Fragile evaluations often require additional evidence or more conservative controls.

---

### 371. Risk Evaluation and Time

AI risk is dynamic.

A risk evaluated today may change because:

* The model changes.
* The user population changes.
* The threat environment changes.
* The system becomes more autonomous.
* New data is introduced.
* Controls deteriorate.
* Business processes change.

Therefore, evaluation should be treated as a **point-in-time governance judgment**, not a permanent declaration.

---

### 372. Temporal Evaluation

An organization can compare:

```text id="u3g1gm"
Initial Evaluation
       ↓
6-Month Evaluation
       ↓
Annual Evaluation
       ↓
Event-Triggered Evaluation
```

Changes should be documented and explained.

---

### 373. Evaluation After an Incident

An AI incident provides new evidence.

Example:

```text id="v8r3kg"
Original Evaluation
      ↓
Incident
      ↓
New Evidence
      ↓
Reanalysis
      ↓
Re-evaluation
```

The organization should not simply retain the original evaluation if the incident demonstrates that its assumptions were incorrect.

---

### 374. Evaluation After a Model Change

A model replacement may change:

* Accuracy.
* Behavior.
* Security characteristics.
* Data requirements.
* Output format.
* Tool interaction.
* Autonomy.

Therefore, a model change may require reassessment of the evaluation.

---

### 375. Evaluation After Increased Autonomy

Increasing AI autonomy can significantly change risk.

Example:

```text id="r0e9pk"
AI Suggests
    ↓
Human Approves
```

compared with:

```text id="c5p1ec"
AI Decides
    ↓
AI Executes
```

The second configuration may have greater:

* Consequence.
* Speed.
* Blast radius.
* Control dependency.

Therefore, the evaluation may need to change materially.

---

### 376. Evaluation of Human-in-the-Loop

Human oversight should not automatically be treated as sufficient.

The evaluator should examine:

* Timing.
* Authority.
* Competence.
* Workload.
* Alert quality.
* Override capability.
* Independence.
* Automation bias.

A nominal human approval step may provide little protection if the reviewer simply accepts AI recommendations without meaningful review.

---

### 377. Automation Bias

Automation bias occurs when people overly trust automated recommendations.

Example:

```text id="a1c4mq"
AI Recommendation
      ↓
Human Reviewer
      ↓
Automatically Accepted
```

If the reviewer rarely challenges the system, the practical effectiveness of human oversight may be lower than documented.

---

### 378. Human Override Capability

A meaningful human control should normally allow the authorized person to:

* Reject the AI recommendation.
* Modify the result.
* Stop the process.
* Escalate the issue.
* Request additional evidence.

If the human cannot meaningfully intervene, the control may be largely symbolic.

---

### 379. Evaluation of AI Autonomy

AI autonomy can be considered along a spectrum:

```text id="yp6qjq"
Information
   ↓
Recommendation
   ↓
Decision Support
   ↓
Automated Decision
   ↓
Automated Action
   ↓
Autonomous Multi-Step Action
```

Greater autonomy can increase the importance of:

* Authorization.
* Monitoring.
* Containment.
* Human oversight.
* Reversibility.

---

### 380. Evaluation of External Dependencies

Third-party AI services can introduce:

* Availability risk.
* Security risk.
* Privacy risk.
* Supply-chain risk.
* Model-change risk.
* Contractual risk.
* Concentration risk.

Evaluation should consider what happens if the external dependency:

* Changes behavior.
* Becomes unavailable.
* Suffers a security incident.
* Changes pricing.
* Changes terms.
* Retires a model.

---

### 381. Vendor Change Risk

An AI provider may silently or routinely change:

* Model version.
* Safety behavior.
* Performance.
* APIs.
* Data processing.
* Infrastructure.

Therefore, risk evaluation should consider the organization's ability to detect and respond to material provider changes.

---

### 382. AI Risk Evaluation and Change Control

A mature process can integrate AI risk evaluation with change management.

```text id="4k8g1x"
Proposed AI Change
       ↓
Change Assessment
       ↓
Risk Impact
       ↓
Re-evaluation Required?
       ↓
Yes → AI Risk Evaluation
```

This helps prevent material AI changes from bypassing governance.

---

### 383. Portfolio Evaluation

At enterprise level, management may want to understand:

> "What is the overall AI risk exposure?"

This requires aggregating information across AI systems.

Example:

```text id="b2k3zt"
AI Customer Service
       ↓
AI Fraud Detection
       ↓
AI HR Assistant
       ↓
AI Developer Assistant
       ↓
AI Analytics
       ↓
Enterprise AI Risk Profile
```

---

### 384. Portfolio Risk Themes

Enterprise evaluation may identify recurring themes:

* Excessive AI permissions.
* Poor data governance.
* Third-party dependency.
* Insufficient monitoring.
* Weak human oversight.
* Inconsistent risk assessment.
* Inadequate model change management.

These themes may be more important to management than individual risk scores.

---

### 385. Concentration Risk Evaluation

Suppose:

> 90% of critical AI applications depend on one model provider.

Even if every individual application is rated Moderate, the organization may have a significant concentration risk.

This demonstrates the importance of evaluating **aggregate exposure**.

---

### 386. Common-Cause Failure

Multiple AI systems can fail for the same reason.

Example:

```text id="djylf4"
Common Cloud Dependency
       ↓
AI-01 ✗
AI-02 ✗
AI-03 ✗
AI-04 ✗
```

This can create systemic enterprise impact.

---

### 387. Residual Risk Evaluation

The evaluator should distinguish:

```text id="7ep3mw"
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Residual Risk
      ↓
Risk Criteria
      ↓
Evaluation
```

The key question is:

> **"Is the residual risk acceptable?"**

Not merely:

> "Do controls exist?"

---

### 388. Residual Risk Above Appetite

If residual risk remains above appetite:

```text id="z2fjv5"
Residual Risk
     ↓
Above Appetite
     ↓
Not Normally Acceptable
     ↓
Treatment / Escalation /
Alternative Decision
```

The exact response depends on organizational policy.

---

### 389. Risk Evaluation and Treatment Priority

Evaluation can help prioritize treatment.

Example:

| Risk                | Residual Rating | Priority  |
| ------------------- | --------------- | --------- |
| AI data leakage     | Critical        | Immediate |
| Model drift         | High            | High      |
| Minor output errors | Moderate        | Normal    |
| Formatting errors   | Low             | Low       |

This helps management allocate resources.

---

### 390. Resource Allocation

Risk evaluation can support decisions about:

* Security budget.
* Engineering resources.
* Testing.
* Monitoring.
* Governance staff.
* External assessments.
* Control implementation.

Higher evaluated risk generally justifies greater attention, subject to organizational priorities.

---

### 391. Evaluation Quality Indicators

Organizations can monitor whether their evaluation process is working effectively.

Possible indicators:

* Percentage of AI systems formally evaluated.
* Percentage with documented criteria.
* Percentage with independent review.
* Number of overdue reassessments.
* Number of risks above appetite.
* Number of unresolved high risks.
* Number of evaluation exceptions.
* Number of incidents involving previously assessed risks.

---

### 392. AI Risk Evaluation Dashboard

A management dashboard could show:

| Metric                     | Example |
| -------------------------- | ------: |
| AI systems assessed        |      42 |
| High/Critical risks        |      11 |
| Risks above appetite       |       6 |
| Open treatment plans       |       8 |
| Overdue reassessments      |       3 |
| Low-confidence assessments |       5 |

This gives leadership a portfolio-level view.

---

### 393. Evaluation Governance Maturity

A basic organization may have:

> Informal expert judgment.

A developing organization:

> Standardized risk matrix.

A mature organization:

> Multi-dimensional criteria, evidence requirements, uncertainty assessment, independent review, portfolio analysis, and event-driven reassessment.

A highly mature organization:

> Integrated AI risk evaluation with enterprise risk, change management, monitoring, incident management, and strategic decision-making.

---

### 394. Practical GRC Example

Consider an AI system used to screen job applicants.

The analysis determines:

```text id="jqh9cv"
Likelihood of Incorrect Classification = Possible
Potential Consequence = Major
Scale = 50,000 applicants/year
Human Review = Available
Monitoring = Monthly
Evidence Confidence = Medium
```

Evaluation should ask:

* Is this within organizational appetite?
* Are there applicable legal constraints?
* Is human review meaningful?
* Is monthly monitoring sufficient?
* Could a particular population be disproportionately affected?
* Is the scale material?
* Is confidence sufficient?

The answer should not be based solely on:

> "Risk Score = 12."

---

### 395. Practical Evaluation Conclusion

A documented conclusion might state:

> "The assessed residual risk is High and exceeds the organization's normal appetite for AI systems influencing employment decisions. Although human review is present, the current monitoring frequency and evidence base provide insufficient assurance that adverse outcomes would be detected promptly. Deployment should therefore remain conditional upon strengthened monitoring, documented review procedures, and additional validation."

This is a much stronger governance conclusion than simply writing:

> "Risk = High."

---

### 396. Senior GRC Perspective

At senior level, AI risk evaluation should answer four questions:

```text id="1qf5y5"
1. How significant is the risk?

2. Is it within organizational boundaries?

3. How confident are we in that conclusion?

4. What governance decision follows?
```

These four questions create a useful mental model for senior GRC practice.

---

### 397. Evaluation Decision Quality

A high-quality evaluation should be:

**Consistent**

> Same criteria produce comparable results.

**Evidence-based**

> Conclusions are supported by evidence.

**Contextual**

> The AI use case and operating environment are considered.

**Transparent**

> The rationale is documented.

**Proportionate**

> Governance effort matches risk.

**Defensible**

> Another qualified reviewer can understand how the conclusion was reached.

---

Remember:

```text id="qf6s0s"

   ↓

   ↓
Context
   ↓
Likelihood + Consequence
   ↓
Controls
   ↓
Residual Risk
   ↓
Evidence + Uncertainty
   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

```

---

### 399. Final Principle

> **Advanced AI risk evaluation is the disciplined process of determining the significance and acceptability of an analyzed AI risk by considering organizational criteria, risk appetite, tolerance, mandatory constraints, evidence, uncertainty, control effectiveness, scale, autonomy, dependencies, and stakeholder consequences.**

The most important distinction remains:

```text id="s2f6we"

"What is the risk and how significant could it be?"

"Does that level of risk fall within our
```

That distinction is fundamental to effective AI risk governance.

### 413. Unauthorized Risk Acceptance

A common governance weakness occurs when someone accepts a risk without having authority to do so.

For example:

Risk evaluation should verify **decision authority**, not merely the existence of a signature.

Strong:

Specific conditions are easier to verify.

### 450. Common AI Risk Evaluation Failures

Organizations should avoid:

**1. Treating the risk score as the decision**

> A score is an input to evaluation, not the entire decision.

**2. Ignoring context**

> The same AI technology can have very different risks in different use cases.

**3. Assuming controls are effective**

> Controls require evidence.

**4. Ignoring uncertainty**

> Lack of evidence does not mean low risk.

**5. Allowing business pressure to distort evaluation**

> Strategic importance does not automatically make risk acceptable.

**6. Ignoring mandatory requirements**

> Legal or regulatory constraints may override ordinary risk appetite.

**7. Accepting risk without authority**

> Risk acceptance must follow the approved governance structure.

* Why the risk is High.
* Why it is acceptable.
* Which criteria were used.
* What evidence exists.
* What conditions apply.
* Who had authority.
* When it must be reviewed.

A stronger record makes the decision reconstructable.

### 452. Professional GRC Interview Explanation

If asked:

> **"How do you evaluate AI risk?"**

A strong answer is:

> "I start by validating the risk analysis and understanding the AI use case, affected stakeholders, and operating context. I then compare the likelihood and consequence against the organization's defined risk criteria, including risk appetite and tolerance. I also consider control effectiveness, residual risk, uncertainty, scale, autonomy, dependencies, and any mandatory legal or regulatory constraints. Based on that evaluation, I determine whether the risk is acceptable, requires conditions or additional treatment, or needs escalation. Finally, I document the rationale, decision authority, conditions, and reassessment triggers so the decision is auditable and defensible."

---

### 453. Professional GRC Example

If asked:

> **"What if the business wants to deploy an AI system but the risk exceeds appetite?"**

A strong response is:

> "I would not simply block the project or override the risk criteria. I would document the risk, validate the analysis, identify treatment options, and determine whether additional controls can reduce the residual risk to an acceptable level. If the residual risk remains above appetite, I would escalate it to the appropriate risk owner or decision authority for formal acceptance or a go/no-go decision. The key is to preserve the integrity of the risk framework while enabling informed business decisions."

---

### 454. Professional GRC Example — Disagreement

If asked:

> **"What if the business says the risk is acceptable but security disagrees?"**

Answer:

> "I would separate the disagreement from the underlying evidence. I would review the risk scenario, criteria, assumptions, and control effectiveness with both parties. If the disagreement remains, I would document the different perspectives and escalate the decision according to the governance structure. I would not simply average the ratings or allow business urgency to override the approved criteria."

---

### 455. Professional GRC Example — Limited Evidence

If asked:

> **"What do you do when there isn't enough evidence to evaluate an AI risk?"**

Answer:

> "I would explicitly document the uncertainty rather than assuming the risk is low. Depending on the potential consequence, I may recommend additional testing, a limited pilot, stronger controls, restricted deployment, or escalation. The level of confidence in the evaluation should be visible to the decision-maker."

---

### 456. Professional GRC Example — Risk Score

If asked:

> **"Do you rely on a risk matrix?"**

Answer:

> "A risk matrix is useful for consistency and prioritization, but I don't treat it as the entire decision. AI risks can have multidimensional impacts, including privacy, security, human, regulatory, and operational consequences. I use the score together with context, evidence, control effectiveness, uncertainty, risk appetite, and mandatory requirements."

### 458. Final Professional Principle

The purpose of AI risk evaluation is not simply to **assign a color or number to a risk**.

Its purpose is to enable the organization to make a **controlled, evidence-based, proportionate, and defensible decision** about AI risk.

The complete relationship is:

```text
IDENTIFY
"What can go wrong?"

ANALYZE
"How likely and consequential is it?"

EVALUATE
"How does that risk compare with our
defined boundaries?"

DECIDE
"What should the organization do?"

DOCUMENT
"Why was that decision made?"

MONITOR
"Does the decision remain valid?"
```

A mature AI GRC professional therefore acts as the bridge between **risk analysis and organizational decision-making**—ensuring that AI risks are neither automatically accepted nor unnecessarily rejected, but evaluated consistently against evidence, organizational boundaries, and governance requirements.
