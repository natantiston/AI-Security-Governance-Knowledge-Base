# 06.05 — AI Risk Evaluation

## Part 1 — Foundations, Purpose, Principles, and Decision Context

### 242. Introduction to AI Risk Evaluation

AI risk evaluation is the process of **comparing analyzed AI risks against established organizational risk criteria to determine their significance and support a risk decision**.

Risk analysis asks:

> **"What is the nature and magnitude of the AI risk?"**

Risk evaluation asks:

> **"Is this level of AI risk acceptable within the organization's defined criteria?"**

This distinction is fundamental.

```text
AI Risk Identification
        ↓
AI Risk Analysis
        ↓
AI Risk Evaluation
        ↓
Risk Decision
```

Risk evaluation therefore converts analytical information into a **governance judgment**.

---

### 243. Analysis vs Evaluation

The two activities should not be confused.

**Risk analysis** determines characteristics such as:

* Likelihood.
* Consequence.
* Exposure.
* Control effectiveness.
* Uncertainty.
* Detectability.
* Reversibility.

**Risk evaluation** determines the significance of those characteristics relative to organizational criteria.

For example:

> Analysis: Likelihood = Likely; Consequence = Major.

> Evaluation: The resulting risk exceeds the organization's acceptable risk threshold and therefore requires management attention before deployment.

---

### 244. Why AI Risk Evaluation Matters

AI systems can create risks that organizations may not be accustomed to evaluating.

Examples include:

* AI-generated misinformation.
* Model bias.
* Autonomous actions.
* Privacy impacts.
* Security vulnerabilities.
* Discriminatory outcomes.
* Loss of human oversight.
* AI supply-chain dependencies.
* Large-scale automated errors.

Risk evaluation determines whether these risks are:

* Acceptable.
* Acceptable with conditions.
* Require additional controls.
* Require escalation.
* Require a different treatment decision.
* Potentially unacceptable.

---

### 245. Evaluation Is Context-Dependent

A risk does not have an absolute meaning outside its context.

Consider:

> An AI system has a 2% error rate.

Whether that is acceptable depends on its use.

For:

> Internal brainstorming

2% error may be relatively tolerable.

For:

> Safety-critical automated control

2% error could be unacceptable.

Therefore:

```text
Risk Characteristics
        +
Business Context
        +
Risk Criteria
        ↓
Risk Evaluation
```

---

### 246. Organizational Risk Criteria

Risk evaluation requires predefined criteria.

These criteria establish what the organization considers:

* Low risk.
* Moderate risk.
* High risk.
* Critical risk.
* Acceptable risk.
* Unacceptable risk.

For example:

| Risk Score | Classification | Evaluation                  |
| ---------: | -------------- | --------------------------- |
|        1–4 | Low            | Generally acceptable        |
|        5–9 | Moderate       | Management attention        |
|      10–16 | High           | Treatment normally required |
|      17–25 | Critical       | Executive escalation        |

The exact thresholds should be established by the organization's risk methodology.

---

### 247. Risk Appetite

**Risk appetite** describes the amount and type of risk an organization is generally willing to pursue or retain in pursuit of its objectives.

For AI governance, risk appetite may differ across areas.

Example:

```text
AI Innovation
     ↓
Higher Risk Appetite

Customer Privacy
     ↓
Low Risk Appetite

Safety-Critical AI
     ↓
Very Low Risk Appetite
```

An organization may be willing to tolerate greater uncertainty in experimentation than in production systems affecting customers.

---

### 248. Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance defines the acceptable degree of variation around the organization's risk appetite or objectives.

For example:

> The organization may tolerate minor AI output errors in an internal productivity tool but have zero tolerance for unauthorized disclosure of certain highly sensitive information.

Risk tolerance therefore helps establish more specific evaluation boundaries.

---

### 249. Risk Capacity

Risk capacity is different from appetite.

It represents the **maximum amount of risk an organization can absorb without threatening its ability to continue operating or achieve its objectives**.

For example:

```text
Risk Appetite
      ↓
What we are willing to accept

Risk Capacity
      ↓
What we can actually withstand
```

An organization may have a relatively high appetite for AI innovation but limited capacity to absorb a major regulatory or operational failure.

---

### 250. Risk Appetite vs Risk Tolerance vs Risk Capacity

| Concept        | Core Question                                     |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk Appetite  | How much risk are we willing to pursue or accept? |
| Risk Tolerance | How much variation is acceptable?                 |
| Risk Capacity  | How much risk can we withstand?                   |

These concepts help provide context for AI risk evaluation.

---

### 251. AI Risk Criteria

AI-specific evaluation criteria may consider more than conventional enterprise risk scores.

Possible criteria include:

* Safety.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Fairness.
* Human oversight.
* Transparency.
* Accountability.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Operational resilience.
* Financial impact.
* Reputational impact.

The organization should determine which criteria are relevant to its AI environment.

---

### 252. Multi-Dimensional AI Evaluation

An AI system could have:

| Dimension    | Assessment |
| ------------ | ---------- |
| Security     | High       |
| Privacy      | High       |
| Financial    | Moderate   |
| Operational  | Moderate   |
| Human impact | High       |
| Reputation   | Moderate   |

A single aggregate score may hide important information.

Therefore, evaluation should preserve material dimensions even when the organization ultimately produces one overall risk rating.

---

### 253. Evaluation Against Objectives

Risk evaluation should consider the organization's objectives.

For example:

> An AI deployment may improve customer-service efficiency by 30% but introduce a significant privacy risk.

The evaluation asks whether the benefit and risk are consistent with organizational objectives and risk criteria.

This creates a balanced governance perspective:

```text
Business Benefit
       ↕
AI Risk
       ↓
Organizational Decision
```

---

### 254. Risk Evaluation Is Not Risk Elimination

The objective is not necessarily to eliminate every risk.

AI systems inherently involve uncertainty and imperfect outcomes.

The objective is to determine:

> **Which risks are acceptable, which require treatment, and which cannot be accepted under the organization's criteria.**

This distinction is essential for practical AI governance.

---

### 255. Inherent Risk and Residual Risk

Evaluation may consider both:

**Inherent risk**

> Risk before considering existing controls.

**Residual risk**

> Risk remaining after considering controls.

Example:

```text
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Existing Controls
     ↓
Residual Risk
     ↓
Evaluation
```

A system may have:

> High inherent risk

but:

> Moderate residual risk.

Whether moderate residual risk is acceptable depends on organizational criteria.

---

### 256. Control Effectiveness and Evaluation

The presence of a control does not automatically make a risk acceptable.

For example:

> Human review exists.

The evaluator should consider:

* Is it consistently performed?
* Is the reviewer qualified?
* Can the reviewer reject the AI output?
* Does the reviewer have sufficient information?
* How quickly can intervention occur?
* Is the control independently tested?

Only then can its effect on residual risk be appropriately considered.

---

### 257. Uncertainty in Evaluation

AI risk evaluation often involves uncertainty.

Sources include:

* Limited historical data.
* New models.
* Emerging threats.
* Changing user behavior.
* Model evolution.
* Limited testing.
* Incomplete vendor information.

The organization should avoid treating uncertain estimates as precise facts.

---

### 258. Confidence in Evaluation

A useful concept is **confidence**.

Example:

```text
Risk Rating: High
Confidence: High
```

means the organization has strong evidence supporting the assessment.

Another example:

```text
Risk Rating: Moderate
Confidence: Low
```

means the rating is less certain and additional evidence may be necessary.

Confidence does not automatically change the risk score, but it can influence the decision about whether additional analysis or controls are needed.

---

### 259. Evaluation Under Uncertainty

Suppose two AI systems both receive a "Moderate" risk rating.

**System A**

> Strong evidence, extensive testing, stable operating history.

**System B**

> Limited testing, new model, uncertain behavior.

The nominal risk rating is the same, but the governance response may differ because the uncertainty is different.

---

### 260. Risk Evaluation and Precaution

Where potential consequences are severe and evidence is limited, organizations may adopt a more cautious approach.

For example:

```text
High Potential Consequence
+
Low Evidence
+
High Uncertainty
        ↓
Greater Need for Caution
```

The exact decision should follow organizational governance and applicable requirements.

---

### 261. Risk Evaluation Criteria Should Be Established Beforehand

Evaluation becomes less reliable if criteria are changed simply to accommodate a desired outcome.

For example, an organization should not decide:

> "This AI project is strategically important, so let's redefine high risk as acceptable."

Instead, criteria should normally be established independently of individual project outcomes.

---

### 262. Consistency Across AI Systems

A mature AI governance program applies consistent evaluation principles.

For example:

```text
AI Use Case A → Evaluation Criteria
AI Use Case B → Same Core Framework
AI Use Case C → Same Core Framework
```

The specific thresholds may vary by risk domain, but the underlying methodology should remain understandable and controlled.

---

### 263. Proportionality

Risk evaluation should be proportionate to the significance of the AI use case.

A low-impact internal AI assistant may require a relatively simple evaluation.

A high-impact autonomous AI system may require:

* Detailed analysis.
* Multiple specialists.
* Independent review.
* Strong evidence.
* Executive approval.
* Formal documentation.

The level of governance should correspond to the level of potential risk.

---

### 264. Materiality

Materiality asks whether a risk is significant enough to affect:

* Business objectives.
* Customers.
* Employees.
* Stakeholders.
* Regulatory obligations.
* Security posture.
* Privacy.
* Financial performance.

A small technical issue may be immaterial in one context but material in another.

---

### 265. AI Risk Evaluation and Criticality

AI systems can be classified by business criticality.

Example:

| AI System                  | Criticality |
| -------------------------- | ----------- |
| Internal writing assistant | Low         |
| Customer chatbot           | Medium      |
| Fraud detection            | High        |
| Financial decision support | High        |
| Safety-control AI          | Critical    |

Criticality can influence the evaluation criteria and required governance response.

---

### 266. AI System Classification

A mature organization may establish AI categories such as:

```text
Category 1
Low-impact productivity

Category 2
Business-support AI

Category 3
Customer-impacting AI

Category 4
High-impact decision AI

Category 5
Critical / safety-sensitive AI
```

The category can influence how risk is evaluated.

---

### 267. Use-Case Risk vs Technology Risk

An important distinction is:

> **Technology risk** concerns the characteristics of the AI technology.

> **Use-case risk** concerns what the organization does with that technology.

For example, a highly capable model may be relatively low risk when used to draft internal documents but high risk when used to make consequential decisions.

Therefore, evaluation should consider **purpose and context**, not technology alone.

---

### 268. AI Risk Evaluation and Stakeholders

Different stakeholders may have different evaluation perspectives.

**Business owner**

> Is the risk acceptable for the business objective?

**Security**

> Is the security exposure acceptable?

**Privacy**

> Is the privacy risk acceptable?

**Legal / Compliance**

> Is the risk consistent with applicable obligations?

**AI Governance**

> Is the use case consistent with organizational AI policy?

The final evaluation should reflect the organization's established governance model.

---

### 269. Stakeholder Disagreement

Disagreement is not necessarily a failure.

For example:

```text
Business → Moderate
Security → High
Privacy → High
Legal → High
```

This may indicate that the risk is multi-dimensional.

The organization should resolve the disagreement through defined governance rather than simply averaging the opinions.

---

### 270. Escalation

An AI risk may require escalation when:

* It exceeds risk thresholds.
* It exceeds risk appetite.
* It affects critical business processes.
* It has significant human consequences.
* It creates major regulatory exposure.
* There is substantial uncertainty.
* The risk owner lacks authority to accept it.

Escalation should follow the organization's governance structure.

---

### 271. Risk Owner

The **risk owner** is accountable for managing the risk within the organization's governance framework.

The risk owner should understand:

* The risk scenario.
* Risk rating.
* Risk criteria.
* Treatment requirements.
* Residual risk.
* Decision authority.

The analyst supports the evaluation but should not automatically assume ownership of the business risk.

---

### 272. Decision Authority

Different risk levels may require different approval levels.

Example:

```text
Low
→ Business Owner

Moderate
→ Department Management

High
→ Executive Management

Critical
→ Senior Risk Committee / Executive Authority
```

The exact structure depends on the organization's governance model.

---

### 273. Risk Acceptance Threshold

An organization may establish a threshold above which formal risk acceptance is required.

Example:

```text
Residual Risk ≤ Threshold
        ↓
May be acceptable

Residual Risk > Threshold
        ↓
Treatment / Escalation Required
```

This creates consistency in decision-making.

---

### 274. Risk Evaluation Output

The output of risk evaluation may be:

**Accept**

> Risk falls within approved criteria.

**Accept with conditions**

> Risk is tolerable provided specified controls or conditions remain in place.

**Treat**

> Additional controls are required.

**Escalate**

> Risk exceeds the authority or appetite of the current decision-maker.

**Reject / Do not proceed**

> Risk is outside acceptable boundaries.

---

### 275. Conditional Acceptance

Conditional acceptance is particularly useful for AI deployments.

Example:

> The AI system may proceed only if:

* Human review remains mandatory.
* Production write access remains disabled.
* Monitoring is implemented.
* High-risk use cases are restricted.
* Periodic reassessment is performed.

This connects risk evaluation directly to governance conditions.

---

### 276. Risk Evaluation as a Gate

AI governance can use evaluation as a decision gate:

```text
AI Proposal
     ↓
Risk Analysis
     ↓
Risk Evaluation
     ↓
┌────┴─────────────┐
↓                  ↓
Within Criteria    Outside Criteria
↓                  ↓
Proceed            Treat / Escalate
```

This creates a structured mechanism for AI deployment decisions.

---

### 277. Evaluation and Business Value

Risk evaluation should not ignore expected benefits.

For example:

> AI automation may reduce processing time by 70% but introduce moderate operational risk.

Management may determine that the risk is acceptable because the benefits are substantial and the residual risk remains within appetite.

This does not mean benefits "cancel out" risk mathematically.

Instead, risk is evaluated within the organization's decision-making context.

---

### 278. Risk-Benefit Consideration

A mature governance process considers:

```text
Expected Benefit
       +
Risk Characteristics
       +
Risk Appetite
       +
Risk Capacity
       +
Legal / Regulatory Constraints
       ↓
Management Decision
```

Certain risks may remain unacceptable regardless of business benefit because of legal, ethical, safety, or organizational constraints.

---

### 279. AI Risk Evaluation and Compliance

Compliance requirements can establish hard boundaries.

For example, an organization may determine that a particular AI practice:

* Cannot proceed.
* Requires additional safeguards.
* Requires specific documentation.
* Requires human oversight.
* Requires a particular approval.

Risk evaluation therefore must account for applicable mandatory requirements rather than treating everything as a discretionary business decision.

---

### 280. Evaluation Should Be Defensible

A strong evaluation should answer:

> **Why was this risk considered acceptable or unacceptable?**

The record should contain:

* Risk analysis.
* Evaluation criteria.
* Threshold.
* Applicable appetite/tolerance.
* Evidence.
* Assumptions.
* Decision.
* Decision authority.
* Conditions.
* Date.

This produces an auditable decision trail.

---

### 281. Evaluation Record Example

```text
Risk ID: AI-R-001

Risk:
Unauthorized disclosure through AI assistant.

Analysis:
Likelihood = Likely
Consequence = Major
Residual Risk = High

Evaluation Criteria:
High risks require treatment or formal escalation.

Risk Appetite:
Low tolerance for unauthorized disclosure of confidential data.

Evaluation:
Outside normal risk appetite.

Decision:
Additional controls required before production.

Decision Authority:
Designated Risk Owner / Management.
```

---

### 282. Core Principle of AI Risk Evaluation

The central principle is:

> **Risk analysis describes the risk; risk evaluation determines its significance against defined criteria and supports the decision about whether that risk can be accepted within the organization's governance boundaries.**

A useful mental model is:

```text
ANALYSIS
"What is the risk?"
        ↓
EVALUATION
"How significant is it against our criteria?"
        ↓
DECISION
"What should the organization do about it?"
```

This distinction should remain clear throughout the AI risk-management process.
