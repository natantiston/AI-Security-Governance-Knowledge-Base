# 06.11 — AI Impact Assessment

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Impact Assessment

### 01. Introduction to AI Impact Assessment

AI impact assessment is a structured process used to understand **how an AI system may affect individuals, groups, organizations, society, and other affected stakeholders**.

It goes beyond asking:

> "What can go wrong with this AI system?"

It also asks:

> **"Who or what could be affected, how could they be affected, and how significant could those effects be?"**

This distinction is important.

Risk assessment primarily focuses on identifying and evaluating **uncertainty and potential adverse events**.

Impact assessment focuses more broadly on the **consequences and effects of deploying or using AI**.

A simplified distinction is:

```text
AI Risk Assessment
       ↓
What could go wrong?
       ↓
How likely?
       ↓
How severe?

AI Impact Assessment
       ↓
Who / what could be affected?
       ↓
How could they be affected?
       ↓
What are the consequences?
       ↓
How significant are those effects?
```

---

### 02. Why AI Impact Assessment Matters

AI systems can influence decisions, services, opportunities, information, and human behavior.

An AI system may therefore create effects that are not immediately visible from a conventional cybersecurity assessment.

For example, an AI system could affect:

* Employees.
* Customers.
* Citizens.
* Applicants.
* Suppliers.
* Business operations.
* Organizational reputation.
* Access to services.
* Decision-making.
* Privacy.
* Security.
* Fundamental rights.
* Environmental resources.

The purpose of an impact assessment is to identify these effects **before they become unmanaged consequences**.

---

### 03. Impact Assessment Is Broader Than Security Assessment

A cybersecurity assessment might ask:

> Can an attacker compromise the AI system?

An AI impact assessment may additionally ask:

> What happens to people or organizations if the AI makes an incorrect decision?

For example:

```text
Cybersecurity Assessment
        ↓
Unauthorized Access
        ↓
Data Compromise

AI Impact Assessment
        ↓
Incorrect AI Decision
        ↓
Affected Person
        ↓
Financial / Social / Operational Consequence
```

Both perspectives can be important.

---

### 04. Impact Versus Risk

Impact and risk are related but not identical.

**Impact** describes the consequence or effect.

**Risk** generally considers uncertainty around potential consequences.

A simplified relationship is:

```text
Risk
=
Likelihood × Impact
```

However, AI impact assessment does not necessarily reduce every impact to a numerical risk score.

An impact assessment may document:

* Nature of the impact.
* Affected stakeholders.
* Severity.
* Scope.
* Duration.
* Reversibility.
* Distribution of effects.
* Existing safeguards.

---

### 05. AI Impact Assessment as a Structured Inquiry

A practical assessment begins with a series of questions:

1. What is the AI system?
2. What is its intended purpose?
3. Who will use it?
4. Who may be affected by it?
5. What decisions or activities does it influence?
6. What information does it process?
7. What outputs does it generate?
8. What actions can result from those outputs?
9. What positive impacts could occur?
10. What negative impacts could occur?
11. How significant could those impacts be?
12. What safeguards are available?

These questions establish the foundation for the assessment.

---

### 06. Understanding the AI System

Before assessing impact, the assessor must understand the system itself.

Relevant information may include:

* AI purpose.
* Business owner.
* Users.
* Intended population.
* Model type.
* Data sources.
* Inputs.
* Outputs.
* Decision authority.
* Human involvement.
* External dependencies.
* Deployment environment.

Without understanding the system, impact assessment becomes speculative.

---

### 07. Define the Intended Purpose

The intended purpose describes **why the organization is using AI**.

Examples:

* Customer-service assistance.
* Fraud detection.
* Document classification.
* Employee productivity.
* Predictive maintenance.
* Recommendation.
* Decision support.
* Content generation.
* Security monitoring.

The intended purpose establishes the context in which impacts should be evaluated.

---

### 08. Intended Use Versus Actual Use

An important distinction is:

> **How the organization intends the AI to be used versus how it may actually be used.**

For example:

**Intended use**

> AI provides recommendations to employees.

**Actual use**

> Employees begin treating AI recommendations as final decisions.

This difference can materially change the impact profile.

---

### 09. Define the Scope

An impact assessment should clearly define what is being assessed.

Scope may include:

* Specific AI application.
* Specific model.
* Specific business process.
* Specific geography.
* Specific population.
* Specific deployment stage.
* Specific use case.

For example:

> "This assessment covers the AI recruitment-screening system used for applicants in the organization's European operations."

A defined scope prevents ambiguity.

---

### 10. Identify Affected Stakeholders

One of the most important parts of an AI impact assessment is identifying **who may experience the effects of the AI system**.

Stakeholders may include:

```text
Organization
   │
   ├── Employees
   ├── Customers
   ├── Applicants
   ├── Suppliers
   ├── Partners
   ├── Contractors
   └── Management
```

Depending on the AI use case, broader groups may also be affected.

---

### 11. Directly Affected Individuals

A directly affected individual interacts with the AI system or is directly subject to its output.

Examples:

* A customer interacting with an AI assistant.
* An employee evaluated by an AI system.
* An applicant screened by AI.
* A customer whose transaction is evaluated by an AI fraud system.

The assessment should consider the nature and severity of the potential effect.

---

### 12. Indirectly Affected Individuals

People can also be affected without directly interacting with the AI.

For example:

```text
AI Decision
     ↓
Employee Action
     ↓
Customer Outcome
```

The customer may never interact with the AI but can still experience its consequences.

Therefore:

> **Impact assessment should not be limited to AI users.**

---

### 13. Affected Groups

Impact may occur at group level.

For example, an AI system may produce different outcomes for different populations.

The assessment should consider whether impacts may be:

* Unevenly distributed.
* Concentrated.
* Disproportionate.
* Systematic.

This is particularly important when AI influences access to opportunities, services, employment, or other significant outcomes.

---

### 14. Organizational Impact

AI does not only affect individuals.

It can also affect the organization itself.

Potential organizational impacts include:

* Operational disruption.
* Financial loss.
* Productivity changes.
* Reputational damage.
* Legal exposure.
* Compliance consequences.
* Security exposure.
* Strategic consequences.
* Dependency on technology providers.

The assessment should therefore consider both **human and organizational effects**.

---

### 15. Positive and Negative Impacts

Impact assessment should not focus exclusively on harm.

AI can create positive effects such as:

* Faster service.
* Improved accessibility.
* Reduced administrative burden.
* Better decision support.
* Improved operational efficiency.
* Increased availability of information.
* Improved detection capabilities.

The assessment should consider both:

```text
AI Use Case
    ↓
Positive Impacts
    +
Negative Impacts
```

The goal is to understand the overall impact profile.

---

### 16. Potential Negative Impact

Negative impact may take different forms.

Examples include:

* Financial harm.
* Operational harm.
* Privacy harm.
* Security harm.
* Loss of opportunity.
* Incorrect decisions.
* Reputational harm.
* Psychological or social effects.
* Restriction of access.
* Loss of autonomy.

The exact impact categories should be appropriate to the AI use case.

---

### 17. Impact Categories

A practical assessment may categorize impacts into several dimensions:

| Dimension      | Example                                    |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Individual     | Incorrect decision affecting a person      |
| Organizational | Operational disruption                     |
| Financial      | Monetary loss                              |
| Privacy        | Exposure or misuse of personal information |
| Security       | Increased security exposure                |
| Social         | Effects on groups or communities           |
| Legal          | Regulatory or contractual consequences     |
| Reputational   | Loss of trust                              |
| Environmental  | Increased resource consumption             |

Not every category will apply to every AI system.

---

### 18. Impact Pathway

A useful technique is to map how an AI output can become an actual consequence.

```text
Input
  ↓
AI Processing
  ↓
AI Output
  ↓
Human / Automated Decision
  ↓
Action
  ↓
Affected Stakeholder
  ↓
Impact
```

This prevents the assessment from stopping at the model output.

---

### 19. Example: AI Recruitment System

Consider an AI system used to support recruitment.

```text
Applicant Data
      ↓
AI Screening
      ↓
Candidate Ranking
      ↓
Recruiter Decision
      ↓
Interview / Rejection
      ↓
Applicant
```

Potential impacts may include:

* Incorrect candidate ranking.
* Missed qualified candidates.
* Reduced employment opportunity.
* Loss of trust.
* Organizational reputational consequences.

The assessment should therefore examine the **entire decision pathway**.

---

### 20. Example: AI Customer-Service System

Consider an AI customer-service assistant.

```text
Customer Query
      ↓
AI Response
      ↓
Customer Action
      ↓
Service Outcome
```

Potential positive impact:

> Faster customer support.

Potential negative impact:

> Incorrect information causes the customer to take an inappropriate action.

The assessment should consider both sides.

---

### 21. Example: AI Fraud Detection

An AI fraud system may identify suspicious transactions.

Positive impact:

> Reduced fraudulent activity.

Potential negative impact:

> Legitimate transactions are incorrectly blocked.

The impact assessment should therefore consider:

* Who may be affected?
* What happens when a legitimate transaction is blocked?
* How quickly can the issue be corrected?
* Is there a human review process?
* Can the customer appeal the decision?

---

### 22. Severity of Impact

Impact severity describes **how serious the consequence could be**.

A simple qualitative scale could be:

```text
Low
 ↓
Moderate
 ↓
High
 ↓
Severe
```

The organization should define what each level means.

For example:

**Low**

Limited inconvenience with minimal consequences.

**Moderate**

Meaningful operational or individual consequence requiring intervention.

**High**

Significant harm, financial loss, operational disruption, or other serious consequence.

**Severe**

Potentially major or irreversible consequences.

---

### 23. Scope of Impact

Severity is not the only consideration.

The number of affected people or systems can also matter.

For example:

```text
High Impact × 1 Person
```

and

```text
Moderate Impact × 100,000 People
```

represent different impact profiles.

Therefore, the assessment should consider both:

* **Magnitude**
* **Scale**

---

### 24. Duration of Impact

Impact may be:

* Temporary.
* Short-term.
* Long-term.
* Persistent.

For example:

> A temporary service interruption may have a different impact from an incorrect decision that permanently affects an individual's opportunity.

Duration should therefore be considered separately from immediate severity.

---

### 25. Reversibility

Another important question is:

> **Can the impact be reversed?**

Examples:

**Highly reversible**

> Incorrect recommendation corrected immediately.

**Partially reversible**

> Incorrect business decision corrected after financial loss.

**Difficult to reverse**

> A decision permanently affects an individual's opportunity.

**Irreversible**

> Certain forms of harm cannot meaningfully be undone.

Reversibility can materially influence impact significance.

---

### 26. Impact on Human Decision-Making

AI can influence human decisions even when it does not formally make the decision.

For example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Reviewer
       ↓
Final Decision
```

The assessment should consider whether the human reviewer:

* Independently evaluates the output.
* Understands AI limitations.
* Can challenge the recommendation.
* Has authority to reject it.

---

### 27. Degree of AI Influence

AI systems can have different levels of influence.

```text
Information
    ↓
Recommendation
    ↓
Decision Support
    ↓
Automated Decision
    ↓
Autonomous Action
```

As AI becomes more influential, the potential impact of incorrect or inappropriate behavior can increase.

The assessment should therefore document the AI's actual role in the process.

---

### 28. Human Control

An impact assessment should identify where humans remain involved.

Questions include:

* Is human review mandatory?
* At what stage?
* Who performs the review?
* What information do they receive?
* Can they override the AI?
* Can they stop the process?
* Is human intervention practical?

Human involvement should be evaluated based on **actual authority and capability**, not merely whether a human is technically present.

---

### 29. Dependency on AI Output

The organization should assess how heavily users or processes depend on the AI output.

For example:

**Low dependency**

> AI provides optional suggestions.

**High dependency**

> Business operations cannot proceed without the AI output.

Higher dependency may increase the potential organizational impact of system failure.

---

### 30. AI Impact Assessment Across the Lifecycle

Impact assessment should not necessarily occur only once.

A useful lifecycle view is:

```text
Concept
  ↓
Design
  ↓
Development
  ↓
Testing
  ↓
Deployment
  ↓
Operation
  ↓
Change
  ↓
Retirement
```

Impact characteristics can change as the system evolves.

---

### 31. Pre-Deployment Assessment

Before deployment, the organization can ask:

> "What impacts could this AI system create if we deploy it as designed?"

This provides an opportunity to identify problems before affected individuals encounter them.

---

### 32. Post-Deployment Assessment

After deployment, the organization can examine:

> "Are the actual impacts consistent with what we expected?"

This is important because real-world use can differ from assumptions made during design.

---

### 33. Change-Driven Reassessment

An AI impact assessment may need to be revisited when there are significant changes.

Examples:

* New model.
* New data source.
* New user population.
* New geography.
* New functionality.
* Increased autonomy.
* New integration.
* Significant change in business process.

A change can alter the impact profile.

---

### 34. Assumptions

Impact assessments often rely on assumptions.

Examples:

> "Users will review AI recommendations before acting."

> "The model will only process approved information."

> "The system will be used only for the defined business purpose."

These assumptions should be explicitly documented.

Why?

Because if an assumption becomes false, the impact assessment may no longer be reliable.

---

### 35. Assessment Evidence

An impact assessment should be supported by evidence where appropriate.

Evidence may include:

* System documentation.
* Architecture diagrams.
* Data-flow diagrams.
* User requirements.
* Business-process documentation.
* Testing results.
* Stakeholder interviews.
* Model documentation.
* Vendor documentation.
* Existing risk assessments.

The quality of the assessment depends partly on the quality of its underlying information.

---

### 36. Stakeholder Consultation

Impact assessment should involve relevant stakeholders.

Depending on the use case, this could include:

* Business owner.
* AI/product owner.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Compliance.
* HR.
* Data governance.
* Internal audit.
* Affected business users.

The purpose is to bring different perspectives into the assessment.

---

### 37. Why Stakeholder Input Matters

A technical team may see:

> "The model is functioning correctly."

A business team may see:

> "The process is creating operational problems."

An affected stakeholder may see:

> "The decision is creating an unfair or harmful outcome."

Impact assessment benefits from considering these perspectives together.

---

### 38. Impact Assessment Questions

A practical interview or assessment questionnaire can include:

### System

* What does the AI system do?
* What is its intended purpose?
* What decisions or actions does it influence?

### Stakeholders

* Who uses the system?
* Who is directly affected?
* Who is indirectly affected?

### Effects

* What positive effects could occur?
* What negative effects could occur?
* How severe could they be?
* How many people or systems could be affected?
* How long could effects last?
* Can they be reversed?

### Controls

* What safeguards exist?
* Where is human oversight applied?
* What happens when the AI is wrong?

---

### 39. Impact Assessment Documentation

A basic assessment record could contain:

| Field               | Description                           |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| AI System           | System being assessed                 |

| Scope               | Assessment boundaries                 |
| Stakeholders        | Affected parties                      |

| Impact Type         | Financial, privacy, operational, etc. |

| Evidence            | Supporting information                |
| Assessment Owner    | Responsible party                     |
| Review Date         | Reassessment point                    |

---

### 40. GRC Role in AI Impact Assessment

The GRC professional does not necessarily need to build the AI model.

The GRC role is to ensure that the assessment process is:

* Structured.
* Consistent.
* Evidence-based.
* Documented.
* Risk-informed.
* Assigned to accountable stakeholders.
* Subject to appropriate review.

The GRC professional acts as a **governance and risk integrator**.

---

### 41. First-Line Responsibility

The business or AI owner typically understands:

* The purpose of the AI system.
* The business process.
* Expected users.
* Operational consequences.
* Intended outcomes.

Therefore, the first line provides essential information about the actual use case and its effects.

---

### 42. Second-Line Oversight

Risk, compliance, privacy, or GRC functions can provide:

* Methodology.
* Standards.
* Challenge.
* Review.
* Risk interpretation.
* Governance oversight.

The second line should not simply accept the business assessment without appropriate challenge.

---

### 43. Independent Assurance

Where appropriate, internal audit or another independent assurance function can evaluate:

* Whether the process was followed.
* Whether documentation is adequate.
* Whether controls are operating.
* Whether management's conclusions are supported.

This provides an additional level of assurance.

---

### 44. Impact Assessment and Accountability

An assessment should identify who is accountable for the AI system.

Possible roles include:

* Business owner.
* Product owner.
* AI system owner.
* Risk owner.
* Control owner.

A document without clear accountability can become merely administrative.

---

### 45. Impact Assessment and Decision-Making

The output of an impact assessment should support a decision.

Possible outcomes include:

```text
Proceed
   ↓
Proceed With Conditions
   ↓
Redesign
   ↓
Restrict Use
   ↓
Do Not Deploy
```

The assessment is therefore not merely documentation.

It should influence whether and how the AI system is used.

---

### 46. Proportionality

Not every AI system requires the same depth of assessment.

A simple internal productivity assistant may require a lighter assessment than an AI system influencing significant decisions about individuals.

A proportional approach considers:

* Potential severity.
* Number of affected people.
* AI autonomy.
* Sensitivity of data.
* Business criticality.
* Regulatory context.
* Reversibility.

---

### 47. Avoiding a Checklist Mentality

A weak approach is:

> "Complete the questionnaire and close the assessment."

A stronger approach is:

> **"Use the assessment to understand the actual consequences of deploying and operating the AI system."**

The quality of the reasoning is more important than simply completing every field.

---

### 48. Impact Assessment as Evidence of Due Diligence

A well-designed assessment can demonstrate that the organization:

* Considered potential effects.
* Identified affected stakeholders.
* Evaluated consequences.
* Considered safeguards.
* Assigned accountability.
* Made an informed deployment decision.

This can strengthen governance and accountability.

---

### 49. Common Mistakes

Common weaknesses include:

**1. Focusing only on cybersecurity**

Ignoring broader human, organizational, or societal effects.

**2. Assessing only the AI model**

Ignoring downstream decisions and actions.

**3. Ignoring indirect stakeholders**

Only considering system users.

**4. Treating impact as a single number**

Ignoring severity, scale, duration, and reversibility.

**5. Assuming human oversight is automatically effective**

Not verifying whether humans can actually intervene.

**6. Treating the assessment as one-time documentation**

Failing to reassess when the system changes.

---

### 50. Core Mental Model

The simplest way to remember AI impact assessment is:

```text
AI System
   ↓
Who is affected?
   ↓
How are they affected?
   ↓
What happens as a result?
   ↓
How significant is the effect?
   ↓
What safeguards exist?
   ↓
Is the impact acceptable?
```

---

### 51. Part 1 Summary

AI impact assessment establishes a structured understanding of the effects an AI system may create.

Key principles:

1. Understand the AI system first.
2. Define its intended purpose.
3. Define assessment scope.
4. Identify direct stakeholders.
5. Identify indirect stakeholders.
6. Consider individuals and groups.
7. Consider organizational effects.
8. Identify positive impacts.
9. Identify negative impacts.
10. Map the impact pathway.
11. Consider severity.
12. Consider scale.
13. Consider duration.
14. Consider reversibility.
15. Consider AI influence on human decisions.
16. Assess actual human control.
17. Consider dependency on AI output.
18. Document assumptions.
19. Use appropriate evidence.
20. Consult relevant stakeholders.
21. Apply proportionality.
22. Consider the full AI lifecycle.
23. Reassess after significant changes.
24. Assign accountability.
25. Use the assessment to support deployment decisions.

### Core Anchor

> **AI System → Purpose → Stakeholders → Impact Pathway → Consequences → Severity → Scale → Duration → Reversibility → Safeguards → Decision**

The central GRC principle is:

> **"An AI impact assessment is not simply an exercise to identify what could go wrong with the technology. It is a structured evaluation of who and what may be affected by the AI system, how those effects may occur, how significant they may be, and whether the organization has sufficient understanding and safeguards to make a responsible decision about its use."**


# 06.11 — AI Impact Assessment

## Part 2 — AI Impact Assessment Methodology and Assessment Process

### 52. From Concept to Formal Assessment

An AI impact assessment should follow a repeatable methodology rather than relying on informal judgment.

A practical process is:

```text
Identify AI System
       ↓
Define Purpose and Scope
       ↓
Identify Stakeholders
       ↓
Map AI Decision / Action Flow
       ↓
Identify Potential Impacts
       ↓
Assess Impact Characteristics
       ↓
Identify Existing Safeguards
       ↓
Document Findings
       ↓
Determine Required Actions
       ↓
Management Decision
```

The methodology should be consistent enough that different AI systems can be assessed using comparable criteria.

---

### 53. Step 1 — Identify the AI System

The first step is to establish exactly what is being assessed.

Capture information such as:

* System name.
* Business owner.
* Technical owner.
* AI provider.
* Model or service used.
* Business process.
* Intended users.
* Deployment environment.
* Data processed.
* AI capabilities.

Example:

> **AI System:** Customer Support Assistant
> **Purpose:** Assist customer-service representatives with response generation
> **Users:** Customer-service employees
> **Output:** Suggested responses and knowledge summaries

This establishes the assessment subject.

---

### 54. Step 2 — Define the Intended Purpose

The assessment should clearly state:

> **What is the AI intended to accomplish?**

For example:

> "The AI system is intended to assist customer-service employees by generating draft responses based on approved organizational knowledge."

This is important because impacts must be evaluated against the system's intended purpose.

---

### 55. Step 3 — Define the Scope

The scope should identify the boundaries of the assessment.

For example:

```text id="slj6pz"
Included
✓ AI application
✓ Model
✓ Input data
✓ Output
✓ Users
✓ Business process
✓ Affected customers

Excluded
✗ Unrelated applications
✗ Unrelated business processes
```

Clear boundaries prevent scope confusion.

---

### 56. Step 4 — Identify the AI Lifecycle Stage

Determine where the system currently sits:

```text id="6lyl5a"
Concept
   ↓
Design
   ↓
Development
   ↓
Testing
   ↓
Pilot
   ↓
Production
   ↓
Modification
   ↓
Retirement
```

The potential impact may differ significantly between these stages.

---

### 57. Step 5 — Identify Stakeholders

Create a stakeholder map.

| Stakeholder    | Relationship             |
| -------------- | ------------------------ |
| Business Owner | Accountable for use case |
| Employees      | Direct users             |
| Customers      | Potentially affected     |
| Security       | Security oversight       |
| Privacy        | Privacy oversight        |
| Legal          | Legal interpretation     |
| GRC            | Risk and governance      |
| Vendor         | Technology provider      |

Not every stakeholder needs to participate in every assessment, but relevant perspectives should be represented.

---

### 58. Stakeholder Mapping

A useful classification is:

```text id="s1x5fl"
Directly Affected
       ↓
Indirectly Affected
       ↓
Operational Stakeholders
       ↓
Governance Stakeholders
       ↓
External Stakeholders
```

This prevents the assessment from focusing exclusively on the technical team.

---

### 59. Step 6 — Map the AI Process

The assessor should understand how information moves through the system.

For example:

```text id="2q7z4q"
User Input
    ↓
Data Processing
    ↓
AI Model
    ↓
AI Output
    ↓
Human Review
    ↓
Business Decision
    ↓
Customer Outcome
```

The impact assessment should consider the **entire pathway**, not just the model.

---

### 60. Step 7 — Identify Decision Points

Determine where AI influences decisions.

For example:

```text id="f4u9sa"
AI Output
    ↓
Human Decision
```

or:

```text id="b2zq4g"
AI Output
    ↓
Automated Decision
```

or:

```text id="8r8u0y"
AI Output
    ↓
AI Agent
    ↓
Autonomous Action
```

The degree of AI involvement can significantly influence the impact assessment.

---

### 61. Step 8 — Identify Potential Positive Impacts

A balanced assessment should identify benefits.

Potential positive impacts include:

* Faster service.
* Improved accessibility.
* Reduced administrative workload.
* Better consistency.
* Improved decision support.
* Reduced operational costs.
* Increased availability of information.
* Improved customer experience.

Example:

> AI-assisted customer service reduces average response preparation time.

The assessment should document these benefits rather than focusing only on negative consequences.

---

### 62. Step 9 — Identify Potential Negative Impacts

Next, identify possible adverse effects.

Examples:

* Incorrect decisions.
* Financial loss.
* Privacy intrusion.
* Discrimination.
* Security exposure.
* Loss of opportunity.
* Operational disruption.
* Reputational damage.
* Reduced human autonomy.

A useful question is:

> **"If the AI does something incorrectly, who experiences the consequence?"**

---

### 63. Impact Scenario

Instead of writing:

> "The AI could be inaccurate."

Develop a specific scenario:

> "The AI generates an incorrect eligibility recommendation, which is accepted by the employee and results in an eligible applicant being incorrectly rejected."

This makes the impact much easier to analyze.

---

### 64. Cause → Event → Impact

A useful structure is:

```text id="o6jvl7"
Cause
  ↓
AI Event
  ↓
Decision / Action
  ↓
Affected Stakeholder
  ↓
Impact
```

Example:

```text
Incorrect training data
        ↓
Incorrect AI classification
        ↓
Incorrect employee recommendation
        ↓
Employee affected
        ↓
Loss of opportunity
```

This creates traceability between the AI behavior and the eventual consequence.

---

### 65. Step 10 — Assess Impact Severity

Impact severity should be defined consistently.

Example scale:

| Level           | Description                                          |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 — Low         | Minor inconvenience                                  |
| 2 — Moderate    | Meaningful but manageable effect                     |
| 3 — Significant | Serious effect requiring intervention                |
| 4 — High        | Major consequence                                    |
| 5 — Severe      | Potentially catastrophic or irreversible consequence |

Organizations should customize definitions to their environment.

---

### 66. Severity Factors

Severity can consider:

* Magnitude of harm.
* Financial consequence.
* Number of affected individuals.
* Sensitivity of the affected population.
* Duration.
* Reversibility.
* Criticality of the decision.
* Legal or regulatory consequence.

Severity should not be based solely on financial loss.

---

### 67. Step 11 — Assess Scale

Scale asks:

> **How many people, systems, transactions, or organizational units could be affected?**

For example:

```text id="0v1p6c"
1 person
      ↓
10 people
      ↓
1,000 people
      ↓
100,000 people
      ↓
Millions
```

An impact affecting a large population may require additional scrutiny even if the individual impact is moderate.

---

### 68. Step 12 — Assess Duration

Determine how long the effect could last.

Example:

| Duration   | Example                            |
| ---------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Immediate  | Temporary incorrect recommendation |
| Short-term | Service disruption                 |
| Long-term  | Persistent incorrect profile       |
| Permanent  | Irreversible consequence           |

Duration should be assessed together with severity.

---

### 69. Step 13 — Assess Reversibility

Ask:

> **Can the consequence be corrected?**

Examples:

**Highly reversible**

> Incorrect AI-generated email corrected before sending.

**Reversible**

> Incorrect customer classification corrected after review.

**Difficult to reverse**

> Incorrect decision causes lost opportunity that cannot easily be restored.

**Irreversible**

> Certain consequences cannot meaningfully be undone.

---

### 70. Step 14 — Assess Detectability

Another useful factor is:

> **How easily can the harmful effect be detected before or after it occurs?**

Example:

```text id="w7r7u0"
Easy to Detect
      ↓
Human Review
      ↓
Moderate Detection
      ↓
Automated Monitoring
      ↓
Difficult to Detect
```

An impact that is difficult to detect may require stronger preventive safeguards.

---

### 71. Step 15 — Assess Exposure

Exposure considers how broadly the AI system can operate.

Examples:

* Number of users.
* Number of customers.
* Number of transactions.
* Number of decisions.
* Geographic scope.
* Business units.
* External integrations.

A system operating across an entire enterprise generally has a different exposure profile from a small internal pilot.

---

### 72. Step 16 — Assess AI Autonomy

Document the level of autonomy.

For example:

```text id="p7av9u"
Level 1
Information Only

Level 2
Recommendation

Level 3
Decision Support

Level 4
Automated Decision

Level 5
Autonomous Action
```

The organization should define its own categories, but the principle is to understand **how much authority the AI has**.

---

### 73. Step 17 — Assess Human Oversight

Document:

* Whether human review exists.
* When review occurs.
* Who performs it.
* What information is available.
* Whether the human can override the AI.
* Whether intervention is practical.
* Whether the reviewer is appropriately trained.

A nominal human approval step may not provide meaningful protection if the reviewer simply accepts AI output without independent evaluation.

---

### 74. Step 18 — Assess Dependency

Determine how dependent the process is on AI.

For example:

**Low dependency**

> AI provides optional suggestions.

**Moderate dependency**

> Employees normally rely on AI but can work without it.

**High dependency**

> The business process relies heavily on AI.

**Critical dependency**

> The process cannot effectively operate without AI.

Dependency affects both operational and organizational impact.

---

### 75. Step 19 — Identify Existing Safeguards

The assessment should identify controls already in place.

Examples:

* Access control.
* Data validation.
* Human review.
* Output filtering.
* Monitoring.
* Audit logging.
* Business continuity.
* Incident response.
* User training.
* Change management.

This prevents the assessment from assuming that the AI operates without controls.

---

### 76. Step 20 — Evaluate Safeguard Adequacy

The existence of a safeguard does not mean it is sufficient.

For each safeguard, ask:

```text id="3r3n7c"
Does it exist?
      ↓
Is it implemented?
      ↓
Is it operating?
      ↓
Is it effective?
      ↓
Does it reduce the impact?
```

This is an important GRC distinction.

---

### 77. Impact Assessment Matrix

A practical assessment can use a matrix:

| Impact Dimension | Rating | Explanation             |
| ---------------- | -----: | ----------------------- |
| Severity         |      4 | Significant consequence |
| Scale            |      3 | Large user population   |
| Duration         |      2 | Short-term              |
| Reversibility    |      2 | Mostly reversible       |
| Detectability    |      3 | Moderate                |
| AI Autonomy      |      2 | Human approval required |

The organization can use these dimensions without necessarily combining them into one number.

---

### 78. Why Multiple Dimensions Matter

Consider two AI systems:

**System A**

> High severity but affects only one person and is easily detected.

**System B**

> Moderate severity affecting hundreds of thousands of people and difficult to detect.

A single severity score may fail to capture the difference.

Multi-dimensional assessment provides a more complete picture.

---

### 79. Impact Heat Map

Organizations may visualize impact dimensions using a heat map.

For example:

```text id="h1y4v9"
                 IMPACT SEVERITY
              Low   Medium   High
            ┌─────┬────────┬───────┐
Small       │  L  │   M    │   H   │
            ├─────┼────────┼───────┤
Medium      │  M  │   H    │  VH   │
            ├─────┼────────┼───────┤
Large       │  H  │   VH   │  CR   │
            └─────┴────────┴───────┘
                 SCALE
```

The exact methodology should be defined by the organization.

---

### 80. Qualitative Assessment

Not every impact needs quantitative measurement.

A qualitative assessment may state:

> "The potential impact is high because an incorrect AI recommendation could significantly affect an individual's access to an important service and may be difficult to reverse."

This can be more meaningful than an arbitrary numerical score.

---

### 81. Quantitative Assessment

Where reliable data exists, organizations can estimate:

* Number of affected individuals.
* Expected financial loss.
* Number of transactions.
* Probability of occurrence.
* Operational downtime.
* Expected remediation cost.

Quantitative estimates should clearly document their assumptions.

---

### 82. Avoiding False Precision

Suppose an assessment produces:

> Impact = 7.63

The number may appear scientifically precise even when based on subjective assumptions.

A more defensible approach may be:

> **High impact — affecting a large population, with potentially significant and difficult-to-reverse consequences.**

The assessment methodology should match the quality of available evidence.

---

### 83. Scenario-Based Assessment

Scenario analysis can improve assessment quality.

Example:

> **Scenario:** AI incorrectly identifies a legitimate customer transaction as fraudulent.

Then evaluate:

* Who is affected?
* What happens?
* How long does the effect last?
* Can it be corrected?
* How many customers could experience it?
* Is human review available?

This produces a more realistic impact assessment.

---

### 84. Worst-Case Scenario

The assessment may also consider a credible worst-case scenario.

For example:

> "What is the most severe reasonably foreseeable consequence if the AI behaves incorrectly?"

This does not mean assuming an unrealistic catastrophe.

The objective is to understand the upper boundary of credible impact.

---

### 85. Reasonably Foreseeable Impact

Impact assessment should avoid both extremes:

**Underestimation**

> "Nothing serious will happen."

**Unrealistic exaggeration**

> "The AI could theoretically destroy the entire organization."

A mature assessment focuses on **credible and reasonably foreseeable consequences**.

---

### 86. Impact Cascades

An AI error can create secondary and tertiary consequences.

Example:

```text id="3u1n9d"
AI Error
   ↓
Incorrect Decision
   ↓
Customer Complaint
   ↓
Operational Workload
   ↓
Reputational Damage
   ↓
Financial Loss
```

The assessment should consider these cascading effects when material.

---

### 87. Direct and Indirect Impact

A useful distinction:

**Direct impact**

> AI incorrectly rejects a customer request.

**Indirect impact**

> The rejection increases complaints, operational workload, and reputational pressure.

Both can be relevant.

---

### 88. Cumulative Impact

Repeated low-level impacts can become significant.

For example:

```text id="b8k0uo"
Small Impact
     +
Small Impact
     +
Small Impact
     +
Small Impact
     ↓
Cumulative Significant Impact
```

Therefore, frequency and scale should be considered alongside individual-event severity.

---

### 89. Distribution of Impact

Impact may not be evenly distributed.

For example:

> An AI system may perform acceptably for the general population while producing significantly worse outcomes for a particular subgroup.

The assessment should therefore ask:

* Who experiences the impact?
* Is the impact evenly distributed?
* Are particular groups disproportionately affected?

---

### 90. Vulnerable or Sensitive Populations

Some use cases may involve people who are particularly vulnerable to adverse consequences.

Examples could include systems affecting:

* Children.
* Patients.
* Job applicants.
* Financially vulnerable customers.
* Individuals dependent on essential services.

The organization should determine whether additional safeguards are appropriate.

---

### 91. Organizational Impact Assessment

Impact assessment should also examine the organization's exposure.

Possible dimensions:

```text id="8x6s7p"
Operational
Financial
Legal
Regulatory
Security
Reputational
Strategic
Third-Party
Business Continuity
```

This creates an enterprise perspective.

---

### 92. Operational Impact

Questions include:

* Could AI failure interrupt operations?
* Could employees become unable to perform critical processes?
* Is there a manual fallback?
* How long could the organization operate without the AI?

This is particularly important for business-critical AI.

---

### 93. Financial Impact

Financial consequences may include:

* Direct financial loss.
* Fraud.
* Incorrect payments.
* Lost revenue.
* Remediation cost.
* Regulatory penalties where applicable.
* Customer compensation.
* Increased operational costs.

The organization should use realistic assumptions rather than arbitrary numbers.

---

### 94. Reputational Impact

AI failures can affect organizational trust.

For example:

> A widely publicized AI failure may cause customers to question the organization's reliability or responsible use of technology.

Reputational impact can be difficult to quantify, but it should not be ignored.

---

### 95. Strategic Impact

AI can create strategic consequences if:

* The organization becomes overly dependent on a provider.
* AI capabilities create competitive advantages or disadvantages.
* The organization adopts technology that becomes difficult to replace.
* AI failure undermines a strategic initiative.

Impact assessment should therefore consider more than immediate operational consequences.

---

### 96. Decision-Making Impact

An important question is:

> **How consequential is the decision influenced by AI?**

Compare:

```text id="k7y9sx"
AI chooses email wording
        ↓
Low consequence
```

with:

```text
AI influences employment decision
        ↓
Potentially high consequence
```

The second scenario generally warrants more rigorous assessment.

---

### 97. Assessment Findings

After evaluating impacts, findings should be documented clearly.

Example:

> "The AI system may generate inaccurate recommendations that could influence customer eligibility decisions. The potential impact is significant because affected decisions may result in financial or service consequences. Human review exists but is not mandatory for all cases."

This is much more useful than:

> "AI risk: Medium."

---

### 98. Assessment Recommendations

Where weaknesses are identified, recommendations should be specific.

Weak:

> "Improve AI governance."

Stronger:

> "Require human validation for AI-generated eligibility recommendations above the defined impact threshold and retain evidence of the review."

Specific recommendations can be assigned and tracked.

---

### 99. Management Decision

The assessment should support an explicit decision.

Possible outcomes:

```text id="1n9z9k"
Approved
   ↓
Approved With Conditions
   ↓
Redesign Required
   ↓
Restricted Deployment
   ↓
Deployment Not Approved
```

The appropriate authority should make the decision according to organizational governance.

---

### 100. Conditions of Approval

An AI system may be approved subject to conditions.

Examples:

* Human review required.
* Certain data excluded.
* Limited user population.
* Restricted geography.
* Additional monitoring.
* Periodic reassessment.
* Additional testing before expansion.

This allows organizations to manage risk without automatically rejecting useful AI.

---

### 101. Documentation of Decision

The assessment record should capture:

* Decision.
* Decision-maker.
* Date.
* Conditions.
* Supporting evidence.
* Outstanding actions.
* Review date.

This creates accountability and auditability.

---

### 102. Review Triggers

The assessment should define when it must be revisited.

Examples:

```text id="f7w2n5"
New Model
New Data
New Users
New Geography
New Function
Increased Autonomy
Major Incident
Regulatory Change
Significant Performance Change
        ↓
Impact Reassessment
```

This is particularly important for AI systems that evolve over time.

---


### 104. Practical Example

Consider an AI system that assists with employee recruitment.

**Purpose**

> Rank applicants for recruiter review.

**Stakeholders**

* Applicants.
* Recruiters.
* HR.
* Business management.
* AI provider.

**Impact scenario**

> An inaccurate recommendation causes a qualified applicant to receive a lower ranking.

**Potential impact**

* Lost employment opportunity.
* Reduced applicant trust.
* HR process impact.
* Reputational consequences.

**Assessment considerations**

```text id="3g5m8j"

Scale          → Potentially Large
Duration       → Potentially Long
Reversibility  → Partially Reversible
AI Influence   → Decision Support
Human Review   → Required
```

The assessment can then determine whether existing safeguards are sufficient.

---

### 105. Practical Example — Customer Service

**AI system**

Customer-service response assistant.

**Potential impact**

> Incorrect AI-generated advice causes a customer to misunderstand an important service requirement.

**Assessment**

```text id="zj7m0p"
AI Response
    ↓
Employee Review
    ↓
Customer
    ↓
Potential Consequence
```

If human review is mandatory and employees are appropriately trained, the impact may be significantly reduced.

---

### 106. Practical Example — Internal Productivity AI

An employee uses an AI assistant to summarize internal documents.

Potential impacts may include:

* Incorrect summary.
* Sensitive information exposure.
* Employee misunderstanding.
* Operational errors.

The assessment may be lower complexity than a system that makes decisions affecting customers, but it should still identify relevant impacts.

---

### 107. Practical Example — Autonomous AI

An AI agent can execute IT changes automatically.

Potential impact:

> An incorrect action causes a critical service interruption.

The assessment should consider:

* Autonomy.
* System criticality.
* Scope of permissions.
* Blast radius.
* Human intervention.
* Rollback.
* Monitoring.
* Business continuity.

This demonstrates why AI impact assessment must consider **what the AI is empowered to do**, not simply what model it uses.

### 108. GRC Professional Checklist

Before closing an AI impact assessment, ask:

**System**

* Do I understand the AI system?
* Is its purpose clearly defined?
* Is the scope clear?

**Stakeholders**

* Have direct stakeholders been identified?
* Have indirect stakeholders been considered?
* Have potentially affected groups been considered?

**Impact**

* Have positive impacts been considered?
* Have negative impacts been identified?
* Are impact scenarios specific?
* Have severity, scale, duration, and reversibility been assessed?

**Control**

* What safeguards already exist?
* Are they adequate?
* Is human oversight meaningful?

**Governance**

* Is there a clear owner?
* Has the appropriate authority reviewed the assessment?
* Are decision conditions documented?
* Are reassessment triggers defined?

---

### 109. Common Assessment Errors

**Error 1 — Assessing the technology instead of the use case**

The same model can have completely different impacts depending on how it is used.

**Error 2 — Looking only at direct users**

People who never interact with AI may still be affected.

**Error 3 — Ignoring downstream decisions**

AI output can influence decisions and actions.

**Error 4 — Using one generic impact score**

Impact has multiple dimensions.

**Error 5 — Ignoring reversibility**

Some effects are much harder to correct than others.

**Error 6 — Assuming human oversight is sufficient**

Human presence does not guarantee meaningful oversight.

**Error 7 — Treating the assessment as static**

AI systems and their environments can change.

---

# 06.11 — AI Impact Assessment

## Part 3 — AI Impact Assessment Governance, Evidence, and Decision-Making

### 112. Governance Context

AI impact assessment should operate within the organization's broader governance structure.

It should not exist as an isolated document created by a project team.

A mature governance structure connects:

```text
AI Governance
      ↓
Impact Assessment
      ↓
Risk / Control Decisions
      ↓
Management Accountability
      ↓
Monitoring and Review
```

The assessment becomes part of the organization's decision-making process.

---

### 113. Ownership of the Assessment

A fundamental governance question is:

> **Who owns the AI impact assessment?**

The answer should be explicitly defined.

Possible responsibilities include:

* AI system owner.
* Business owner.
* Product owner.
* Risk owner.
* GRC function.
* Privacy function.
* Compliance function.

The person responsible for the assessment should have sufficient authority and access to information.

---

### 114. Accountability Versus Responsibility

These concepts should be distinguished.

**Responsibility**

> Performing or contributing to the assessment.

**Accountability**

> Being answerable for the decision and outcome.

For example:

```text
GRC
  ↓
Provides methodology

AI Owner
  ↓
Provides system information

Risk Function
  ↓
Provides challenge

Management
  ↓
Makes accountable decision
```

The exact allocation depends on the organization's governance model.

---

### 115. Three Lines Perspective

A three-lines model can help organize responsibilities.

**First line**

Owns and operates the AI system.

**Second line**

Provides risk, compliance, privacy, security, and governance oversight.

**Third line**

Provides independent assurance.

The objective is to prevent the AI impact assessment from becoming solely a compliance exercise owned by one department.

---

### 116. Independence of Assessment

Where impact is potentially significant, the assessment should receive appropriate independent challenge.

For example:

```text
AI Owner
    ↓
Completes Assessment
    ↓
GRC / Risk Review
    ↓
Challenge
    ↓
Management Decision
```

The reviewer should be able to challenge assumptions and conclusions.

---

### 117. Assessment Methodology Ownership

The organization should define who owns the assessment methodology.

The methodology may specify:

* Required assessment stages.
* Impact dimensions.
* Rating criteria.
* Evidence requirements.
* Approval thresholds.
* Review frequency.
* Escalation requirements.
* Reassessment triggers.

This creates consistency across AI systems.

---

### 118. Standardization

Without standardization, two teams may evaluate similar AI systems differently.

For example:

```text
Team A
"High impact"

Team B
"Medium impact"
```

even though both systems have similar characteristics.

A standardized methodology improves:

* Comparability.
* Governance.
* Reporting.
* Auditability.
* Management decision-making.

---

### 119. Assessment Thresholds

Organizations can establish thresholds determining when an assessment is mandatory.

For example:

```text
Low-impact AI
      ↓
Simplified Assessment

Moderate-impact AI
      ↓
Standard Assessment

High-impact AI
      ↓
Enhanced Assessment

Critical-impact AI
      ↓
Enhanced Assessment + Senior Approval
```

The organization should define these thresholds according to its risk appetite and applicable requirements.

---

### 120. Proportionality in Governance

Governance should be proportional to potential impact.

A simple AI system used internally for low-consequence administrative tasks should not necessarily require the same governance burden as an AI system influencing significant decisions about individuals.

The principle is:

> **Higher potential impact → stronger governance and scrutiny.**

---

### 121. Evidence Requirements

An impact assessment should be supported by evidence.

Examples include:

* AI system documentation.
* Architecture diagrams.
* Data-flow diagrams.
* Business-process documentation.
* User requirements.
* Model documentation.
* Testing results.
* Validation results.
* Stakeholder interviews.
* Control documentation.
* Monitoring results.

Evidence allows reviewers to challenge conclusions objectively.

---

### 122. Evidence Traceability

A strong assessment connects conclusions to evidence.

For example:

```text
Impact Finding
      ↓
Impact Scenario
      ↓
Supporting Evidence
      ↓
Control Evaluation
      ↓
Assessment Conclusion
```

This creates traceability.

---

### 123. Assessment Assumptions

Assumptions should be explicitly recorded.

Examples:

> "Human reviewers will validate AI recommendations."

> "The AI system will only process approved data."

> "Users will not use the system for autonomous decision-making."

These assumptions should not remain hidden.

---

### 124. Assumption Validation

An assumption should be tested where practical.

For example:

**Assumption**

> Employees independently review AI recommendations.

**Evidence**

> Review procedure, training records, audit samples, and workflow controls.

If evidence shows employees routinely accept AI recommendations without review, the original assumption may be invalid.

---

### 125. Assessment Limitations

The assessment should also document limitations.

Examples:

* Insufficient historical data.
* Limited testing population.
* Incomplete vendor information.
* Unknown future use cases.
* Limited operational experience.
* Uncertain impact magnitude.

Transparency about limitations is better than presenting unsupported certainty.

---

### 126. Stakeholder Interviews

Interviews can reveal impacts that technical documentation does not show.

For example, a technical team may state:

> "The system only provides recommendations."

A business user may explain:

> "In practice, employees normally follow the recommendation without further analysis."

This difference can materially change the impact assessment.

---

### 127. Stakeholder Challenge

Stakeholders should be encouraged to challenge assumptions.

Useful questions include:

* What happens if the AI is wrong?
* Who notices?
* Who is affected?
* Who can correct it?
* What happens if the human reviewer disagrees?
* What happens if the AI is unavailable?
* What happens if usage expands beyond the original purpose?

These questions improve assessment quality.

---

### 128. Documentation Quality

A mature impact assessment should be:

**Clear**

The reader understands what is being assessed.

**Specific**

Impacts are expressed as concrete scenarios.

**Evidence-based**

Important conclusions have supporting information.

**Traceable**

Findings can be connected to evidence and decisions.

**Current**

The assessment reflects the current system.

**Actionable**

Identified issues have owners and actions.

---

### 129. Version Control

AI impact assessments should be version-controlled.

A record may contain:

| Version | Date         | Change             | Owner    |
| ------- | ------------ | ------------------ | -------- |
| 1.0     | Initial      | Initial assessment | AI Owner |
| 1.1     | Update       | New data source    | AI Owner |
| 2.0     | Major update | New model          | AI Owner |

This establishes assessment history.

---

### 130. Change Management

Changes to an AI system should trigger consideration of whether the impact assessment remains valid.

Relevant changes include:

* New model.
* Model retraining.
* New training data.
* New data source.
* New users.
* New geography.
* New integration.
* New functionality.
* Increased autonomy.
* Expanded population.

The assessment should be linked to the AI change-management process.

---

### 131. Assessment Approval Workflow

A practical workflow may look like:

```text

   ↓
Draft Assessment
   ↓
Business Review
   ↓
GRC / Risk Review
   ↓
Specialist Review
   ↓
Management Approval
   ↓
Deployment / Continued Operation
```

The exact workflow should reflect the organization's governance structure.

---

### 132. Specialist Review

Some AI systems may require specialist input.

Possible reviewers include:

* Cybersecurity.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Compliance.
* HR.
* Data governance.
* Safety.
* Procurement.
* Business continuity.

Specialist involvement should be based on the characteristics of the AI system.

---

### 133. Escalation

An impact assessment should define when issues must be escalated.

Examples:

```text
Low Impact
   ↓

Moderate Impact
   ↓
Risk / GRC Review

High Impact
   ↓
Senior Management Review

Critical Impact
   ↓
Executive / Specialized Governance
```

The actual thresholds should be defined by the organization.

---

### 134. Risk Appetite and Impact

Impact assessment should connect to organizational risk appetite.

For example:

> The organization may have very low tolerance for AI systems that could materially affect individuals without meaningful human oversight.

This helps translate assessment results into management decisions.

---

### 135. Impact Acceptance

Management may decide that a particular impact is acceptable under defined conditions.

For example:

> "The identified impact is accepted provided that human review remains mandatory and monitoring is implemented."

Acceptance should be:

* Explicit.
* Authorized.
* Documented.
* Time-bound where appropriate.
* Supported by rationale.

---

### 136. Conditional Approval

An AI system may receive conditional approval.

Example:

```text
AI System
   ↓
Impact Assessment
   ↓
High Impact Identified
   ↓
Additional Safeguards Required
   ↓
Conditional Approval
   ↓
Control Implementation
   ↓
Production
```

This creates a connection between impact findings and implementation requirements.

---

### 137. Assessment Findings to Actions

Each material finding should ideally produce an actionable response.

Example:

| Finding                                       | Action               | Owner          | Due |
| --------------------------------------------- | -------------------- | -------------- | --- |
| AI output may contain unsupported information | Require validation   | Business Owner | Q3  |
| Limited monitoring                            | Implement monitoring | IT             | Q3  |
| Users lack AI training                        | Deliver training     | HR/AI Owner    | Q3  |

This prevents findings from remaining purely theoretical.

---

### 138. Corrective Actions

Corrective actions may address:

* System design.
* Data.
* Human oversight.
* User permissions.
* Process controls.
* Monitoring.
* Documentation.
* Training.
* Business continuity.

Actions should be proportionate to the identified impact.

---

### 139. Preventive Actions

Preventive controls attempt to reduce the likelihood that harmful impacts occur.

Examples:

* Input validation.
* Restricted access.
* Approved use cases.
* Human review.
* Output constraints.
* Permission boundaries.
* Testing.
* User training.

Preventive controls are especially valuable where impacts could be severe.

---

### 140. Detective Controls

Detective controls identify problematic outcomes.

Examples:

* Monitoring.
* Audit logs.
* Quality checks.
* Exception reports.
* User feedback.
* Outcome sampling.
* Performance monitoring.

Detection becomes particularly important when prevention cannot eliminate all possible errors.

* Correcting inaccurate records.
* Suspending AI functionality.
* Escalating affected cases.
* Retraining users.
* Updating system configuration.

Corrective capability is important where impacts can occur despite preventive measures.

# 06.11 — AI Impact Assessment

## Part 4 — Advanced AI Impact Assessment, Practical Application, and GRC Mastery

### 166. Impact Assessment as a Decision Instrument

An AI impact assessment should ultimately help answer a management question:

> **"Should this AI system be deployed, continued, modified, restricted, or stopped?"**

The assessment therefore functions as a **decision instrument**, not merely as a compliance document.

A mature process connects:

```text
AI Use Case
     ↓
Potential Impacts
     ↓
Evidence
     ↓
Safeguards
     ↓
Residual Concerns
     ↓
Management Decision
```

---

### 167. Assess the Use Case, Not Just the Model

The same AI model can produce very different impact profiles depending on how it is used.

For example:

```text
Same AI Model
     │
     ├── Internal brainstorming
     │       ↓
     │    Lower potential impact
     │
     ├── Customer recommendations
     │       ↓
     │    Moderate potential impact
     │
     └── Employment decision support
             ↓
          Potentially significant impact
```

Therefore:

> **Impact assessment is primarily contextual.**

The model alone does not determine the impact.

---

### 168. Context Determines Impact

The assessment should consider:

* Purpose.
* Users.
* Affected population.
* Decision significance.
* Data involved.
* AI autonomy.
* Human oversight.
* Operational dependency.
* Consequence of failure.

This means two technically similar systems may require completely different governance.

---

### 169. Intended Purpose and Actual Purpose

An important advanced consideration is the difference between **documented purpose** and **real-world purpose**.

Example:

> Documented purpose: "Assist recruiters."

Actual practice:

> Recruiters routinely use the AI ranking as the basis for rejecting applicants.

The impact assessment should reflect **actual operational behavior**, not only formal documentation.

---

### 170. Function Creep

AI systems may gradually be used for purposes beyond their original scope.

Example:

```text
Original:
Customer-service assistance
       ↓
Expanded:
Customer profiling
       ↓
Expanded:
Customer prioritization
       ↓
Expanded:
Automated customer decisions
```

Each expansion may change the impact profile.

Therefore:

> **Changes in purpose should trigger impact reassessment.**

---

### 171. Use-Case Drift

Use-case drift occurs when actual AI usage gradually diverges from the approved use case.

Indicators may include:

* New user groups.
* New data.
* New business processes.
* New decision types.
* New integrations.
* Increased automation.

Monitoring should therefore consider not only technical performance but also **how the system is being used**.

---

### 172. Automation Bias

Humans may place excessive trust in AI recommendations.

For example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human assumes AI is correct
       ↓
Human approves
       ↓
Incorrect outcome
```

The assessment should consider whether users may become overly dependent on AI output.

---

### 173. Automation Bias Controls

Possible controls include:

* User training.
* Mandatory review procedures.
* Explanation of limitations.
* Independent validation.
* Escalation procedures.
* Random quality checks.
* Override monitoring.

The objective is meaningful human judgment rather than symbolic human involvement.

---

### 174. Human-in-the-Loop Versus Human-on-the-Loop

These concepts should be distinguished.

**Human-in-the-loop**

> Human actively reviews or approves the AI output before an action occurs.

**Human-on-the-loop**

> Human supervises the system and intervenes when necessary.

The impact assessment should determine whether the chosen level of human involvement is appropriate for the potential consequences.

---

### 175. Meaningful Human Oversight

Human oversight should be evaluated based on whether the human has:

* Sufficient knowledge.
* Sufficient time.
* Relevant information.
* Authority to intervene.
* Ability to override.
* Ability to stop the process.
* Understanding of AI limitations.

Simply placing a human at the end of an automated workflow does not automatically create effective oversight.

---

### 176. Impact of AI Errors

AI errors should be evaluated based on **what happens after the error**.

Consider:

```text
AI Error
   ↓
Detected immediately
   ↓
Human correction
   ↓
No material consequence
```

versus:

```text
AI Error
   ↓
Not detected
   ↓
Automated action
   ↓
Affected person
   ↓
Difficult-to-reverse consequence
```

The same technical error can therefore produce very different impacts.

---

### 177. Error Propagation

An AI error can propagate through interconnected systems.

Example:

```text
Incorrect AI Classification
          ↓
CRM Update
          ↓
Automated Workflow
          ↓
Customer Notification
          ↓
Customer Action
          ↓
Financial / Operational Consequence
```

The assessment should consider the entire chain.

---

### 178. Blast Radius

**Blast radius** describes how broadly an AI failure could spread.

Consider:

* Number of users.
* Number of transactions.
* Number of systems.
* Number of customers.
* Geographic coverage.
* Automation level.

A highly automated AI system with broad permissions may have a significantly larger blast radius than a manually reviewed system.

---

### 179. Containment

Impact assessment should consider whether harmful outcomes can be contained.

Questions include:

* Can the system be stopped quickly?
* Can permissions be revoked?
* Can affected decisions be identified?
* Can actions be rolled back?
* Can affected individuals be notified?
* Is there a manual fallback?

Strong containment capability can reduce the practical consequences of an incident.

---

### 180. Reversibility and Recovery

Reversibility should be assessed operationally.

For example:

> Can the organization restore the previous state?

Possible recovery mechanisms include:

* Rollback.
* Transaction reversal.
* Manual review.
* Data correction.
* Reprocessing.
* Customer remediation.

Recovery capability should be validated rather than assumed.

---

### 181. Impact on Trust

AI systems can affect trust between:

* Organization and customer.
* Employer and employee.
* Service provider and user.
* Organization and regulator.
* Business and partner.

Loss of trust can become a secondary organizational impact.

---

### 182. Transparency as an Impact Consideration

The assessment should consider whether affected stakeholders can understand:

* That AI is being used.
* What role AI plays.
* What decisions it influences.
* What human oversight exists.
* How errors can be challenged.

The appropriate level of transparency depends on the context.

---

### 183. Contestability

Where AI influences significant outcomes, the organization should consider whether affected people can challenge or seek correction of an outcome.

A practical assessment question is:

> **"If the AI produces an incorrect outcome, what mechanism exists to challenge and correct it?"**

This connects impact assessment with accountability.

---

### 184. Accessibility of Remediation

A correction mechanism may technically exist but still be ineffective if it is difficult to access.

For example:

```text
Appeal Process Exists
       ↓
Complex procedure
       ↓
Long delay
       ↓
Affected person cannot realistically use it
```

Impact assessment should therefore consider **practical accessibility**, not merely formal existence.

---

### 185. Uncertainty

AI impact assessment operates under uncertainty.

Unknowns may include:

* Future usage.
* Model behavior.
* Data quality.
* User behavior.
* Emerging failure modes.
* External dependencies.

The assessment should explicitly identify material uncertainty.

---

### 186. Uncertainty Should Not Be Ignored

A weak conclusion is:

> "No evidence of impact exists."

A stronger conclusion is:

> "Available evidence is insufficient to determine the full impact with confidence; therefore, additional monitoring and controlled deployment are required."

Lack of evidence is not necessarily evidence of lack of impact.

---

### 187. Scenario Analysis

Advanced assessments can develop several scenarios:

```text
Expected Scenario
      ↓
AI performs as intended

Failure Scenario
      ↓
AI produces incorrect output

Misuse Scenario
      ↓
User applies AI outside approved purpose

Worst Credible Scenario
      ↓
Multiple safeguards fail
```

This provides a more complete view.

---

### 188. What-If Analysis

Useful questions include:

* What if the AI output is wrong?
* What if the data changes?
* What if users misuse the system?
* What if the vendor changes the model?
* What if human oversight fails?
* What if the system becomes unavailable?
* What if the system is used at ten times the expected scale?

These questions expose hidden assumptions.

---

### 189. Sensitivity Analysis

If an assessment uses assumptions or quantitative ratings, test how the conclusion changes when assumptions change.

For example:

```text
Expected Usage
    ↓
10,000 decisions/month
    ↓
Impact = Moderate

Expanded Usage
    ↓
500,000 decisions/month
    ↓
Impact = High
```

This shows how scale can change the assessment.

---

### 190. Scenario Thresholds

Organizations can define thresholds that trigger additional governance.

Example:

```text
Impact < Threshold
       ↓
Standard Controls

Impact ≥ Threshold
       ↓
Enhanced Review
```

Thresholds can be based on:

* Number of affected individuals.
* Decision significance.
* Severity.
* AI autonomy.
* Data sensitivity.
* Business criticality.

---

### 191. Impact Concentration

An assessment should consider whether impact is concentrated in particular populations.

For example:

> Most users experience minimal effects, but a smaller group experiences significantly greater consequences.

Average impact may therefore hide important outliers.

---

### 192. Outlier Analysis

An advanced assessment may ask:

> "Who experiences the worst outcome?"

rather than only:

> "What is the average outcome?"

This can identify material impacts that would otherwise disappear inside aggregate statistics.

---

### 193. Cumulative Exposure

The same individual may encounter multiple AI systems.

For example:

```text
AI System A
    ↓
Customer Profiling

AI System B
    ↓
Fraud Assessment

AI System C
    ↓
Service Prioritization
```

Each system may appear acceptable independently, while their combined effects may create greater consequences.

Enterprise governance should therefore consider **portfolio-level impact** where relevant.

---

### 194. Interconnected AI Systems

Modern organizations may use multiple AI systems that interact.

Example:

```text
AI Model A
     ↓
Data Platform
     ↓
AI Model B
     ↓
Business Workflow
     ↓
AI Agent
     ↓
Customer
```

Impact assessment should consider material dependencies between systems.

---

### 195. Third-Party Model Changes

A third-party AI provider may change:

* Model version.
* Model behavior.
* Processing location.
* Features.
* Safety mechanisms.
* Performance characteristics.

These changes may alter the organization's impact profile.

Therefore, material provider changes should be considered as reassessment triggers.

### 196. Impact Assessment and Procurement

AI impact assessment can be incorporated into procurement.

* What data will be processed?

* What evidence is available?
* How will changes be communicated?

This brings impact assessment into the AI lifecycle early.

### 197. Impact Assessment and Architecture

For example:

```text
      ↓
Large blast radius

      ↓
Potentially smaller blast radius
```

Similarly:

```text

```

may create greater impact potential than:

```text

```

Architecture should therefore be considered during assessment.

### 198. Impact Assessment and Security Architecture

Security controls can influence the magnitude of AI impacts.

Examples:

These controls can reduce the scope of harmful actions.

### 199. Impact Assessment and Business Continuity

Possible consequences:

### 205. Quality Assurance

Quality assurance can use sampling.

### 206. Common Advanced Failure

One common failure is **impact inflation**.

Teams may classify everything as high impact because they believe this demonstrates caution.

The result:

```text
Everything = High
       ↓
Nothing is differentiated
       ↓
Management loses prioritization
```

A mature methodology should differentiate impacts meaningfully.

---

### 207. Another Failure — Impact Underestimation

The opposite problem is:

> "The AI only recommends; therefore there is little impact."

This ignores the possibility that humans may heavily rely on recommendations.

The actual effect depends on the **decision pathway**, not simply the technical label assigned to the AI.

---

### 208. Another Failure — Control Confusion

Organizations may confuse:

> "A control exists"

with:

> "The control effectively reduces impact."

For example:

> "Human review is required."

does not prove that:

* Review occurs.
* Reviewers understand the output.
* Reviewers can challenge it.
* Reviewers have sufficient time.
* Reviewers actually override incorrect recommendations.

Control effectiveness must be evaluated.

---

### 209. Another Failure — Static Assessment

An assessment completed at deployment may become outdated.

For example:

```text
Initial System
   ↓
100 users
   ↓
Original Impact

Six Months Later
   ↓
10,000 users
   ↓
Different Impact Profile
```

The assessment should therefore be treated as a living governance artifact.

### 210. Advanced GRC Interview Answer

If asked:

> **"How would you conduct an AI impact assessment?"**

A strong answer is:

> "I would start by understanding the AI use case, intended purpose, scope, users, affected stakeholders, data, outputs, and the decisions or actions influenced by the system. I would then map the impact pathway from AI input and output through human or automated decision-making to the final consequence. I would identify both positive and negative impacts and assess their severity, scale, duration, reversibility, detectability, autonomy, and potential blast radius. I would evaluate existing safeguards, including meaningful human oversight, monitoring, containment, and remediation. Finally, I would document assumptions, evidence, findings, residual concerns, ownership, and management decisions, with defined reassessment triggers for material changes."

---

### 211. Advanced GRC Scenario

**Question:**

> "The business says an AI recruitment tool is low impact because a human makes the final decision. What would you do?"

A strong response:

> "I would not automatically accept that conclusion. I would examine how the AI output influences the recruiter, whether the recruiter independently reviews candidates, whether the recommendation can be challenged or overridden, and what happens when the AI is incorrect. I would also assess the severity, scale, duration, reversibility, and affected population. The existence of a human decision-maker is a control, but I would need to determine whether it provides meaningful oversight."

---

### 212. Advanced GRC Scenario — AI Customer Service

**Question:**

> "An AI assistant occasionally provides incorrect answers. How would you assess the impact?"

A strong response:

> "I would not assess the impact solely from the error rate. I would identify the types of incorrect answers, who receives them, what actions customers or employees may take as a result, how quickly errors are detected, whether human review exists, and whether the resulting consequences can be reversed. A low error rate can still create significant impact if the affected decisions are highly consequential."

---

### 213. Advanced GRC Scenario — Autonomous AI

**Question:**

> "An AI agent can automatically execute infrastructure changes. What would you focus on?"

A strong response:

> "I would focus on the AI's authority, permissions, blast radius, decision pathway, human oversight, containment, rollback capability, monitoring, and business continuity. I would identify credible failure scenarios and determine how quickly harmful actions could be detected and reversed. The assessment should consider not only the probability of an error but the potential consequence of autonomous action."

---

### 214. Advanced GRC Scenario — Vendor AI

**Question:**

> "The AI is provided by a major vendor. Does the organization still need an impact assessment?"

A strong response:

> "Yes. The vendor may provide technical controls and assurance information, but the organization remains responsible for understanding the impact of its own use case. I would assess how the organization uses the service, who is affected, what data and decisions are involved, what safeguards are available, and what dependencies or limitations exist."

---

### 215. Advanced GRC Scenario — Expanding AI Use

**Question:**

> "The business wants to use an existing low-impact AI tool for a new purpose. What would you do?"

A strong response:

> "I would treat the new purpose as a potential change to the approved use case. I would reassess the stakeholders, decisions influenced, data, autonomy, potential impacts, safeguards, and overall impact profile. I would not assume that the original assessment remains valid simply because the underlying technology has not changed."

### 222. Final Master Summary

AI impact assessment is a structured method for understanding the consequences of deploying and operating an AI system.

The mature approach goes beyond asking whether an AI model is accurate or secure.

It asks:

> **Who can be affected?**

> **How can they be affected?**

> **What happens when the AI output enters the real-world decision or action process?**

> **How severe, widespread, persistent, and reversible could the consequences be?**

> **What safeguards exist, and are they actually effective?**

> **Who is accountable for accepting the remaining exposure?**

The assessment therefore connects **AI technology to real-world consequences and organizational decision-making**.
