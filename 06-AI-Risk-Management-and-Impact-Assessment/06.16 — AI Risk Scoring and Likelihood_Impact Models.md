# 06.16 — AI Risk Scoring and Likelihood_Impact Models

## Part 1 — Foundations, Purpose, Principles, and AI Risk Scoring Concepts

---

## 1. Introduction

AI risk scoring converts identified AI risks into a structured representation that helps an organization prioritize attention, treatment, escalation, and governance decisions.

The basic concept is:

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Likelihood
   +
Impact
   ↓
Risk Score
   ↓
Risk Level
   ↓
Governance Decision
```

The purpose is **not** to create a mathematically perfect representation of risk.

The purpose is to create a:

* Consistent.
* Explainable.
* Repeatable.
* Comparable.
* Decision-useful.

method for evaluating AI risk.

---

# 2. What Is AI Risk Scoring?

**AI risk scoring** is the process of assigning a structured rating to an AI-related risk based on defined assessment criteria.

A common approach considers:

> **Likelihood × Impact = Risk Score**

For example:

```text
Likelihood = 4
Impact     = 5

Risk Score = 4 × 5
           = 20
```

The organization then maps the score to a risk level according to its approved methodology.

---

# 3. Why AI Risk Scoring Matters

AI systems can create many different types of risk.

For example:

* Security risk.
* Privacy risk.
* Bias risk.
* Compliance risk.
* Operational risk.
* Model risk.
* Reputational risk.
* Human-impact risk.
* Fundamental-rights risk.

Without a structured scoring method, organizations may struggle to determine:

> **Which AI risks require the greatest attention?**

---

# 4. Risk Scoring Is a Prioritization Mechanism

A risk score should help management answer:

* Which risks are most significant?
* Which risks need immediate treatment?
* Which risks can be monitored?
* Which risks require escalation?
* Which risks can potentially be accepted?
* Where should resources be allocated?

Therefore:

> **Risk scoring supports decisions; it does not replace professional judgment.**

---

# 5. Inherent AI Risk

**Inherent risk** represents the level of risk before considering the effect of controls.

Example:

```text id="n3x7v2"
AI System
   ↓
Potential Threat / Harm
   ↓
Inherent Likelihood
   +
Inherent Impact
   ↓
Inherent Risk
```

For example:

> An AI system makes high-impact automated decisions with no safeguards.

Its inherent risk may be high.

---

# 6. Residual AI Risk

**Residual risk** is the risk remaining after considering implemented controls.

Conceptually:

```text id="h7q2m8"
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

Example:

```text id="k5r9p3"
Inherent Risk = 20
Controls = Strong
Residual Risk = 8
```

The actual calculation method depends on the organization's methodology.

---

# 7. Likelihood

**Likelihood** represents the probability or expected frequency of a risk event occurring within a defined context and period.

For example:

| Score | Example Description |
| ----: | ------------------- |
|     1 | Rare                |
|     2 | Unlikely            |
|     3 | Possible            |
|     4 | Likely              |
|     5 | Almost certain      |

The organization should define these terms precisely.

---

# 8. Impact

**Impact** represents the severity of consequences if the AI risk materializes.

Potential impact dimensions include:

* Financial.
* Operational.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Regulatory.
* Reputational.
* Human.
* Social.
* Fundamental-rights impact.

---

# 9. AI Impact Is Multidimensional

Unlike some traditional IT risks, AI impact can extend beyond the organization.

Example:

```text id="p8c2m5"
AI Failure
   ├── Financial Loss
   ├── Operational Disruption
   ├── Privacy Harm
   ├── Customer Harm
   ├── Discrimination
   ├── Regulatory Exposure
   └── Reputational Damage
```

Therefore, impact assessment should not automatically rely on financial loss alone.

---

# 10. Likelihood × Impact Matrix

A common model uses a matrix.

Example:

| Likelihood \ Impact |  1 |  2 |  3 |  4 |  5 |
| ------------------- | -: | -: | -: | -: | -: |
| 5                   |  5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 |
| 4                   |  4 |  8 | 12 | 16 | 20 |
| 3                   |  3 |  6 |  9 | 12 | 15 |
| 2                   |  2 |  4 |  6 |  8 | 10 |
| 1                   |  1 |  2 |  3 |  4 |  5 |

The numerical score can then be mapped to organizational risk levels.

---

# 11. Example Risk Bands

One possible structure:

| Score | Risk Level |
| ----: | ---------- |
|   1–4 | Low        |
|   5–9 | Moderate   |
| 10–14 | High       |
| 15–25 | Critical   |

These ranges are **examples only**.

An organization should use its approved risk methodology rather than automatically adopting these thresholds.

---

# 12. The Importance of Defined Scales

A score has little meaning if the scoring criteria are vague.

For example:

> Likelihood = 4

is meaningless unless everyone understands what "4" represents.

A mature methodology defines:

* Description.
* Probability.
* Time horizon.
* Evidence requirements.
* Examples.

---

# 13. Likelihood Should Be Evidence-Based

Do not simply ask:

> "How likely does this feel?"

Instead consider:

* Historical incidents.
* Testing results.
* Model behavior.
* Threat intelligence.
* Data quality.
* Exposure.
* Frequency of use.
* Number of decisions.
* Control effectiveness.
* Environmental conditions.

---

# 14. Frequency of AI Decisions

Scale matters.

Suppose an AI system makes:

```text id="b5m8r2"
10 decisions / month
```

versus:

```text id="q3n7k1"
10 million decisions / month
```

Even a low probability of failure can create substantial aggregate exposure at very high volume.

---

# 15. Exposure

Likelihood can increase when an AI system has greater exposure.

Consider:

* Internet accessibility.
* Number of users.
* Number of transactions.
* Number of integrations.
* Geographic scope.
* Public accessibility.
* Data sensitivity.

Exposure is therefore an important input into likelihood analysis.

---

# 16. AI-Specific Likelihood Factors

AI risk likelihood may depend on:

### Data instability

Training or operational data changes frequently.

### Model complexity

Complex systems may be harder to predict or validate.

### External dependency

The system depends on third-party models or APIs.

### Human interaction

Human users can introduce misuse or errors.

### Attack surface

AI components may be exposed to adversarial activity.

### Deployment scale

The system operates across large populations.

---

# 17. Impact Should Reflect Consequences

Ask:

> **"What happens if this risk materializes?"**

Consider:

```text id="w2j8m4"
Who is affected?
       ↓
What happens?
       ↓
How severe?
       ↓
How many?
       ↓
How long?
       ↓
Can it be reversed?
```

---

# 18. Severity Versus Likelihood

A highly severe event can remain important even if unlikely.

Example:

```text id="x6p3v9"
Likelihood = 1
Impact     = 5
Score      = 5
```

A low-frequency event may still require special treatment because of its consequences.

This is why organizations may use:

* Risk appetite.
* Escalation rules.
* Minimum-impact thresholds.
* Special treatment for catastrophic events.

---

# 19. Low Probability Does Not Mean Low Importance

For AI systems, consider:

> **"What if the event is rare but catastrophic?"**

Examples may include:

* Large-scale privacy breach.
* Severe discriminatory outcome.
* Critical safety failure.
* Major regulatory violation.
* Large-scale autonomous error.

The organization should not allow a low likelihood rating to obscure severe consequences.

---

# 20. Risk Scoring Versus Risk Judgment

The score is an input into decision-making.

Example:

```text id="n8c4r7"
Score = 12
      ↓
High Risk
      ↓
Management Judgment
      ↓
Treatment / Acceptance / Escalation
```

The score does not automatically determine the final decision.

---

# 21. Qualitative Risk Scoring

Not every AI risk can be quantified precisely.

For example:

> "Potential long-term societal harm from an emerging generative AI capability."

There may not be enough historical data to calculate a reliable probability.

A qualitative approach may therefore use:

* Rare.
* Unlikely.
* Possible.
* Likely.
* Almost certain.

with documented rationale.

---

# 22. Quantitative Risk Scoring

Where reliable data exists, organizations may use quantitative estimates.

Examples:

* Probability of occurrence.
* Expected financial loss.
* Number of affected users.
* Expected downtime.
* Expected incident frequency.

For example:

```text id="r5m2x8"
Probability = 5%
Potential Loss = €1,000,000

Expected Loss = €50,000
```

This can be useful, but not every AI impact can be meaningfully reduced to monetary value.

---

# 23. Semi-Quantitative Models

Many GRC programs use a semi-quantitative approach.

Example:

```text id="g7v3n1"
Likelihood:
1–5

Impact:
1–5

Risk:
Likelihood × Impact
```

This provides structure without pretending that the underlying estimates are perfectly precise.

---

# 24. Avoid False Precision

Consider:

```text id="u4p8k2"
Risk Score = 17.35
```

This can create an illusion of accuracy if the underlying likelihood and impact judgments are subjective.

A better approach may be:

> High Risk — supported by documented evidence and assessment criteria.

The model should be as precise as the evidence allows.

---

# 25. AI Risk Scoring Should Be Consistent

Different assessors should ideally reach reasonably comparable conclusions when reviewing the same evidence.

This requires:

* Standard definitions.
* Standard scales.
* Scoring guidance.
* Examples.
* Calibration.
* Review.

---

# 26. Risk Scoring Calibration

Two assessors might evaluate the same AI system:

### Assessor A

Likelihood = 4

### Assessor B

Likelihood = 2

If both are using the same methodology, the difference should be explainable.

Calibration sessions can help align interpretation.

---

# 27. Risk Scoring Bias

Risk assessment itself can contain bias.

For example:

> A business owner may underestimate risk because high risk could delay their AI project.

Another assessor may overestimate risk because they are highly risk-averse.

Therefore, governance should establish:

* Scoring criteria.
* Evidence expectations.
* Independent challenge.
* Escalation.

---

# 28. Risk Owner Versus Risk Assessor

These roles should be distinguished.

### Risk owner

Accountable for managing the risk.

### Risk assessor

Performs or supports the assessment.

### GRC / Second Line

May challenge and oversee the assessment.

### Management

May accept risk within delegated authority.

This creates accountability.

---

# 29. Risk Scenario

A risk score should normally be associated with a specific scenario.

Weak:

> "AI risk = High."

Better:

> "The customer fraud model may incorrectly classify legitimate transactions, resulting in disproportionate account restrictions."

The second statement allows meaningful assessment.

---

# 30. Risk Statement Structure

A useful format is:

> **Because of [cause], [event] may occur, resulting in [impact].**

Example:

> Because training data contains underrepresented populations, the AI model may perform less accurately for certain users, resulting in unequal outcomes.

This creates a clear basis for scoring.

---

# 31. Cause → Event → Impact

A strong AI risk statement separates:

```text id="e4m9q2"
CAUSE
   ↓
RISK EVENT
   ↓
IMPACT
```

Example:

```text
Insufficient training data
        ↓
Poor subgroup performance
        ↓
Unequal AI decisions
        ↓
Customer harm / compliance exposure
```

This makes likelihood and impact easier to assess.

---

# 32. Multiple Impact Dimensions

An AI event can create several impacts.

Example:

```text id="p7x3m8"
AI Error
  ├── Financial
  ├── Operational
  ├── Privacy
  ├── Regulatory
  ├── Reputational
  └── Human
```

The methodology should define whether these are:

* Aggregated.
* Scored separately.
* Weighted.
* Evaluated using the highest-impact dimension.

---

# 33. Highest-Impact Method

One approach is to identify the highest material impact.

Example:

| Dimension   | Score |
| ----------- | ----: |
| Financial   |     3 |
| Operational |     2 |
| Privacy     |     4 |
| Human       |     5 |

Overall impact:

> 5 — based on the highest material consequence.

This is simple but may hide other dimensions.

---

# 34. Weighted Impact Method

Another approach assigns weights.

Example:

```text id="z8q4m1"
Financial      × 20%
Operational    × 15%
Privacy        × 20%
Regulatory     × 20%
Human Impact   × 25%
```

The weighted score produces an aggregate result.

This is more sophisticated but requires careful governance.

---

# 35. Multi-Criteria Risk Scoring

AI risk may be assessed across multiple dimensions:

```text id="c3n7p5"
Security
Privacy
Fairness
Compliance
Safety
Operational
Human Impact
Reputation
```

Each can receive a rating.

This creates a more comprehensive AI risk profile.

---

# 36. Risk Vector

Instead of reducing everything to one number:

```text id="m9v2k6"
AI Risk Profile

Security       → High
Privacy        → Moderate
Fairness       → High
Operational    → Low
Compliance     → High
```

This can provide management with greater insight.

A single score can then be used only for prioritization if needed.

---

# 37. Why One Number Can Be Misleading

Suppose two AI systems both score:

> Risk = 15

But:

### System A

* Security = Critical.
* Privacy = Low.

### System B

* Security = Low.
* Human impact = Critical.

The same overall score does not mean the risks are equivalent.

Therefore:

> **Always preserve the underlying risk dimensions.**

---

# 38. Risk Appetite Integration

Risk scoring becomes useful when connected to risk appetite.

Example:

```text id="h6r3p8"
Risk Score
    ↓
Risk Appetite
    ↓
Within Appetite?
   ├── Yes → Monitor / Manage
   └── No  → Treat / Escalate
```

Risk scoring without decision criteria has limited governance value.

---

# 39. Risk Tolerance

Risk appetite describes the broad level of exposure the organization is willing to accept.

Risk tolerance provides more specific boundaries.

Example:

> "High-impact AI systems must not operate with unresolved critical control failures."

This creates an actionable boundary.

---

# 40. AI Risk Thresholds

Thresholds may trigger:

* Additional review.
* Senior approval.
* Legal review.
* Security review.
* Privacy assessment.
* Independent validation.
* Deployment restriction.
* Risk committee escalation.

---

# 42. Risk Scoring and Governance Gates

Risk scoring can support lifecycle gates.

```text id="b8m4q1"
AI Proposal
     ↓
Initial Score
     ↓
Risk Gate
     ↓
Detailed Assessment
     ↓

     ↓
```

---

# 43. Initial Versus Detailed Assessment

An organization may use:

### Initial screening

Quickly classify AI systems.

### Detailed assessment

Perform deeper analysis for systems with greater risk.

This prevents low-risk AI from being subjected to the same governance burden as high-impact AI.

---

# 44. Dynamic AI Risk Scores

AI risk can change over time.

For example:

```text id="x1c7m9"
2026 Q1 → Moderate
2026 Q2 → High
2026 Q3 → Critical
```

Possible causes:

* New deployment.
* More users.
* New data.
* New model.
* New regulation.
* New attack.
* New incident.

Therefore, risk scores should not necessarily remain static.

---

# 45. Trigger-Based Reassessment

Reassess when:

* Model changes.
* Data changes.
* Purpose changes.
* Population changes.
* Geography changes.
* Vendor changes.
* Risk events occur.
* New vulnerabilities emerge.
* Regulatory requirements change.

---

# 46. Risk Score Aging

A risk score becomes less reliable as its evidence becomes outdated.

Example:

> Assessment performed in January.

By December:

* Model changed.
* Dataset changed.
* User base doubled.

The original score may no longer accurately represent the current risk.

---

# 47. Assessment Validity Period

Organizations may establish review periods such as:

* Annual.
* Semiannual.
* Quarterly.
* Event-driven.

The frequency should be proportionate to risk.

---

# 48. Risk Score Documentation

Every score should have a rationale.

Example:

```text id="q5n8m2"
Likelihood: 4

Rationale:
Model processes 2M transactions monthly,
has experienced three material false-positive
incidents, and relies on rapidly changing data.

Impact: 4

Rationale:
Incorrect decisions may restrict legitimate
customer transactions and create regulatory
and customer-impact exposure.
```

This makes the score auditable.

---

# 49. Evidence Supporting Likelihood

Possible evidence:

* Historical incidents.
* Testing.
* Failure rates.
* Threat intelligence.
* Exposure statistics.
* Operational data.
* Model monitoring.
* Expert judgment.

---

# 50. Evidence Supporting Impact

Possible evidence:

* Number of affected people.
* Financial exposure.
* Service criticality.
* Legal requirements.
* Privacy sensitivity.
* Human consequences.
* Business criticality.
* Reversibility.

---

# 51. Expert Judgment

AI risk assessment often requires expert judgment because historical data may be limited.

Expert judgment should be:

* Documented.
* Reasoned.
* Evidence-supported.
* Subject to challenge where appropriate.

It should not simply be:

> "The assessor feels this is high."

---

# 52. Uncertainty

AI risk assessments can contain significant uncertainty.

Examples:

* New technology.
* Limited historical data.
* Unknown model behavior.
* Emerging threats.
* Uncertain societal effects.

The assessment should document uncertainty rather than hiding it.

---

# 53. Confidence Level

Some organizations may record confidence.

Example:

| Risk          | Score | Confidence |
| ------------- | ----: | ---------- |
| Security      |    12 | High       |
| Privacy       |    15 | Medium     |
| Social impact |    16 | Low        |

This communicates that:

> **A risk score and confidence in that score are not the same thing.**

---

# 54. Conservative Scoring

Where evidence is limited, organizations may choose a conservative approach.

For example:

> If credible evidence cannot distinguish between Moderate and High impact, escalate for additional review rather than automatically selecting the lower category.

The organization's methodology should define when conservative treatment is appropriate.

---

# 55. Risk Scoring Governance Principle

A mature principle is:

> **Do not allow the scoring model to create false confidence.**

The score is a structured representation of judgment and evidence—not an objective measurement of reality.

# 58. Senior GRC Anchor

> **"I use AI risk scoring as a structured decision-support mechanism rather than treating the score as an absolute measurement of risk. I begin with a clearly defined risk scenario that separates the cause, risk event and potential impact. I then assess likelihood and impact using the organization's approved scales, supported by evidence such as historical incidents, testing, exposure, model behavior, affected populations, financial consequences, regulatory requirements and human impact. For AI, I make sure the impact assessment is multidimensional because a single risk event can create security, privacy, compliance, operational, reputational and human consequences. I distinguish inherent risk from residual risk after controls, preserve the underlying risk dimensions even when an aggregate score is used, and document the rationale and uncertainty behind the assessment. Finally, I connect the resulting risk level to risk appetite, escalation thresholds and governance authority so that the score leads to an appropriate management decision rather than becoming a number recorded in a risk register."**

# 06.16 — AI Risk Scoring and Likelihood–Impact Models

## Part 2 — Risk Scoring Methodology, AI-Specific Factors, Scenarios, and Control Integration

### 59. Risk Scoring Methodology

A risk-scoring methodology defines **how an organization consistently evaluates and prioritizes AI risks**.

A basic structure is:

```text
Risk Scenario
     ↓
Likelihood Assessment
     ↓
Impact Assessment
     ↓
Inherent Risk Score
     ↓
Control Assessment
     ↓
Residual Risk Score
     ↓
Risk Treatment
```

The methodology should be documented before assessments are performed.

---

# 60. Define the Scoring Scale First

Before assigning scores, define what each score means.

For example:

### Likelihood

| Score | Definition     |
| ----: | -------------- |
|     1 | Rare           |
|     2 | Unlikely       |
|     3 | Possible       |
|     4 | Likely         |
|     5 | Almost certain |

### Impact

| Score | Definition    |
| ----: | ------------- |
|     1 | Insignificant |
|     2 | Minor         |
|     3 | Moderate      |
|     4 | Major         |
|     5 | Severe        |

The organization should define these terms using objective criteria.

---

# 61. Likelihood Criteria

Likelihood can be based on:

* Historical frequency.
* Exposure.
* Number of transactions.
* Number of users.
* Attack probability.
* Model failure frequency.
* Data instability.
* Control maturity.
* Threat environment.

For example:

> A model processing millions of transactions with known recurring errors may have a higher likelihood of producing an adverse event than an AI system used occasionally for internal drafting.

---

# 62. Impact Criteria

Impact can consider:

### Financial

Potential financial loss.

### Operational

Service disruption or process failure.

### Regulatory

Potential regulatory consequences.

### Privacy

Potential harm involving personal data.

### Security

Confidentiality, integrity or availability consequences.

### Human

Potential harm to individuals.

### Reputational

Loss of trust or organizational reputation.

---

# 63. Human Impact Should Not Be Reduced to Money

A serious AI governance weakness is treating every consequence as a financial number.

For example:

> A discriminatory employment decision may cause serious individual harm even when the organization's direct financial loss is small.

Therefore:

> **Human and rights-related impact should be explicitly represented where relevant.**

---

A single AI system can have risks in multiple categories.

# 66. Risk Event Definition

Do not score a vague statement such as:

> "AI security risk."

Instead define a specific event:

> "An attacker manipulates an AI application's instructions, causing unauthorized disclosure of confidential information."

Now likelihood and impact can be evaluated meaningfully.

---

# 67. Risk Statement Template

Use:

> **Because [cause], [risk event] may occur, resulting in [impact].**

Example:

> Because the AI application accepts untrusted user input, prompt injection may cause unauthorized disclosure of confidential information, resulting in privacy, security and regulatory exposure.

---

# 68. Risk Scoring Example

Suppose:

* Likelihood = 4.
* Impact = 5.

Then:

```text id="p6m2x8"
4 × 5 = 20
```

If the organization's matrix defines 15–25 as Critical:

> **Risk = Critical**

The assessor should document why the likelihood is 4 and impact is 5.

---

# 69. Never Score Without Rationale

Weak:

> Likelihood = 4.

Strong:

> Likelihood = 4 because the application is externally accessible, processes approximately 500,000 interactions monthly, and has experienced repeated attempts to exploit similar weaknesses.

The second statement is auditable.

---

# 70. Inherent Risk Example

Consider an AI credit-decision system.

Potential risk:

> Incorrect model output results in inappropriate credit decisions.

Before controls:

```text id="c7v4m2"
Likelihood = 4
Impact     = 5

Inherent Risk = 20
```

The organization therefore considers the risk Critical.

---

# 71. Control Assessment

Now consider controls:

* Data-quality validation.
* Model validation.
* Fairness testing.
* Human review.
* Access controls.
* Monitoring.
* Appeals.

The controls may reduce either:

* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Or both.

---

# 72. Residual Risk

After controls:

```text id="n8q3r6"
Likelihood = 2
Impact     = 5

Residual Risk = 10
```

The risk has reduced from 20 to 10.

But the impact remains severe.

This distinction matters.

---

# 73. Controls Do Not Always Reduce Impact

Suppose:

> A catastrophic AI failure would affect thousands of customers.

Adding preventive controls may reduce the probability from Likely to Unlikely.

But if the failure still occurs, the consequences may remain severe.

Therefore:

```text id="x4m9p1"
Likelihood ↓
Impact → unchanged
```

can be completely reasonable.

---

# 74. Preventive Controls and Likelihood

Preventive controls commonly reduce likelihood.

Examples:

* Secure development.
* Input validation.
* Data validation.
* Access controls.
* Model testing.
* Human approval.

---

# 75. Detective Controls

Detective controls may reduce the duration or extent of impact.

Examples:

* Monitoring.
* Alerts.
* Anomaly detection.
* Bias monitoring.
* Incident detection.

---

# 76. Corrective Controls

Corrective controls can reduce consequences after an event.

Examples:

* Rollback.
* Model suspension.
* Reprocessing.
* Customer remediation.

# 118. Senior GRC Anchor

> **"When I score AI risk, I first make sure the risk scenario is specific enough to evaluate, separating the cause, event and consequence. I apply the organization's approved likelihood and impact scales and support each rating with evidence rather than subjective intuition. For AI, I consider not only financial and operational consequences but also security, privacy, regulatory, fairness, human and potentially fundamental-rights impacts. I calculate or determine the inherent risk before controls, then evaluate whether the controls are actually designed and operating effectively before determining residual risk. I also consider AI-specific factors such as automation level, human oversight, model and data drift, third-party dependencies, concentration risk and the scale of AI decisions. Finally, I connect the residual risk to risk appetite, treatment options, escalation authority and monitoring requirements. The important point is that the risk score should drive a governance action—it should not simply become another number in the risk register."**

# 06.16 — AI Risk Scoring and Likelihood–Impact Models

## Part 3 — Advanced Risk Analysis, Scoring Challenges, Validation, and Governance

### 119. Risk Scoring Is a Decision Model

AI risk scoring should be understood as a **decision model**.

It converts complex information into a structured basis for deciding:

* What requires attention.
* What requires treatment.
* What requires escalation.
* What can be accepted.
* What requires additional evidence.
* What should not proceed.

The score itself is not the objective.

```text
Evidence
   ↓
Risk Analysis
   ↓
Risk Score
   ↓
Decision
   ↓
Action
```

---

# 120. The Difference Between Risk and Uncertainty

These concepts should not be confused.

### Risk

There is enough information to estimate the possibility and consequence of an event.

### Uncertainty

The available information is insufficient to confidently estimate what may happen.

AI systems can contain significant uncertainty because:

* The technology may be new.
* Historical data may be limited.
* Model behavior may be difficult to predict.
* Threats may be emerging.
* The population may change.
* The consequences may be difficult to quantify.

Therefore:

> **Uncertainty should be documented rather than hidden inside a numerical score.**

---

# 121. Uncertainty Should Influence Governance

Suppose two systems both receive:

> Risk Score = 15

But:

### System A

The score is supported by extensive historical evidence.

### System B

The score is based largely on expert judgment because there is little historical evidence.

The governance response should not necessarily be identical.

System B may require:

* Additional testing.
* Conservative assumptions.
* Pilot deployment.
* Enhanced monitoring.
* Independent review.

---

# 122. Confidence in the Assessment

A useful supplementary field is:

> **Assessment confidence**

For example:

| Risk                     | Score | Confidence |
| ------------------------ | ----: | ---------- |
| Security                 |    16 | High       |
| Privacy                  |    12 | High       |
| Bias                     |    15 | Medium     |
| Emerging societal impact |    15 | Low        |

This helps management distinguish:

> **high risk**

from:

> **high uncertainty about risk.**

---

# 123. Risk Scoring Under Limited Data

New AI systems may have no meaningful historical incident data.

In that situation, consider:

* Expert judgment.
* Testing.
* Threat modeling.
* Scenario analysis.
* Analogous systems.
* Vendor evidence.
* Pilot results.
* Conservative assumptions.

Do not fabricate precision where evidence does not exist.

---

# 124. Scenario Analysis

Scenario analysis can be useful for emerging AI risks.

Example:

### Scenario A — Expected

AI operates normally.

### Scenario B — Degraded

Model performance deteriorates.

### Scenario C — Adverse

AI produces materially harmful outcomes.

### Scenario D — Severe

Failure produces large-scale consequences.

Then assess:

```text

   ↓
Likelihood
   +

   ↓
```

For complex AI systems, consider:

```text

    ↓

    ↓

```

The purpose is not to predict the future precisely.

It is to understand the range of plausible outcomes.

# 126. Stress Testing

AI systems may also be stress-tested.

Examples:

* Unusual input.
* High transaction volume.
* Data distribution changes.
* Adversarial input.
* Missing data.
* Extreme conditions.
* Unexpected user behavior.

Stress testing can provide evidence for likelihood and impact assessment.

# 127. Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis asks:

> **"What happens to the risk score if our assumptions change?"**

Example:

```text
Likelihood = 3
Impact = 5
Risk = 15
```

If likelihood changes to 4:

```text
4 × 5 = 20
```

The risk moves from one governance category to another.

This indicates that the assessment may be sensitive to likelihood assumptions.

---

# 128. Sensitivity to Impact

Similarly:

```text
Likelihood = 4
Impact = 4
Risk = 16
```

If impact increases to 5:

```text
4 × 5 = 20
```

This may trigger a higher governance threshold.

```text
├── Third-party model dependency

```

This tells management **why** the risk is high.

# 130. Top Risk Drivers

A useful risk report may identify:

1. Decision consequence.
2. Exposure.
3. Control weakness.
4. Model uncertainty.
5. Data quality.

# 131. Risk Decomposition

Break a complex AI risk into components.

# 132. Risk Aggregation Versus Risk Decomposition

Break one complex risk into its components.

### Aggregation

Both are important.

# 138. Risk Velocity

Another useful concept is **risk velocity**.

Risk velocity asks:

> **How quickly could the impact materialize after the risk event begins?**

Example:

### Slow velocity

AI bias develops over several months.

### Fast velocity

An autonomous AI system makes thousands of incorrect transactions within minutes.

Fast-moving risks may require:

* Faster detection.
* Stronger automated controls.
* Lower escalation thresholds.
* More frequent monitoring.

---

# 139. Risk Persistence

Risk persistence asks:

> **How long can the consequences continue?**

Example:

```text
Short-lived
AI recommendation error
        ↓
Corrected immediately

Long-lived
Incorrect automated decision
        ↓
Historical records affected
        ↓
Long-term consequences
```

Persistence can influence impact.

---

# 140. Reversibility

Ask:

> **Can the consequence be reversed?**

### Highly reversible

Incorrect recommendation corrected immediately.

### Less reversible

Incorrect decision permanently affects an individual.

### Difficult to reverse

AI-generated information causes irreversible physical or societal consequences.

Lower reversibility generally increases the seriousness of potential impact.

---

# 141. Scale

AI can operate at enormous scale.

Consider:

* Number of decisions.
* Number of affected individuals.
* Geographic coverage.
* Transaction volume.
* Frequency.

A small error rate can still produce substantial harm.

Example:

```text
1% error rate
×
10,000,000 decisions
=
100,000 potentially incorrect outcomes
```

The absolute number of affected cases matters.

---

# 142. Speed

AI can also operate rapidly.

A human may make:

> 100 decisions per day.

An automated system may make:

> 100,000 decisions per hour.

This changes the risk profile because errors can propagate much faster.

---

# 143. Automation and Scale Together

Consider:

```text
High Automation
      +
High Volume
      +
High Consequence
      ↓
Potentially High AI Risk
```

These characteristics should be explicitly considered in assessment.

---

# 144. Blast Radius

**Blast radius** describes the extent of potential impact if the AI system fails.

Ask:

* How many systems depend on it?
* How many people can be affected?
* How many business processes use it?
* How many locations are involved?

A centralized AI service may have a much larger blast radius than an isolated internal tool.

---

# 145. Containment

Risk impact can sometimes be reduced through containment.

Examples:

* Transaction limits.
* Permission restrictions.
* Sandbox environments.
* Human approval.
* Geographic restrictions.
* Rate limits.
* Segmented deployment.

Containment controls can reduce the potential scale of harm.

---

# 146. Fail-Safe Design

For high-risk AI systems, consider:

> **What happens when the AI is uncertain or fails?**

Possible responses:

```text
AI Uncertain
    ↓
Stop
OR
Escalate
OR
Require Human Review
OR
Use Safe Default
```

Fail-safe design can significantly reduce consequences.

---

# 147. Fail-Open Versus Fail-Closed

### Fail-open

System continues operating when a control fails.

### Fail-closed

System restricts or stops operation when a critical control fails.

The appropriate approach depends on the use case.

For a high-consequence AI decision system, fail-safe behavior may be particularly important.

---

# 148. Confidence Scores

AI systems may provide confidence scores.

But:

> **Model confidence does not automatically equal correctness.**

A model can be highly confident and still be wrong.

Therefore, confidence information should not automatically be treated as sufficient risk evidence.

---

# 149. Threshold Governance

Decision thresholds can significantly affect risk.

Example:

```text
Threshold = 0.50
```

versus:

```text
Threshold = 0.80
```

Changing the threshold can alter:

* False positives.
* False negatives.
* Selection rates.
* Customer impact.
* Operational workload.

Threshold changes should therefore be governed.

---

# 150. Risk-Based Threshold Setting

A threshold should be evaluated against:

* Business objective.
* Error consequences.
* Fairness.
* Human review capacity.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Customer impact.

There is rarely a universally "correct" threshold.

---

# 151. Model Performance Is Not Risk

A model with 99% accuracy is not automatically low risk.

Suppose:

```text
Accuracy = 99%
```

but:

> The 1% error affects high-consequence decisions.

The risk could still be significant.

Therefore:

> **Accuracy is a performance measure, not a complete risk assessment.**

---

# 152. Error Cost

Different errors may have different consequences.

Example:

### False positive

Legitimate customer is flagged.

### False negative

Fraudulent transaction is missed.

If the second event has substantially greater consequences, the risk model should recognize that difference.

---

# 153. Asymmetric Risk

AI risks can be asymmetric.

For example:

```text
False Positive
→ €10 cost

False Negative
→ €100,000 cost
```

Even if both occur at the same frequency, their impact differs substantially.

Therefore, impact assessment should consider **error type**, not only overall error rate.

---

# 154. Risk-Based Model Validation

Validation should focus on the risks that matter most.

For example:

A high-impact credit model may require deeper validation of:

* Accuracy.
* Fairness.
* Stability.
* Explainability.
* Data quality.
* Thresholds.

A low-impact internal writing assistant may require much less extensive validation.

---

# 155. Independent Challenge

High-risk AI assessments should receive appropriate challenge.

Possible reviewers:

* GRC.
* Risk.
* Compliance.
* Privacy.
* Security.
* Internal audit.
* Independent model validation.

The goal is not to obstruct the AI project.

The goal is to challenge assumptions and strengthen decision quality.

# 156. Risk Assessment Quality Review

A reviewer can ask:

### Risk definition

Is the risk specific?

### Likelihood

Is the rating supported?

### Impact

Are all relevant consequences considered?

### Controls

Do controls actually address the risk?

### Residual risk

Is it realistic?

### Evidence

Can the assessment be defended?

Does the treatment match the risk?

A strong GRC assessor should ask:

# 171. Common Risk Scoring Mistakes

### Mistake 1 — Vague risk statements

"AI risk is high."

### Mistake 2 — Unsupported scores

"Likelihood = 4" with no rationale.

### Mistake 3 — Ignoring human impact

Only financial consequences are considered.

### Mistake 4 — Treating accuracy as risk

High accuracy is assumed to mean low risk.

### Mistake 5 — Ignoring scale

Small error rates are considered harmless despite millions of decisions.

### Mistake 6 — Ignoring uncertainty

A precise number is assigned despite weak evidence.

### Mistake 7 — Static scoring

The risk is never reassessed.

### Mistake 8 — Control presence equals effectiveness

A documented policy is assumed to reduce risk.

### Mistake 9 — Blind aggregation

Scores are simply added together.

### Mistake 10 — No governance action

High risk is recorded but nothing changes.

# 227. Interview Question

### "How do you assess AI risk?"

A strong answer:

> "I start by defining the AI use case and a specific risk scenario rather than assigning a generic AI risk rating. I identify the cause, risk event and potential consequences, then assess likelihood and impact using the organization's approved methodology. For AI, I consider security, privacy, compliance, fairness, human impact, scale, automation, model uncertainty and third-party dependencies. I determine inherent risk first, map and evaluate the effectiveness of controls, and then determine residual risk. I compare the residual exposure with risk appetite and escalation thresholds, assign ownership and treatment actions, and establish monitoring indicators. Finally, I reassess the risk when there are material changes, incidents or new evidence."

---

# 228. Interview Question

### "Why shouldn't we rely only on a 5×5 risk matrix?"

Answer:

> "A 5×5 matrix is useful for consistency and prioritization, but it can oversimplify AI risk. AI can create multidimensional consequences involving privacy, security, fairness, regulatory exposure and human impact. Two systems can have the same numerical score but very different risk profiles. I therefore use the matrix as a decision-support mechanism while preserving the underlying risk dimensions, evidence, uncertainty, control effectiveness and business context."

---

# 229. Interview Question

### "What is the difference between inherent and residual AI risk?"

Answer:

> "Inherent risk is the exposure before considering controls, while residual risk is the exposure remaining after considering the design and operating effectiveness of controls. For example, an AI system might have high inherent risk because of sensitive data and high-impact decisions. Strong authentication, data minimization, human review and monitoring may reduce the likelihood of an adverse event, resulting in a lower residual risk. However, the impact may remain high because the consequences, if the event occurs, have not fundamentally changed."

---

# 230. Interview Question

### "What if the business disagrees with your risk score?"

Answer:

> "I would first understand the basis for the disagreement and compare both assessments against the approved scoring criteria and available evidence. I would challenge assumptions objectively rather than treating my assessment as automatically correct. If uncertainty remains material, I would document it and escalate according to the governance process. The objective is a defensible and evidence-based assessment, not simply winning an argument over a number."

---

# 231. Interview Question

### "What if the risk is low likelihood but catastrophic impact?"

Answer:

> "I would not allow the low likelihood rating to automatically make the risk acceptable. I would examine the organization's risk appetite, minimum-impact thresholds, scenario analysis, reversibility, blast radius and potential human or regulatory consequences. If the impact is potentially catastrophic, additional controls or escalation may be appropriate even where the probability is low."

---

# 232. Interview Question

### "How do you ensure risk scores remain accurate?"

Answer:

> "I treat the assessment as dynamic. I establish review periods and event-driven triggers such as material model changes, data changes, incidents, new vulnerabilities, changes in the affected population or regulatory developments. I also use KRIs and control indicators to provide new evidence. When material conditions change, I reassess the likelihood, impact and control effectiveness rather than relying on the original score."

---

# 233. Interview Question

### "How do you handle uncertainty in AI risk?"

Answer:

> "I explicitly document uncertainty rather than hiding it inside a precise numerical score. Where historical evidence is limited, I use expert judgment, scenario analysis, stress testing, analogous systems and conservative assumptions where appropriate. I also record confidence in the assessment and may require enhanced monitoring, additional testing or independent review when uncertainty is significant."

---

# 234. Interview Question

### "How does AI risk scoring support governance?"

Answer:

> "The score creates a consistent basis for prioritization, but the governance value comes from connecting the score to action. Risk levels can determine assessment depth, required controls, monitoring frequency, approval authority and escalation. I therefore make sure every material risk assessment leads to a clear treatment, acceptance or escalation decision with an accountable owner."

---

# 235. Interview Question

### "What is your role as a GRC professional?"

Answer:

> "My role is to provide structure, challenge and oversight rather than simply produce risk scores. I help ensure that risks are properly defined, assessments are evidence-based, controls are mapped and tested, residual risk is understood and decisions are made within approved risk appetite and authority. I also maintain traceability so management and auditors can understand how the conclusion was reached."

---

# 236. Executive Anchor

Memorize this:

> **"I don't treat AI risk scoring as a mathematical exercise. I use it as a structured decision-support mechanism. I start with a specific AI risk scenario, assess likelihood and multidimensional impact using evidence, determine inherent risk, evaluate control effectiveness and calculate or determine residual risk. I then consider uncertainty, scale, automation, blast radius, reversibility and risk velocity because these factors can materially change the practical exposure. Finally, I compare the residual risk with appetite and governance thresholds and translate the result into treatment, acceptance or escalation. I also establish KRIs and reassessment triggers so that the score remains current as the AI system and its environment change. The key objective is not to produce a number—it is to produce a defensible governance decision."**

# 238. 06.16 — Final Takeaway

The complete 06.16 topic can be reduced to **five questions**:

### 1. What can go wrong?

**Risk scenario.**

### 2. How likely and how severe is it?

**Likelihood + Impact.**

### 3. What are we doing about it?

**Controls and treatment.**

### 4. What risk remains?

**Residual risk + uncertainty.**

### 5. What should management do?

**Accept, reduce, avoid or escalate.**

The strongest GRC professional does not stop at:

> **"The risk score is 15."**

They continue:

> **"Here is why it is 15, here is the evidence, here are the controls, here is the residual exposure, here is the uncertainty, here is whether it is within appetite, and here is the management action required."**

That is the core of **AI risk scoring as a governance discipline**.
