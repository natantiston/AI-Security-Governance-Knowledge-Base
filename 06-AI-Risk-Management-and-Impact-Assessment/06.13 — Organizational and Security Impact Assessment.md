# 06.13 — Organizational and Security Impact Assessment

## Part 1 — Foundations, Purpose, Scope, and Core Concepts

### 01. Introduction

Organizational and Security Impact Assessment evaluates how the adoption, operation, modification, failure, misuse, or compromise of an AI system could affect an organization's:

* Business operations
* Information assets
* Security posture
* People and processes
* Technology environment
* Resilience
* Regulatory obligations
* Reputation
* Financial position
* Strategic objectives

The central GRC question is:

> **"What could happen to the organization and its security environment because of this AI system, and what level of protection and governance is required?"**

This assessment extends beyond asking whether an AI model is technically secure.

It examines the **organizational consequences of using AI**.

---

# 02. Why Organizational Impact Matters

AI systems increasingly become embedded in business processes.

An AI system may:

* Make recommendations.
* Process sensitive information.
* Automate operational activities.
* Interact with customers.
* Support employees.
* Control workflows.
* Generate business decisions.
* Connect to enterprise systems.
* Influence security operations.

As AI becomes more deeply integrated, a failure may affect more than the model itself.

For example:

```text
AI Failure
    ↓
Incorrect Output
    ↓
Business Process Disruption
    ↓
Operational Impact
    ↓
Financial / Customer Impact
    ↓
Organizational Consequences
```

Therefore, AI risk assessment should consider the **business environment surrounding the AI**.

---

# 03. Why Security Impact Matters

AI introduces security considerations that may differ from traditional applications.

Potential issues include:

* Sensitive data exposure
* Unauthorized model access
* Prompt injection
* Data poisoning
* Model manipulation
* Excessive permissions
* Insecure integrations
* Supply-chain compromise
* Unauthorized AI-generated actions
* Model theft
* Information leakage
* Abuse of AI capabilities

The security impact assessment asks:

> **"If this AI system is attacked, manipulated, misused, or compromised, what could happen?"**

---

# 04. Organizational Impact Versus Security Impact

These are related but distinct.

### Organizational impact

Focuses on consequences to the organization.

Examples:

* Revenue loss
* Service disruption
* Productivity loss
* Customer impact
* Regulatory exposure
* Reputation damage
* Strategic disruption

### Security impact

Focuses on consequences arising from security compromise or security weaknesses.

Examples:

* Confidentiality loss
* Integrity compromise
* Availability disruption
* Unauthorized access
* Data leakage
* Account compromise
* System manipulation

A mature assessment considers both.

---

# 05. The CIA Triad

The traditional security foundation remains relevant.

```text
          CONFIDENTIALITY
                 ▲
                 │
                 │
INTEGRITY ◄──────┼──────► AVAILABILITY
```

### Confidentiality

Could unauthorized parties gain access to AI-related information?

### Integrity

Could AI inputs, models, outputs, or decisions be manipulated?

### Availability

Could AI services become unavailable when required?

These security consequences must then be translated into **business impact**.

---

# 06. Security Impact Is Not Just a Technical Score

Suppose an AI API becomes unavailable.

Technically:

> Service unavailable.

Organizationally:

> Customer-service operations cannot process requests.

Business consequence:

> Customer response times increase.

Potential consequence:

> Service-level commitments may be missed.

Therefore:

```text
Technical Failure
       ↓
Security / Technology Impact
       ↓
Business Impact
       ↓
Organizational Consequence
```

This translation is central to GRC.

---

# 07. AI System Criticality

Not every AI system deserves the same level of security governance.

A useful question is:

> **"How critical is this AI system to the organization's objectives and operations?"**

Possible categories:

### Low criticality

AI assists with low-impact administrative tasks.

### Moderate criticality

AI supports important business processes.

### High criticality

AI supports essential operational activities.

### Critical

AI is deeply integrated into functions where failure could create severe organizational consequences.

Criticality should drive proportional controls.

---

# 08. Business Process Dependency

An important assessment dimension is:

> **How dependent is the business process on the AI system?**

Consider:

```text
AI Optional
    ↓
AI Helpful
    ↓
AI Important
    ↓
AI Operationally Required
    ↓
AI Mission-Critical
```

The greater the dependency, the greater the potential organizational impact of failure.

---

# 09. AI Dependency Analysis

Ask:

* Can the business operate without the AI?
* For how long?
* Is there a manual process?
* Is there an alternative system?
* How quickly can operations recover?
* Does staff retain the necessary expertise?
* Can the AI be safely disabled?

These questions help establish the organization's resilience.

---

# 10. Single Point of Failure

An AI platform may become a single point of failure.

For example:

```text
Customer Requests
       ↓
AI Service
       ↓
All Customer Responses
```

If the AI service fails:

```text
AI Failure
    ↓
Customer Service Disruption
```

The organization should determine whether alternative processing exists.

---

# 11. Business Impact Analysis

A Business Impact Analysis, or BIA, identifies the consequences of disruption to business processes.

For AI governance, the BIA perspective can help determine:

* Critical processes.
* Dependencies.
* Maximum tolerable disruption.
* Recovery objectives.
* Resource requirements.
* Business consequences.

AI impact assessment can therefore be connected to existing business continuity practices.

---

# 12. Recovery Time Objective

The **Recovery Time Objective (RTO)** represents the target time within which a process or service should be restored following disruption.

For an AI-enabled process:

> "How quickly must the AI capability or an acceptable alternative be restored?"

Example:

```text
Critical AI Service
RTO = 1 hour

Non-critical AI Tool
RTO = 2 business days
```

The actual target should be determined by the organization's business requirements.

---

# 13. Recovery Point Objective

The **Recovery Point Objective (RPO)** concerns the amount of data loss that can be tolerated.

For AI systems, this may include:

* Training data
* Configuration
* Model versions
* Prompt configurations
* Knowledge bases
* Audit records
* Decision logs

Example:

> If the AI platform is restored from backup, how much recent information can the organization afford to lose?

# 16. Third-Party Dependency

A key question is:

> **"What happens to our organization if the AI provider changes, fails, is compromised, or becomes unavailable?"**

Consider:

* Provider outage.
* Provider termination.
* Contract changes.
* Model changes.
* Pricing changes.
* Service degradation.
* Security incident.
* Regulatory restrictions.

These are organizational impact considerations.

# 17. Data Dependency

AI systems often depend heavily on data.

Potential data dependencies include:

* Customer data
* Employee data
* Operational data
* Security data
* Financial data
* Knowledge repositories
* Logs
* External datasets

If critical data becomes unavailable or corrupted, AI performance may degrade.

# 18. Data Integrity Impact

Consider:

```text
Incorrect Data
      ↓
AI Processes Data
      ↓
Incorrect Output
      ↓
Business Decision
      ↓
Organizational Impact
```

Therefore, data integrity is not merely a data-quality issue.

For critical AI systems, it can become a security and business continuity issue.

---

# 19. Model Integrity

Model integrity concerns whether the AI model itself has been altered or compromised.

Potential scenarios include:

* Unauthorized model modification.
* Malicious model replacement.
* Compromised model artifact.
* Tampered model configuration.
* Unauthorized fine-tuning.

Potential consequence:

> The organization unknowingly operates an AI system that produces manipulated outputs.

---

# 20. Output Integrity

Even if the model itself has not been compromised, outputs can potentially be manipulated through:

* Malicious prompts.
* Adversarial inputs.
* Context manipulation.
* Data poisoning.
* Instruction manipulation.

Therefore, the assessment should distinguish:

```text
Model Integrity
       +
Input Integrity
       +
Output Integrity
```

---

# 21. AI Access Control

Access should be assessed across multiple layers.

```text
User
 ↓
Application
 ↓
AI API
 ↓
Model
 ↓
Data
 ↓
Tools / Systems
```

Questions include:

* Who can access the AI?
* Who can modify it?
* Who can change prompts?
* Who can change system instructions?
* Who can access training data?
* Who can approve integrations?
* Who can invoke high-impact actions?

---

# 22. Privilege and AI Agents

AI agents can introduce additional organizational impact because they may have permission to act.

For example:

```text
AI
 ↓
Email
 ↓
Database
 ↓
Ticketing System
 ↓
Business Application
```

An AI system with excessive permissions can create significantly greater consequences than an AI system that only generates text.

Therefore:

> **AI capability should be assessed together with AI authority.**

---

# 23. Least Privilege

A fundamental security principle is:

> **Give AI only the permissions necessary to perform its authorized function.**

For example:

```text
AI Assistant
     ↓
Read customer records
     ✓

Delete customer records
     ✗
```

Where possible, permissions should be:

* Limited.
* Purpose-specific.
* Time-bound where appropriate.
* Monitored.
* Revocable.

---

# 24. Segregation of Duties

AI governance should consider separation of responsibilities.

For example:

```text
AI Developer
      ≠
AI Approver
      ≠
Production Administrator
```

This reduces the risk that one individual can independently develop, approve, deploy, and modify a consequential AI capability without oversight.

---

# 25. Authentication

AI-related interfaces should use appropriate authentication mechanisms.

Potential components include:

* Strong identity verification.
* Multi-factor authentication.
* Service identities.
* API authentication.
* Role-based access control.
* Privileged-access management.

Authentication requirements should reflect the sensitivity and criticality of the AI system.

---

# 26. Authorization

Authentication answers:

> **Who are you?**

Authorization answers:

> **What are you allowed to do?**

For AI systems, authorization may apply to:

* Model access.
* Data access.
* Prompt configuration.
* Tool use.
* Deployment.
* Model changes.
* Administrative functions.

---

# 27. Logging and Accountability

AI security governance should provide appropriate logging.

Potential records include:

* User identity.
* AI request.
* Relevant input.
* Model/version.
* Tool invocation.
* Output.
* Administrative changes.
* Access events.
* Security events.

Logging supports:

* Investigation.
* Accountability.
* Incident response.
* Audit.
* Forensics.

---

# 28. Security Monitoring

AI systems should be monitored according to their risk.

Potential monitoring areas include:

* Unauthorized access.

* Suspicious prompts.
* Abnormal outputs.
* Data leakage.

# 29. Prompt Injection

Prompt injection is an AI-specific security concern.

Conceptually:

```text
Trusted Instruction
        +
Untrusted Input
        ↓
AI interprets malicious instruction
        ↓
Unexpected behavior
```

The organizational assessment should ask:

> **"What could happen to the business if an attacker successfully manipulates the AI through its inputs?"**

---

# 30. Tool-Use Risk

The risk increases when AI can interact with external systems.

For example:

```text
User
 ↓
AI
 ↓
Tool
 ↓
Production System
```

A manipulated AI response could potentially lead to an unauthorized action.

Therefore, organizations should consider:

* Tool authorization.
* Approval requirements.
* Action limits.
* Transaction thresholds.
* Monitoring.
* Rollback.

# 39. AI Incident Response

Organizations should determine how AI-related incidents enter existing incident-management processes.

Potential incident categories include:

* AI security compromise.
* Data leakage.
* Model manipulation.
* Unauthorized AI action.
* Malicious prompt exploitation.
* AI service outage.
* Significant erroneous behavior.

---

# 40. Incident Response Questions

Ask:

1. How is the incident detected?
2. Who owns response?
3. Can the AI system be isolated?
4. Can access be revoked?
5. Can the model be rolled back?
6. Can affected actions be reversed?
7. Who must be notified?
8. How are affected people protected?
9. What evidence must be preserved?
10. What determines return to service?

---

# 41. Containment

A critical security capability is the ability to contain an AI incident.

Possible actions:

```text

 ↓

 ↓
Activate Manual Process
 ↓
Investigate
```

The exact response depends on the system.

# 42. Recovery

### Organizational recovery

# 43. Business Continuity

AI should be incorporated into business continuity planning when it supports important processes.

Questions include:

* What happens if AI is unavailable?
* Can humans perform the function?
* Is a backup AI available?
* Can another provider be used?
* How long can the organization operate manually?

---

# 44. Manual Fallback

A manual fallback is one of the most important resilience controls for consequential AI.

Example:

```text
AI unavailable
      ↓
Manual process activated
      ↓
Business continues
      ↓
AI investigated
      ↓
Controlled restoration
```

However, the fallback should be tested.

# 45. Organizational Resilience

> "The AI service can be restored."

> **"The organization can continue delivering its required function despite AI disruption."**

This distinction is important.

# 52. Gross Impact Versus Residual Impact

An important GRC distinction is:

### Gross impact

Potential consequence before considering controls.

### Residual impact

Potential consequence after considering existing controls.

Conceptually:

```text
Potential Impact
      ↓
Existing Controls
      ↓
Residual Exposure
```

This distinction supports management decision-making.

---

# 53. Inherent Organizational Exposure

Before controls, ask:

> **"If this AI system failed or were compromised, what could happen?"**

This establishes the inherent exposure.

Do not allow existing controls to hide the underlying consequence.

---

# 54. Control-Adjusted Exposure

Then ask:

> **"Given the controls currently implemented, what exposure remains?"**

This creates a clearer governance picture.

Example:

```text
Inherent Impact: High
        ↓
Strong Preventive Controls
        ↓
Effective Detection
        ↓
Manual Fallback
        ↓
Residual Impact: Moderate
```

The exact rating should follow the organization's methodology.

---

# 55. Criticality and Security Requirements

Higher criticality generally requires stronger controls.

For example:

```text
Low Criticality
   ↓
Baseline Security

Medium Criticality
   ↓
Enhanced Security

High Criticality
   ↓
Strong Security + Resilience

Critical
   ↓
Defense-in-Depth + Continuous Oversight
```

This supports proportionality.

# 57. Core GRC Questions

For any AI system, ask:

### Business

> What business process depends on this AI?

### Criticality

> How important is that process?

### Security

> What could compromise the AI?

### Consequence

> What happens if the AI fails or is compromised?

### Resilience

> Can the organization continue without it?

### Control

> What safeguards exist?

### Accountability

> Who owns the risk?

### Evidence

> How do we know the controls work?

# 63. Advanced GRC Perspective

The most important mindset is:

> **Do not ask only "Can the AI be attacked?" Ask "What would the organization lose if the AI were attacked?"**

Likewise:

> **Do not ask only "Can the AI fail?" Ask "What business process fails with it?"**

And:

> **Do not ask only "Is the model secure?" Ask "Is the organization's dependency on the model resilient?"**

Remember these foundational principles:

1. Organizational impact is broader than technical impact.
2. Security impact should be translated into business consequences.
3. AI criticality should influence security requirements.
4. Business-process dependency is a key impact factor.
5. AI ecosystems include models, data, APIs, infrastructure, vendors, and users.
6. Confidentiality, integrity, and availability remain foundational.
7. AI agents can increase security impact through action authority.
8. Least privilege applies to AI systems.
9. Logging supports accountability and incident response.
10. AI incidents should connect to organizational incident management.
11. Business continuity should account for critical AI dependencies.
12. Manual fallback can reduce organizational exposure.
13. Third-party AI introduces supply-chain dependency.
14. Data integrity can become an organizational risk.
15. Model integrity and output integrity should be distinguished.
16. Shadow AI can create unmanaged organizational exposure.
17. Gross and residual exposure should be distinguished.
18. Higher criticality generally requires stronger resilience and security controls.
19. Organizational impact should consider operational, financial, regulatory, customer, strategic, and reputational consequences.
20. The assessment should ultimately support a governance decision.
