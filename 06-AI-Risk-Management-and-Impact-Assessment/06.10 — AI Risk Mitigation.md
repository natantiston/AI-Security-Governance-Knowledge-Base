# 06.10 — AI Risk Mitigation

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Risk Mitigation

### 1. Introduction to AI Risk Mitigation

AI risk mitigation is the process of **reducing the likelihood and/or impact of identified AI risks through appropriate controls and safeguards**.

The fundamental objective is not necessarily to eliminate risk completely.

Instead, mitigation seeks to bring risk to a level that the organization can reasonably manage and operate within.

A simple model is:

```text
AI Activity
     ↓
Risk Identified
     ↓
Risk Assessment
     ↓
Mitigation Measures
     ↓
Reduced Risk
     ↓
Ongoing Monitoring
```

---

### 2. What Is AI Risk Mitigation?

AI risk mitigation involves implementing measures that reduce the potential negative consequences associated with an AI system.

For example, an AI system may have a risk of producing incorrect outputs.

Possible mitigation measures include:

* Human review.
* Testing.
* Validation.
* Monitoring.
* Accuracy thresholds.
* Restricted use cases.
* Escalation procedures.

The objective is to reduce the risk to an acceptable level.

---

### 3. Mitigation Does Not Mean Elimination

One of the most important concepts is:

> **Mitigation reduces risk; it does not necessarily eliminate it.**

For example:

```text
Initial Risk
    ↓
Control
    ↓
Lower Risk
    ↓
Residual Risk
```

Some level of residual risk may remain after controls are implemented.

---

### 4. Why AI Risk Requires Specific Mitigation

AI systems can introduce characteristics that traditional IT systems may not have to the same degree.

Examples include:

* Probabilistic outputs.
* Model uncertainty.
* Data dependency.
* Model drift.
* Unexpected behavior.
* Bias.
* Hallucination.
* Adversarial manipulation.
* Automation risk.
* Explainability limitations.

Therefore, traditional cybersecurity controls alone may not address every AI-specific risk.

---

### 5. AI Risk Mitigation in GRC

From a GRC perspective, mitigation connects:

```text
Risk
 ↓
Control
 ↓
Control Owner
 ↓
Evidence
 ↓
Testing
 ↓
Residual Risk
```

This provides a structured way to demonstrate that identified AI risks are being actively managed.

---

### 6. Risk-Based Mitigation

Mitigation should be proportional to the level of risk.

A low-risk AI application may require relatively simple controls.

A high-risk AI application may require:

* Formal approval.
* Extensive testing.
* Human oversight.
* Strong access controls.
* Continuous monitoring.
* Independent assessment.
* Incident response.
* Periodic reassessment.

The principle is:

> **Higher risk generally requires stronger and more comprehensive mitigation.**

---

### 7. Inherent Risk

Before mitigation, the organization should understand the **inherent risk**.

Inherent risk represents the level of risk associated with the AI activity before considering the effectiveness of controls.

For example:

```text
AI Use Case
     ↓
Threat + Vulnerability + Exposure
     ↓
Inherent Risk
```

This establishes the baseline against which mitigation effectiveness can be evaluated.

---

### 8. Control Selection

Once the risk is understood, appropriate controls can be selected.

A GRC professional should ask:

* What causes the risk?
* What could happen?
* Who or what could be affected?
* Can likelihood be reduced?
* Can impact be reduced?
* What controls already exist?
* What additional controls are required?

---

### 9. Likelihood Reduction

Some mitigation controls primarily reduce the probability of an adverse event.

Examples:

* Access controls.
* Security testing.
* Input validation.
* Model testing.
* User training.
* Data validation.
* Configuration controls.

Conceptually:

```text
Likelihood
    ↓
Preventive Controls
    ↓
Lower Likelihood
```

---

### 10. Impact Reduction

Other controls primarily reduce the consequences if an event occurs.

Examples:

* Human intervention.
* Transaction limits.
* Backup systems.
* Incident response.
* Fail-safe mechanisms.
* Rollback capabilities.
* Containment.

Conceptually:

```text
Risk Event
    ↓
Impact-Limiting Controls
    ↓
Lower Impact
```

---

### 11. Reducing Both Likelihood and Impact

Some controls can reduce both.

For example, restricting an AI system's privileges can:

* Reduce the probability of unauthorized actions.
* Reduce the potential damage if the AI behaves incorrectly.

This makes access control particularly important for AI systems with operational capabilities.

---

### 12. Preventive Controls

Preventive controls attempt to stop an undesirable event before it occurs.

Examples include:

* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Input filtering.
* Data validation.
* Secure configuration.
* Access restrictions.
* Approved-model requirements.
* Environment isolation.

A simplified structure:

```text
Potential Risk
      ↓
Preventive Control
      ↓
Risk Event Prevented
```

---

### 13. Detective Controls

Detective controls identify problems after or while they occur.

Examples:

* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* Anomaly detection.
* Model-performance monitoring.
* Security alerts.
* Audit reviews.
* Data-quality monitoring.

Example:

```text
AI Activity
     ↓
Monitoring
     ↓
Abnormal Behavior Detected
```

Detection allows the organization to respond before the issue becomes more severe.

---

### 14. Corrective Controls

Corrective controls address a problem after detection.

Examples:

* Model rollback.
* Configuration correction.
* Access removal.
* Data correction.
* Incident remediation.
* Model retraining.
* System isolation.

Corrective controls help restore the system to an acceptable operating condition.

---

### 15. Compensating Controls

Sometimes the preferred control cannot be implemented.

A **compensating control** provides an alternative mechanism for reducing the risk.

For example:

> If automated model validation is unavailable, additional human review may provide an alternative safeguard.

The compensating control should still provide sufficient risk reduction for the specific situation.

---

AI mitigation controls can generally be considered across several dimensions:

### 17. Governance Controls

Governance controls establish organizational expectations.

Examples:

* AI policy.
* AI standards.
* Risk criteria.
* Approval requirements.
* Roles and responsibilities.
* AI inventory requirements.
* Risk assessment procedures.
* Exception procedures.

Governance establishes **what the organization expects**.

---

### 18. Security Controls

Security controls address threats against AI systems and their surrounding infrastructure.

Examples:

* Identity and access management.
* Network security.
* Encryption.
* Vulnerability management.
* Secure configuration.
* Endpoint security.
* Secrets management.
* Security monitoring.

These controls help protect the AI system and its supporting environment.

---

### 19. Data Controls

AI systems depend heavily on data.

Data-related mitigation may include:

* Data classification.
* Data quality controls.
* Data validation.
* Data minimization.
* Access restrictions.
* Encryption.
* Data lineage.
* Retention controls.

Poor-quality or inappropriate data can produce significant AI risk.

---

### 20. Model Controls

Model-level mitigation can include:

* Model testing.
* Validation.
* Performance evaluation.
* Robustness testing.
* Bias testing.
* Security testing.
* Version control.
* Model documentation.

The objective is to understand and control model behavior within its intended operating context.

---

### 21. Application Controls

The AI application itself may require controls such as:

* Input validation.
* Output validation.
* Business-rule enforcement.
* Rate limiting.
* Authorization.
* Transaction limits.
* Human approval.
* Workflow controls.

These controls help prevent AI outputs from directly producing uncontrolled consequences.

---

### 22. Human Controls

Human involvement can be an important mitigation mechanism.

Examples include:

* Human review.
* Human approval.
* Escalation.
* User training.
* Specialist oversight.
* Manual override.

However, human involvement should be meaningful rather than merely procedural.

---

### 23. Human Oversight as a Control

Consider:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Approve / Reject
       ↓
Business Action
```

The human reviewer should have sufficient:

* Authority.
* Knowledge.
* Time.
* Context.
* Ability to intervene.

Otherwise, the control may not be effective.

---

### 24. Operational Controls

AI systems require operational safeguards.

Examples include:

* Change management.
* Backup.
* Recovery.
* Capacity management.
* Availability monitoring.
* Configuration management.
* Incident management.
* Business continuity.

Operational controls reduce the risk that AI failures disrupt business operations.

---

### 25. Third-Party Controls

When AI services are provided by external organizations, mitigation should extend to third-party risk management.

Examples:

* Vendor due diligence.
* Contractual requirements.
* Security assessments.
* Service-level requirements.
* Data-processing requirements.
* Incident notification obligations.
* Change notification.

The organization should understand what risks remain outside its direct control.

---

### 26. Layered AI Risk Mitigation

A mature AI control environment uses multiple layers.

For example:

```text
Policy
   ↓
Identity
   ↓
Access Control
   ↓
Data Protection
   ↓
Model Controls
   ↓

   ↓
```

If one control fails, another layer may reduce the consequences.

---

### 27. Defense in Depth

This approach is commonly described as **defense in depth**.

The principle is:

> Do not rely on a single control to manage a significant AI risk.

For example, protecting sensitive information may involve:

```text
Classification
      +
Access Control
      +
DLP
      +
Encryption
      +
Monitoring
      +
Incident Response
```

---

### 28. Control Effectiveness

Having a control documented does not mean the risk has been adequately mitigated.

GRC should distinguish between:

**Control existence**

> Does the control exist?

and:

**Control effectiveness**

> Does the control actually reduce the intended risk?

For example:

> A policy prohibiting sensitive data uploads to an AI service exists, but employees can still upload the information without technical restriction.

The policy exists, but its effectiveness may be limited.

---

### 29. Design Effectiveness

A control must first be appropriately designed.

Questions include:

* Does the control address the identified risk?
* Is its scope appropriate?
* Is the control sufficiently specific?
* Is the control assigned to the correct owner?

A poorly designed control may fail even if it is consistently executed.

---

### 30. Operating Effectiveness

A properly designed control must also operate consistently.

Questions include:

* Is it actually implemented?
* Is it executed as required?
* Is it performed consistently?
* Is there evidence?
* Are exceptions identified?

This distinction is important in audits and control assessments.

---

### 31. Control Gap Analysis

GRC can compare:

```text
Required Control
       ↓
Existing Control
       ↓
Gap
       ↓
Remediation
```

Example:

**Requirement**

> AI outputs used for high-impact decisions require documented human review.

**Current state**

> Human review is informal and undocumented.

**Gap**

> Insufficient evidence of meaningful oversight.

**Mitigation**

> Implement a documented approval workflow.

---

### 32. AI Risk Treatment Plan

A mitigation plan should define:

* Risk.
* Risk owner.
* Treatment.
* Control.
* Control owner.
* Target date.
* Status.
* Expected residual risk.
* Evidence.

Example:

| Risk                   | Mitigation       | Owner          | Status      |
| ---------------------- | ---------------- | -------------- | ----------- |
| Incorrect AI output    | Human validation | Business Owner | In Progress |
| Unauthorized AI access | RBAC             | Security       | Implemented |
| Poor data quality      | Data validation  | Data Owner     | Planned     |

---

### 33. Control Ownership

Every important mitigation should have a clear owner.

For example:

```text
Risk
 ↓
Control
 ↓
Control Owner
 ↓
Evidence
```

Without ownership, mitigation activities can become ambiguous and difficult to enforce.

---

### 34. Risk Owner vs Control Owner

These roles are not necessarily the same.

**Risk owner**

> Accountable for managing the risk.

**Control owner**

> Responsible for implementing and operating a particular control.

One risk may have several controls and therefore several control owners.

---

### 35. Prioritizing Mitigation

Organizations often have limited resources.

Therefore, mitigation should prioritize the most significant risks.

A practical prioritization model considers:

* Risk severity.
* Business criticality.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Likelihood.
* Potential impact.
* Control weakness.
* Exploitability.
* Time sensitivity.

---

### 36. Risk-Based Resource Allocation

For example:

```text
Critical AI Risk
      ↓
Immediate Mitigation

High AI Risk
      ↓
Priority Remediation

Moderate AI Risk
      ↓
Planned Treatment

Low AI Risk
      ↓
Routine Monitoring
```

This prevents resources from being distributed equally regardless of risk.

---

### 37. Mitigation Cost

Risk mitigation has a cost.

Costs may include:

* Technology.
* Personnel.
* Training.
* Testing.
* Monitoring.
* Vendor services.
* Operational overhead.

The organization should consider whether the cost of mitigation is proportionate to the risk.

---

### 38. Cost-Benefit Consideration

A simplified decision model is:

```text
Risk Exposure
      ↓
Mitigation Cost
      ↓
Expected Risk Reduction
      ↓
Business Value
      ↓
Management Decision
```

This does not mean organizations should ignore serious risks because mitigation is expensive.

Some controls may be required regardless of cost because of legal, regulatory, safety, or organizational requirements.

---

### 39. AI Risk Mitigation and Risk Appetite

After controls are applied, the organization evaluates the remaining risk.

Conceptually:

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Mitigation Controls
      ↓
Residual Risk
      ↓
Compare With Risk Appetite
```

If residual risk is within the organization's defined tolerance, the activity may continue subject to monitoring and governance.

---

### 40. Continuous Risk Mitigation

AI risk mitigation should not be treated as a one-time project.

AI environments change through:

* Model updates.
* Data changes.
* New threats.
* New users.
* New integrations.
* New regulations.
* Provider changes.
* Changes in business purpose.

Therefore, mitigation must be periodically reassessed.

---

### 41. AI Lifecycle Perspective

Mitigation should exist throughout the AI lifecycle.

```text
Planning
   ↓
Design
   ↓
Development
   ↓
Testing
   ↓
Deployment
   ↓
Operation
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Change
   ↓
Retirement
```

Controls may need to change as the system progresses through these stages.

---

### 42. Mitigation During Planning

Before development begins, organizations can mitigate risk by:

* Defining acceptable use.
* Establishing requirements.
* Identifying risk owners.
* Selecting appropriate architecture.
* Defining data boundaries.
* Establishing security requirements.

Early mitigation is often more efficient than correcting problems after deployment.

---

### 43. Mitigation During Development

During development, mitigation may include:

* Secure coding.
* Data validation.
* Model testing.
* Access control.
* Dependency management.
* Security testing.
* Documentation.

This embeds risk management into the development process.

---

### 44. Mitigation During Testing

Testing can identify weaknesses before deployment.

Examples:

* Functional testing.
* Security testing.
* Robustness testing.
* Performance testing.
* Data-quality testing.
* Adversarial testing.
* Scenario testing.

The exact testing approach should reflect the AI system's risk profile.

---

### 45. Mitigation During Deployment

Deployment controls can include:

* Controlled rollout.
* Approval gates.
* Environment separation.
* Access restrictions.
* Monitoring.
* Logging.
* Rollback capability.

A controlled deployment reduces the consequences of unexpected behavior.

---

### 46. Mitigation During Operation

Operational mitigation includes:

* Continuous monitoring.
* Incident detection.
* Performance monitoring.
* Access reviews.
* Model evaluation.
* Change management.
* Periodic risk assessment.

This helps identify emerging problems.

---

### 47. Mitigation During Change

A material change may require reassessment.

Examples:

* New model version.
* New data source.
* New AI capability.
* New external integration.
* New user group.
* New geography.
* Increased autonomy.

The organization should determine whether existing controls remain adequate.

---

### 48. Mitigation During Retirement

Even retirement can introduce risk.

The organization may need to manage:

* Data deletion.
* Access removal.
* API deactivation.
* Vendor termination.
* Record retention.
* Dependency removal.

This ensures that risk does not remain after the AI system is no longer actively used.

---

### 49. Risk Mitigation Documentation

Good documentation should explain:

> **What risk are we mitigating, how are we mitigating it, who is responsible, and how do we know the mitigation works?**

This creates accountability and auditability.

---

### 50. The GRC Perspective

A GRC professional should not simply say:

> "Implement more controls."

A stronger approach is:

> "Identify the specific risk scenario, determine the cause and potential impact, evaluate existing controls, identify the control gap, select proportionate mitigation measures, assign ownership, establish evidence requirements, and reassess the resulting residual risk."

This demonstrates mature risk-management thinking.
