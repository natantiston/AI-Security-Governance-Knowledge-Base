# 06.20 — AI Risk Assessment Methodology

## Part 1 — Introduction, Purpose, Scope, and Core Concepts

AI governance becomes difficult when an organization knows it needs to "manage AI risk" but does not have a **repeatable method** for determining what the risk actually is.

That is the purpose of an **AI Risk Assessment Methodology**.

The methodology converts a broad question:

> **"Is this AI system safe and acceptable?"**

into a structured GRC process:

> **What is the AI system? → What can go wrong? → How significant is the risk? → What controls exist? → What risk remains? → What decision is required?**

---

# 1. What Is an AI Risk Assessment Methodology?

An AI Risk Assessment Methodology is a defined approach for:

* Identifying AI risks.
* Analyzing those risks.
* Evaluating their significance.
* Assessing existing controls.
* Determining residual risk.
* Defining treatment.
* Establishing approval and escalation.
* Supporting ongoing monitoring.

In simple terms:

> **It provides a consistent way to determine how risky an AI system is and what the organization should do about that risk.**

---

# 2. Why Organizations Need a Methodology

Without a standardized methodology, different teams may assess identical risks differently.

For example:

### Team A

> "The AI risk is Low."

### Team B

> "The AI risk is High."

Both may be using subjective judgment.

A methodology introduces:

* Consistency.
* Repeatability.
* Comparability.
* Accountability.
* Evidence.
* Defensible decisions.

---

# 3. AI Risk Assessment Is Not Just a Risk Score

One of the most important principles is:

> **The objective is not to produce a number. The objective is to support a risk decision.**

For example:

```text id="w1v9z7"
Risk Score = 16
```

is meaningless by itself.

A GRC professional needs to understand:

* What creates the risk?
* What could happen?
* Who could be affected?
* Why is the score 16?
* What controls exist?
* How effective are they?
* What risk remains?
* Is the remaining risk acceptable?

---

# 5. The Difference Between Inherent and Residual Risk

This distinction is fundamental.

## Inherent Risk

The level of risk **before considering controls**.

Example:

> An AI agent has permission to modify customer records.

Potential inherent risk:

**High**

---

## Residual Risk

The risk remaining **after considering controls**.

For example:

* Strong authentication.
* Authorization.
* Transaction limits.
* Human approval.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.

Residual risk may become:

**Moderate**

---

# 6. Simple Example

Consider an AI-powered financial approval system.

### Inherent risk

High.

### Controls

* Human approval.
* Segregation of duties.
* Transaction limits.
* Audit logging.
* Access control.

### Residual risk

Moderate.

Then compare:

```text id="z2l4m5"
Residual Risk
     ↓
Moderate
     ↓
Risk Appetite
     ↓
Moderate
     ↓
Within Appetite
```

The organization may permit the system to operate.

AI risk assessment shares many traditional GRC concepts:

* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Threats.
* Vulnerabilities.
* Controls.
* Residual risk.
* Risk appetite.

However, AI can introduce additional considerations such as:

* Model behavior.
* Data quality.
* Model uncertainty.
* Hallucination.
* Bias.
* Explainability.
* AI autonomy.
* Prompt manipulation.

Therefore:

> **An AI risk methodology should integrate with enterprise risk management while incorporating AI-specific risk characteristics.**

# 9. Assess the AI System in Context

Suppose an organization uses the same language model in two scenarios.

### Scenario A

AI summarizes public documents.

### Scenario B

AI recommends whether a customer should receive credit.

The underlying model may be identical.

But the **risk context is completely different**.

Therefore:

> **Risk belongs to the use case and operating context, not simply to the model itself.**

> **What is the AI system intended to do?**

The intended purpose establishes the assessment context.

# 18. AI Risk Statement

Use a structured format:

> **Because [cause], [risk event] may occur, resulting in [consequence].**

Example:

> Because the AI agent has broad access to customer systems, unauthorized or incorrect tool execution may occur, resulting in customer-data modification or disclosure.

This gives the risk assessment a clear structure.

---

# 19. Risk Cause

The cause describes:

> **Why could the event occur?**

Examples:

* Excessive permissions.
* Poor data quality.
* Insufficient testing.
* Weak authentication.
* Inadequate monitoring.
* Model limitations.

---

# 20. Risk Event

The event describes:

> **What could happen?**

Examples:

* AI produces incorrect output.
* AI discloses sensitive information.
* AI performs an unauthorized action.
* AI becomes unavailable.
* AI makes a biased recommendation.

---

# 21. Risk Consequence

The consequence describes:

> **What happens if the event occurs?**

Potential consequences:

* Financial loss.
* Privacy impact.
* Security incident.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Operational disruption.
* Customer harm.
* Reputational damage.

---

# 22. Example Risk Construction

Weak:

> "Hallucination."

Better:

> "The AI may generate inaccurate information."

Strong:

> "Because the AI system can generate unsupported information, users may rely on incorrect outputs when making customer-service decisions, resulting in incorrect advice, customer dissatisfaction and potential operational or financial impact."

The final version provides a much better basis for assessment.

---

# 23. Likelihood

Likelihood represents:

> **How probable is the risk event?**

A simple scale might be:

| Score | Likelihood     |
| ----: | -------------- |
|     1 | Rare           |
|     2 | Unlikely       |
|     3 | Possible       |
|     4 | Likely         |
|     5 | Almost Certain |

The exact definitions should be established by the organization's enterprise risk methodology.

---

# 24. Impact

Impact represents:

> **How severe would the consequences be?**

A simple scale:

| Score | Impact        |
| ----: | ------------- |
|     1 | Insignificant |
|     2 | Minor         |
|     3 | Moderate      |
|     4 | Major         |
|     5 | Severe        |

Again, the organization should define the actual thresholds.

---

# 25. Basic Risk Calculation

A common approach is:

**Risk = Likelihood × Impact**

Example:

```text id="q1v0cs"
Likelihood = 4
Impact = 5

Risk = 4 × 5
     = 20
```

This would normally represent a high or critical risk depending on the organization's risk matrix.

---

# 26. Risk Matrix

A simple matrix:

| Likelihood ↓ / Impact → |  1 |  2 |  3 |  4 |  5 |
| ----------------------- | -: | -: | -: | -: | -: |
| 5                       |  5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 |
| 4                       |  4 |  8 | 12 | 16 | 20 |
| 3                       |  3 |  6 |  9 | 12 | 15 |
| 2                       |  2 |  4 |  6 |  8 | 10 |
| 1                       |  1 |  2 |  3 |  4 |  5 |

The organization's methodology determines how these values translate into:

* Low.
* Moderate.
* High.
* Critical.

---

# 27. Why AI Risk Scoring Can Be Difficult

AI risk can involve significant uncertainty.

For example:

> How likely is an AI hallucination to cause actual customer harm?

The answer may not be precise.

Therefore:

> **Risk scoring should be supported by evidence, assumptions and expert judgment.**

Do not create false precision.

---

# 28. Confidence and Uncertainty

A mature assessment can document:

* Evidence available.
* Evidence missing.
* Assumptions.
* Uncertainty.
* Confidence level.

Example:

> Likelihood = 3, based on limited production evidence.

This is more transparent than presenting "3" as an absolute fact.

---

# 29. Existing Controls

After determining inherent risk, identify current controls.

Examples:

* Access control.
* Encryption.
* Data minimization.
* Human approval.
* Model validation.
* Security testing.
* Monitoring.
* Logging.
* DLP.
* Vendor controls.

Do not assume a control is effective merely because it exists.

---

# 30. Control Effectiveness

Ask two questions:

### Design

> Is the control appropriately designed?

### Operation

> Does it actually work?

Example:

> Policy requires human approval.

But testing shows:

> 20% of transactions bypass approval.

Therefore:

> The control exists but may not be operating effectively.

---

# 31. Residual Risk Assessment

After considering controls:

> **What risk remains?**

Example:

```text id="8qj1ne"
Inherent Risk
     ↓
High
     ↓
Controls
     ↓
Effective
     ↓
Residual Risk
     ↓
Moderate
```

The residual risk becomes the basis for the management decision.

---

# 32. Risk Treatment

If residual risk is too high, determine treatment.

Potential options:

* Avoid.
* Reduce.
* Share.
* Accept.

Risk treatment should be documented.

---

# 33. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance should identify:

* Risk.
* Residual level.
* Rationale.
* Risk owner.
* Approver.
* Date.
* Review period.

Example:

> "Residual risk is Moderate and within approved risk appetite. The business owner accepts the risk subject to quarterly monitoring."

---

# 34. Risk Escalation

If:

```text id="j9y4w1"
Residual Risk = High
Risk Appetite = Moderate
```

then:

> **The risk is above appetite.**

The assessment should trigger:

* Additional treatment.
* Scope reduction.
* Redesign.
* Escalation.
* Potential non-approval.

# 38. Risk Assessment Quality

A high-quality assessment should be:

### Consistent

Different assessors produce reasonably comparable results.

### Evidence-based

Conclusions have supporting information.

### Traceable

Risks connect to controls and decisions.

### Repeatable

Another assessor can follow the same process.

### Defensible

Management can explain why the decision was made.

---

# 39. Common AI Risk Assessment Mistakes

### Mistake 1

> Treating the AI model as the entire risk.

The use case and context matter.

### Mistake 2

> Focusing only on cybersecurity.

AI risk is broader.

### Mistake 3

> Giving every AI system the same assessment.

Risk should be proportionate.

### Mistake 4

> Treating a questionnaire as the assessment.

The questionnaire collects information; professional analysis creates the assessment.

### Mistake 5

> Assuming controls are effective because they exist.

Effectiveness must be validated.

---

# 40. Another Major Mistake — Risk Score Without Rationale

Bad:

> Risk = 15.

Better:

> Likelihood = 3 because testing identified occasional failures under defined conditions; impact = 5 because failure could affect a large customer population. Therefore inherent risk = 15.

The rationale makes the score defensible.

| Define use case | A/R      | C   | C             | C       | C     |
| Identify risks  | R        | A/C | C             | C       | C     |
| Assess security | C        | C   | A/R           | C       | C     |

**R = Responsible**
**C = Consulted**

The exact RACI should reflect organizational structure.

# 51. Senior GRC Interview Answer

If the interviewer asks:

> **"Walk me through your AI risk assessment methodology."**

A strong answer is:

> **"I start by defining the AI system and its intended business purpose, including the data, users, stakeholders, dependencies and level of autonomy. I then establish the risk context and identify the potential AI, cybersecurity, privacy, operational, compliance and third-party risks.**
>
> **I analyze each material risk based on likelihood and impact using the organization's approved methodology, while documenting the evidence and assumptions behind the rating. I then assess the existing controls, including their design and operating effectiveness, to determine residual risk.**
>
> **I compare the residual risk with the organization's risk appetite and determine whether the risk should be accepted, treated, reduced, shared or escalated. Finally, I document the decision, assign ownership, establish monitoring requirements and define reassessment triggers for material changes, incidents, new data, model changes or other events that could alter the risk profile."**

---

# 52. The Most Important Principle

The entire chapter can be reduced to:

> **Do not assess AI in isolation. Assess the AI system, its purpose, its environment, its data, its users, its autonomy, its dependencies, its controls and the consequences of failure.**

Then ask:

> **"Is the remaining risk acceptable to the organization?"**

That is the central question of an effective **AI Risk Assessment Methodology**.


# 06.20 — AI Risk Assessment Methodology

## Part 2 — AI Risk Identification, Risk Taxonomy, Scenarios, and Assessment Techniques

Part 1 established the **overall AI Risk Assessment Methodology**.

Part 2 goes deeper into the most important practical skill:

> **How do we systematically identify the risks that an AI system creates?**

A strong AI risk assessment does not begin with a generic checklist. It begins by understanding the **AI system, its use case, data, architecture, decision authority, stakeholders, dependencies, and potential consequences**.

The core sequence is:

**SYSTEM → USE CASE → ASSETS → THREATS → VULNERABILITIES → RISK EVENTS → CONSEQUENCES → RISK**

---

# 53. Risk Identification

Risk identification answers:

> **"What could go wrong?"**

For AI, this question should be asked across multiple dimensions.

For example:

```text id="5qf8zj"
                 AI SYSTEM
                     ↓
       ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
       ↓             ↓             ↓
     DATA          MODEL         PEOPLE
       ↓             ↓             ↓
    SECURITY      RELIABILITY     MISUSE
       ↓             ↓             ↓
       └─────────────┼─────────────┘
                     ↓
                RISK EVENTS
                     ↓
                CONSEQUENCES
```

# 54. Do Not Start With "What Controls Do We Have?"

A weak assessment starts with:

> "Do we have MFA?"

> "Do we have encryption?"

> "Do we have logging?"

That is a **control assessment**, not risk identification.

Start with:

> **What can happen?**

Then determine:

> **What controls should address it?**

Suppose an organization assesses only:

But ignores:

# 59. Use-Case Analysis

The same AI technology can have very different risks depending on how it is used.

Example:

### Use Case A

Generate meeting summaries.

### Use Case B

Recommend employee promotions.

### Use Case C

Approve financial transactions.

The underlying technology may be similar.

The risk profiles are not.

# 60. Decision Authority

A critical question is:

> **What authority does the AI have?**

Consider:

### Informational

AI provides information.

### Advisory

AI recommends an action.

### Semi-automated

AI initiates an action subject to controls.

### Autonomous

AI independently executes actions.

Generally:

> **Greater decision authority requires greater risk scrutiny.**

---

# 61. Human Dependency

Also assess how much humans depend on AI outputs.

For example:

```text id="j7o0bx"
AI Output
   ↓
Human Independently Verifies
   ↓
Decision
```

versus:

```text id="y0q2mi"
AI Output
   ↓
Human Automatically Accepts
   ↓
Decision
```

The second creates greater **automation-bias and overreliance risk**.

---

# 62. Automation Bias

Automation bias occurs when users place excessive trust in automated recommendations.

Example:

> A customer-service employee accepts an AI-generated answer without checking the customer's actual account information.

Potential consequence:

> Incorrect information is provided to the customer.

Therefore, human oversight should be assessed for **quality**, not merely existence.

# 68. Hallucination Risk

A hallucination occurs when an AI system produces information that appears plausible but is unsupported or incorrect.

The GRC question is not simply:

> "Can the model hallucinate?"

Most generative AI systems can produce incorrect outputs.

The important question is:

> **"What happens if users rely on the incorrect output?"**

---

# 69. Hallucination Impact Depends on Context

Example:

### Low consequence

AI creates an incorrect draft of an internal brainstorming note.

### Higher consequence

AI generates incorrect financial guidance.

### Potentially severe consequence

AI produces incorrect information that directly influences a high-impact decision.

Therefore:

> **The risk is determined by consequence, not merely model behavior.**

---

# 70. Reliability Risk

Reliability asks:

> Can the AI system consistently perform its intended function?

Consider:

* Availability.
* Accuracy.
* Stability.
* Performance.

# 80. Threat vs. Risk

These terms should not be confused.

### Threat

Something capable of causing harm.

Example:

> Malicious user.

### Vulnerability

A weakness that can be exploited or lead to failure.

Example:

> AI agent has excessive permissions.

### Risk event

What actually could happen.

Example:

> Agent performs an unauthorized transaction.

### Consequence

What happens afterward.

Example:

> Financial loss.

# 111. Advanced Interview Question

### "How is AI risk different from cybersecurity risk?"

Strong answer:

> **"Cybersecurity is an important component of AI risk, but AI risk is broader. Cybersecurity focuses heavily on protecting confidentiality, integrity and availability, while AI risk may also involve model reliability, inaccurate outputs, human overreliance, data quality, explainability, autonomy, accountability and other consequences arising from how AI behaves and is used. I would therefore integrate AI security into the enterprise cybersecurity framework while maintaining a broader AI risk assessment."**

# 112. Advanced Scenario Question

Answer:

> **"Because technical security does not establish that the AI system is appropriate for its intended use. A model could be well protected against unauthorized access while still producing unreliable outputs, processing inappropriate data, creating privacy concerns, creating automation-bias risks or operating with excessive autonomy. The assessment needs to consider the complete use case and business context."**

# 06.20 — AI Risk Assessment Methodology

## Part 3 — Risk Scoring, Control Effectiveness, Residual Risk, Treatment, and Risk Decisions

Part 1 established the **AI risk assessment lifecycle**.

Part 2 established **how to identify and construct AI risk scenarios**.

Part 3 focuses on the part that turns risk identification into a **management decision**:

> **How do we determine the level of risk, evaluate controls, calculate residual risk, and decide what the organization should do?**

The core sequence is:

**INHERENT RISK → CONTROL ASSESSMENT → CONTROL EFFECTIVENESS → RESIDUAL RISK → RISK APPETITE → TREATMENT → DECISION**

---

# 120. Inherent Risk

Inherent risk is the level of risk that exists:

> **Before considering mitigating controls.**

For an AI system, inherent risk should reflect the characteristics of the use case itself.

Consider:

* Sensitivity of data.
* Business criticality.
* Number of affected users.
* Degree of AI autonomy.
* Decision impact.
* Threat exposure.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Potential consequences.

---

# 121. Example of Inherent Risk

Suppose an AI agent can:

* Access customer records.
* Update CRM information.
* Send customer communications.
* Trigger account changes.

Before controls are considered:

> **Inherent risk may be High or Critical.**

This does not mean the system cannot be deployed.

It means:

> **Strong controls will likely be required.**

---

# 122. Risk Scoring

A simple methodology is:

**Risk = Likelihood × Impact**

For example:

* Likelihood = 4.
* Impact = 5.

Therefore:

**4 × 5 = 20**

The organization's risk matrix determines whether 20 is:

* High.
* Critical.
* Or another defined category.

---

# 123. Likelihood

Likelihood asks:

> **How likely is the risk event to occur?**

A five-point scale could be:

| Score | Description    |
| ----: | -------------- |
|     1 | Rare           |
|     2 | Unlikely       |
|     3 | Possible       |
|     4 | Likely         |
|     5 | Almost certain |

The organization should define objective criteria for each level.

---

# 124. Impact

Impact asks:

> **What would happen if the risk materialized?**

Possible dimensions include:

* Financial.
* Operational.
* Privacy.
* Security.
* Regulatory.
* Legal.
* Customer.
* Reputation.
* Strategic.

---

# 125. Multi-Dimensional Impact

An AI incident may have different impacts simultaneously.

Example:

| Dimension   | Assessment |
| ----------- | ---------- |
| Financial   | Moderate   |
| Operational | High       |
| Privacy     | High       |
| Regulatory  | High       |
| Reputation  | Moderate   |

The enterprise methodology may use the highest impact, weighted scoring, or another approved approach.

Never invent the aggregation rule.

---

# 126. Risk Rating Requires Rationale

Do not write:

> Likelihood = 4.

Instead:

> **Likelihood = 4 because the AI agent is externally accessible, has broad permissions, and testing identified repeatable exploitation conditions.**

Likewise:

> **Impact = 5 because successful exploitation could expose highly sensitive customer information at significant scale.**

The rationale makes the assessment defensible.

---

# 127. Evidence-Based Scoring

Good evidence may include:

* Security testing.
* Model evaluation.
* Incident data.
* Historical failures.
* Production metrics.
* Threat intelligence.
* Vendor assessments.
* Audit findings.
* Privacy analysis.

The more consequential the risk, the stronger the evidence should be.

---

# 128. Avoid False Precision

AI risk can involve uncertainty.

For example:

> "There is a 37.4% probability that the model will produce harmful output."

Unless supported by appropriate evidence, this creates **false precision**.

Instead:

> "Likelihood is assessed as Possible based on testing and observed model behavior."

---

# 129. Confidence Level

For mature assessments, document confidence.

Example:

| Rating   | Confidence | Reason                     |
| -------- | ---------- | -------------------------- |
| High     | High       | Strong production evidence |
| High     | Medium     | Limited production data    |
| Moderate | Low        | Significant evidence gaps  |

This distinguishes:

> **What we know**

from:

> **What we estimate.**

---

# 130. Risk Uncertainty

AI systems can create uncertainty because:

* Model behavior may vary.
* New inputs may produce unexpected outputs.
* Training data may be incomplete.
* Vendor models may change.
* Production behavior may differ from testing.

Therefore:

> **Uncertainty itself should be considered when determining governance requirements.**

---

# 131. Control Identification

After determining inherent risk:

> **Identify the controls that reduce that risk.**

Controls may be:

### Preventive

Stop the event from occurring.

### Detective

Identify the event.

### Corrective

Reduce consequences or restore operations.

---

# 132. Preventive AI Controls

Examples:

* Access control.
* Least privilege.
* Input validation.
* Data classification.
* Prompt restrictions.
* Model approval.
* Human authorization.
* Segregation of duties.

---

# 133. Detective AI Controls

Examples:

* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* Anomaly detection.
* DLP alerts.
* Model-performance monitoring.
* Access reviews.
* Incident detection.

---

# 134. Corrective AI Controls

Examples:

* Incident response.
* Account rollback.
* Model rollback.
* Data restoration.
* Service fallback.
* Human intervention.
* Provider escalation.

---

# 135. Control Design Effectiveness

Ask:

> **Is the control appropriately designed to address the risk?**

Example:

Risk:

> AI agent performs unauthorized actions.

Control:

> Human approval is required for high-value transactions.

That is potentially a well-designed control.

---

# 136. Operating Effectiveness

Then ask:

> **Does the control actually operate as designed?**

Suppose policy says:

> All high-value transactions require human approval.

Testing shows:

> 15% of transactions bypass approval.

The control exists.

But:

> **Its operating effectiveness is deficient.**

---

# 137. Control Effectiveness Is Not Binary

Avoid:

> Effective / ineffective.

A more useful model can be:

| Level | Meaning             |
| ----- | ------------------- |
| 0     | Not implemented     |
| 1     | Weak                |
| 2     | Partially effective |
| 3     | Effective           |
| 4     | Highly effective    |

The organization's approved control-rating methodology should be used where one exists.

---

# 138. Control Coverage

A control may address only part of a risk.

Example:

Risk:

> Prompt injection leads to unauthorized customer-data disclosure.

Controls:

* Input filtering.
* Access control.
* DLP.
* Logging.

No single control completely eliminates the risk.

Therefore assess:

> **How much of the risk scenario is actually covered?**

---

# 139. Control Layering

AI systems should often use multiple layers.

```text id="5w2q8x"

                   ↓
            Input Controls
                   ↓
           Authentication
                   ↓
            Authorization
                   ↓
             AI MODEL
                   ↓
           Output Validation
                   ↓
              DLP
                   ↓
             Monitoring
                   ↓
               User
```

This is **defense in depth**.

---

# 140. Compensating Controls

Sometimes a preferred control cannot be implemented.

Example:

> The AI model cannot provide reliable explainability.

A compensating control could be:

* Human review.
* Restricted use.
* Manual verification.
* Limited decision authority.

The question becomes:

> **Does the alternative control reduce the risk sufficiently?**

---

# 141. Control Dependency

Some controls depend on other controls.

Example:

```text id="4x4t1q"

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

```

If the identity system fails, authorization controls may also be affected.

Therefore:

> **Control dependencies should be understood.**

---

# 142. Inherited Controls

Some controls may come from another environment.

For example:

> The AI vendor provides infrastructure security.

The organization may inherit some protection.

But:

> **Inherited controls do not automatically mean inherited accountability.**

The organization still needs evidence and contractual assurance.

---

# 143. Shared Responsibility

AI services frequently use a shared-responsibility model.

For example:

### Provider

May manage:

* Infrastructure.
* Base model platform.
* Physical security.
* Certain platform controls.

### Customer

May manage:

* User access.
* Prompts.
* Data.
* Configuration.
* Integration.
* Business process.
* Human oversight.

The risk assessment should identify the boundary clearly.

---

# 144. Residual Risk

Residual risk is:

> **The risk remaining after considering controls.**

Example:

```text id="8j5p3d"
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Critical
     ↓
Controls
     ↓
Strong
     ↓
Residual Risk
     ↓
Moderate
```

The key management question becomes:

> **Is Moderate acceptable?**

---

# 145. Residual Risk Is Not Zero

A common misunderstanding is:

> "We have controls, so there is no risk."

That is rarely realistic.

Controls generally:

> **Reduce risk rather than eliminate it completely.**

---

# 146. Residual Risk Example

### Risk

AI agent sends confidential information to an unauthorized recipient.

### Inherent risk

High.

### Controls

* Identity verification.
* DLP.
* Approval workflow.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.

### Residual risk

Moderate.

The organization may accept the remaining risk if it falls within appetite.

---

# 147. Risk Appetite Comparison

The assessment now asks:

> **Is residual risk within the organization's approved risk appetite?**

Example:

```text id="5r2c7n"
Residual Risk = Moderate
Risk Appetite = Moderate

                ↓

        Within Appetite

                ↓

       May be Accepted
```

---

# 148. Risk Above Appetite

Example:

```text id="q5n9v2"
Residual Risk = High
Risk Appetite = Moderate

                ↓

       Above Appetite

                ↓

 Treatment / Escalation
```

The system may need:

* Additional controls.
* Reduced functionality.
* Additional monitoring.
* Executive approval.
* Delayed deployment.

---

# 149. Risk Treatment Options

The classic options are:

### Avoid

Stop the activity.

### Reduce

Implement controls.

### Transfer/Share

Move or share part of the exposure through contractual or insurance mechanisms.

### Accept

Acknowledge and formally accept the remaining risk.

---

# 150. Risk Avoidance

Example:

> An organization decides not to use generative AI for highly sensitive legal decisions because the risk cannot be sufficiently controlled.

This is risk avoidance.

---

# 151. Risk Reduction

Example:

AI agent originally has:

> Full customer-account access.

Risk reduction:

> Read-only access plus human approval for changes.

The use case remains.

The risk is reduced.

---

# 152. Risk Transfer

Example:

The organization uses contractual provisions requiring a third-party AI provider to:

* Meet security obligations.
* Notify incidents.
* Support audits.
* Maintain defined service levels.

This may **share or transfer certain consequences**, but it does not necessarily eliminate the underlying risk.

Risk acceptance means:

> **Management knowingly accepts the remaining risk.**

It should not mean:

> "Nobody addressed it."

Formal acceptance should identify:

* Risk.
* Residual rating.
* Rationale.
* Risk owner.
* Approver.
* Expiration/review date where appropriate.

# 161. Control Prioritization

When resources are limited, prioritize controls that address:

> **High-impact and high-likelihood scenarios.**

For example:

If an AI agent has excessive privileges:

> Least privilege may be more important than adding another dashboard.

This is why risk-based control selection matters.

# 162. Cost vs. Risk

Risk treatment should consider:

> **Is the control proportionate to the risk?**

A $1 million control for a negligible risk may not be reasonable.

Conversely:

> A critical customer-data risk should not remain untreated simply because remediation is expensive.

The decision should be risk-based.

# 176. Interview Question

### "How do you determine residual risk?"

Strong answer:

> **"I first establish the inherent risk based on the likelihood and impact of the scenario without considering controls. I then identify the relevant preventive, detective and corrective controls and assess both their design and operating effectiveness. Based on the evidence, I determine how much risk is actually reduced and establish the residual risk. I then compare that residual risk against the organization's risk appetite and determine whether it can be accepted, requires further treatment or needs escalation."**

---

# 177. Interview Question

### "How do you assess control effectiveness?"

Answer:

> **"I assess both design effectiveness and operating effectiveness. Design effectiveness asks whether the control is appropriately designed to address the identified risk. Operating effectiveness asks whether the control is actually implemented and consistently operating as intended. I use evidence such as testing results, logs, access reviews, audit evidence and monitoring data rather than assuming that a documented control is effective."**

---

# 178. Interview Question

### "What if the residual risk remains high?"

Answer:

> **"I compare it with risk appetite and delegated acceptance authority. If the residual risk is above appetite, I would identify additional treatment options, consider reducing the AI system's scope or autonomy, introduce compensating controls, or escalate the risk to the appropriate decision-maker. I would not simply mark the system as approved because the business wants to deploy it."**

---

# 179. Interview Question

### "Who owns AI risk?"

Strong answer:

> **"The accountable business or designated risk owner should own the risk because they own the business outcome and decision. GRC facilitates the methodology, challenges the assessment and provides governance oversight, while technical, security, privacy and legal teams provide specialized input. The exact accountability model should be defined through the organization's governance framework."**

---

# 180. Advanced Scenario

### Interviewer:

> "The business says the AI system is safe because the vendor has SOC 2 and ISO certification. Would you approve it?"

Strong answer:

> **"Those certifications provide useful assurance, but they would not be sufficient by themselves. I would evaluate whether the vendor's controls actually address the risks associated with our specific use case, including data handling, model behavior, access, retention, incident management, subcontractors, service changes and contractual obligations. I would combine vendor assurance with our own use-case risk assessment and determine the residual risk."**

---

# 181. Advanced Scenario

### Interviewer:

> "The risk score is High, but the business wants to proceed."

Answer:

> **"I would not automatically block the initiative, but I would establish why the risk is High, verify the evidence and compare it with the organization's risk appetite. If the residual risk is above appetite, I would work with the business to identify additional treatment, reduce scope or autonomy, introduce compensating controls, or escalate the risk to the appropriate acceptance authority. The final decision should be documented and made by the authorized risk owner."**

---

# 182. Advanced Scenario

### Interviewer:

> "Can controls reduce a Critical risk to Low?"

Answer:

> **"Potentially, depending on the methodology and evidence, but I would be careful not to assume that controls automatically reduce risk by a fixed numerical amount. I would assess the actual effectiveness of the controls and the remaining exposure. Some inherent characteristics of the use case may remain significant even with strong controls."**

This is a strong senior-level answer because it avoids simplistic mathematics.

---

# 183. The Senior GRC Principle

One of the most important concepts in this entire section is:

> **Controls do not determine risk; controls influence residual risk.**

Risk should first be understood independently.

Then:

> **Controls are evaluated based on evidence.**

Then:

> **Residual risk is determined.**

# 186. Final Master Answer

If the interviewer asks:

> **"How do you take an identified AI risk through to a final governance decision?"**

Use:

> **"Once I identify and structure the risk scenario, I establish the inherent risk using the organization's approved likelihood and impact criteria and document the evidence and assumptions supporting the rating. I then identify the relevant controls and assess both their design and operating effectiveness. Based on that evidence, I determine the residual risk and compare it against the organization's risk appetite and acceptance authority.**
>
> **If the risk is within appetite, it may be accepted with appropriate monitoring. If it is above appetite, I would work with the stakeholders to reduce or otherwise treat the risk, potentially by strengthening controls, reducing the system's scope or autonomy, introducing compensating controls, or escalating it to the appropriate authority. I would document the final decision, risk owner, treatment actions, conditions and monitoring requirements, and establish reassessment triggers for material changes."**

# 188. AI Risk Assessment Is a Governance Process

A common mistake is to treat an AI risk assessment as a questionnaire completed by the project team.

The assessment should answer:

# 189. Three Lines of Defense

AI risk assessment can be aligned with the **Three Lines Model**.

### First Line — Business / AI Owner

Owns the AI system and associated risks.

Responsibilities include:

* Operating the system.
* Implementing controls.
* Managing risks.
* Providing evidence.

### Second Line — GRC / Risk / Compliance

Provides:

* Methodology.
* Oversight.
* Challenge.
* Risk aggregation.
* Policy guidance.
* Monitoring.

### Third Line — Internal Audit

Provides:

* Independent assurance.
* Audit testing.
* Governance assessment.
* Control effectiveness evaluation.

The important principle is:

> **GRC should challenge the business, but should not become the business risk owner.**

# 233. Second-Level Challenge

GRC should ask challenging questions.

Examples:

> "Why did you rate likelihood as Possible rather than Likely?"

> "What evidence supports that assumption?"

> "How do you know the human-review control is effective?"

> "What happens if the AI provider changes the model?"

> "What happens if the human reviewer accepts the AI output without validation?"

This is **risk challenge**.

---

# 234. Challenge Without Blocking

A mature GRC professional should not approach every AI project as:

> "No."

Instead:

> **"Here is the risk. Here is the evidence. Here are the options. Here is the control gap. Here is what is required for acceptable residual risk."**

This positions GRC as a business-enabling function.

---

# 235. Risk-Based Recommendations

Instead of:

> "You cannot deploy AI."

Say:

> "The current residual risk is above appetite because the AI agent has unrestricted transaction authority. Restricting the agent to read-only access and requiring human approval for transactions would materially reduce the exposure."

This is much more professional.

---

# 236. Governance Decision Categories

A practical decision model:

### Approved

Risk within appetite.

### Approved with Conditions

Risk acceptable if defined controls/actions are completed.

### Remediation Required

Risk too high until specific actions are completed.

### Escalated

Risk requires higher authority.

### Rejected

Risk cannot be justified or adequately controlled.

# 237. Example End-to-End Scenario

Consider:

> An organization wants an AI agent to automatically process customer refunds.

### Step 1 — Purpose

Automate refund processing.

### Step 2 — Data

Customer account and transaction data.

### Step 3 — Authority

AI can initiate refunds.

### Step 4 — Risks

* Unauthorized refund.
* Incorrect refund.
* Fraud.
* Data exposure.

### Step 5 — Inherent Risk

High.

### Step 6 — Controls

* RBAC.
* Transaction limits.
* Human approval.
* Fraud detection.
* Logging.

### Step 7 — Control Assessment

Controls are effective but transaction limits need improvement.

### Step 8 — Residual Risk

Moderate.

### Step 9 — Appetite

Moderate is acceptable.

### Step 10 — Decision

Approved with conditions.

### Step 11 — Monitoring

Monitor:

* Refund anomalies.
* Override rate.
* Fraud alerts.
* AI errors.

### Step 12 — Reassessment

Trigger on:

* Increased refund authority.
* New model.
* Material incident.

This is the complete AI GRC lifecycle.

# 238. What Makes This a Senior-Level Approach?

A junior assessor may say:

> "I complete the AI risk assessment."

A senior GRC consultant says:

> **"I establish the risk context, challenge the assumptions, validate the control environment, determine residual risk against appetite, facilitate the governance decision, and ensure the risk remains monitored throughout the AI lifecycle."**

That difference is important in interviews.

---

# 239. Senior Interview Question

### "How do you make AI risk assessments sustainable?"

Answer:

> **"I would embed the assessment into the AI lifecycle rather than treating it as a one-time exercise. I would establish an AI inventory, intake and risk-tiering process, define assessment and approval gates, connect material changes and incidents to reassessment triggers, maintain risk and control traceability, and establish ongoing KRIs and control monitoring. This creates a continuous governance process rather than a point-in-time compliance exercise."**

---

# 240. Senior Interview Question

### "How do you ensure consistency across AI assessments?"

Answer:

> **"I would establish a standardized methodology with defined risk criteria, scoring guidance, evidence requirements and approval thresholds. I would use common templates, provide assessor training, perform peer review for higher-risk cases and periodically calibrate assessments to identify inconsistent scoring. GRC would also perform quality assurance and challenge unsupported assumptions."**

---

# 241. Senior Interview Question

### "What would you do if the business disagrees with your risk rating?"

Answer:

> **"I would first understand the basis for the disagreement and compare both positions against the approved methodology and evidence. If the difference results from an assumption, I would clarify or validate the evidence. If professional judgment is still required, I would document the rationale from both sides and escalate according to the governance model rather than allowing the disagreement to become an undocumented decision. The final rating should be determined through the approved risk governance process."**

---

# 242. Senior Interview Question

### "How do you prevent AI governance from becoming bureaucracy?"

Answer:

> **"I use proportionality. Low-risk AI use cases should have a lightweight process, while high-impact or highly autonomous systems should receive deeper assessment and stronger controls. I also integrate AI governance into existing enterprise risk, cybersecurity, privacy and change-management processes rather than creating unnecessary parallel processes. The objective is to enable informed business decisions, not simply create additional paperwork."**

---

# 243. Senior Interview Question

### "What happens after the AI system is approved?"

Answer:

> **"Approval is not the end of the risk lifecycle. I would establish monitoring requirements, control indicators, risk thresholds and reassessment triggers. Material model changes, new data, increased autonomy, incidents, regulatory changes or significant performance degradation should trigger reassessment. The objective is to ensure that the residual risk remains within appetite throughout the system's lifecycle."**

# 252. Final Senior-Level Answer

If asked:

> **"Describe your complete AI risk assessment approach."**

Use this:

> **"My approach starts with establishing an AI inventory and understanding the system's intended purpose, business process, data, users, dependencies and level of autonomy. I then risk-tier the use case and identify relevant risks across cybersecurity, privacy, data, model, operational, human, compliance and third-party dimensions.**
>
> **For material risks, I construct specific risk scenarios and assess likelihood and impact using the organization's approved methodology, documenting the evidence and assumptions supporting the rating. I then evaluate the relevant controls for both design and operating effectiveness and determine the residual risk.**
>
> **I compare the residual risk with the organization's risk appetite and determine whether the appropriate response is acceptance, reduction, transfer, avoidance or escalation. The decision is documented with a clear risk owner, treatment plan, approval authority and monitoring requirements.**
>
> **Finally, I treat the assessment as a lifecycle process. Material changes, incidents, new data, model changes, increased autonomy, regulatory changes or significant performance degradation trigger reassessment. This ensures AI risk remains aligned with the organization's risk appetite throughout the system's lifecycle."**
