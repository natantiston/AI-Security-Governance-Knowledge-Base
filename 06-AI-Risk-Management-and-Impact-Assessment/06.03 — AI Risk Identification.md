# 06.03 — AI Risk Identification

## Part 1 — Foundations and Core Principles

### 1. Definition of AI Risk Identification

AI risk identification is the systematic process of discovering, documenting, and describing potential risks associated with an AI system throughout its lifecycle.

The objective is to answer:

> **What could go wrong, why could it happen, and what could be affected?**

AI risk identification occurs before detailed risk analysis and risk evaluation.

A simplified sequence is:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Business Context
    ↓
Risk Identification
    ↓
Risk Analysis
    ↓
Risk Evaluation
    ↓
Risk Treatment
```

The quality of subsequent risk-management activities depends heavily on whether material risks were identified in the first place.

---

### 2. Purpose of AI Risk Identification

The primary objectives are to:

* Discover potential AI risk scenarios.
* Identify sources of risk.
* Identify vulnerabilities and weaknesses.
* Identify possible threats and triggers.
* Identify affected assets and stakeholders.
* Identify potential consequences.
* Establish a complete risk inventory.
* Support later risk analysis.
* Prevent important risks from being overlooked.

Risk identification should be **systematic rather than dependent on individual intuition**.

---

### 3. Identification Is Not Risk Assessment

A common mistake is to combine identification and assessment.

They are different activities.

**Risk identification:**

> What risks exist?

**Risk analysis:**

> How likely and how severe could they be?

**Risk evaluation:**

> Is the resulting risk acceptable?

For example:

```text
Identification:
AI chatbot may disclose confidential information.

Analysis:
Likelihood = Medium
Impact = High

Evaluation:
Risk exceeds organizational tolerance.
```

The first step identifies the scenario; later steps determine its significance.

---

### 4. AI Risk Identification Begins With the AI System

Before identifying risks, the organization should understand what the AI system actually does.

Important questions include:

* What is the AI system?
* What is its purpose?
* Who uses it?
* What decisions does it support?
* What actions can it perform?
* What data does it process?
* What systems does it connect to?
* Who can be affected?
* How critical is it to the business?

Without understanding the system, risk identification becomes speculative.

---

### 5. AI Business Purpose

The intended business purpose is one of the most important identification inputs.

Consider two systems:

```text
AI System A
Internal document summarization

AI System B
Automated credit decision
```

Both use AI, but their potential risks are substantially different.

The second system may introduce significantly greater:

* Financial risk.
* Regulatory risk.
* Human impact.
* Fairness risk.
* Model risk.

Therefore:

> **AI risk identification must always consider intended use.**

The use case describes how AI is actually being used.

Examples include:

* Customer service.
* Fraud detection.
* Recruitment.
* Marketing.
* Software development.
* Document processing.
* Medical decision support.
* Financial forecasting.
* Autonomous workflow execution.

The use case provides the context for identifying meaningful risk scenarios.

### 8. Identify AI Assets

AI risk identification should identify what needs protection.

Potential assets include:

* Training data.
* Production data.
* Models.
* Prompts.
* System instructions.
* Algorithms.
* Source code.
* APIs.
* Infrastructure.
* AI-generated outputs.
* Knowledge bases.
* Credentials.
* Intellectual property.

The assets define what could be harmed or compromised.

### 14. Identify Affected Stakeholders

Possible stakeholders include:

* Customers.

* Suppliers.
* Business partners.
* Management.
* Regulators.
* Members of the public.
* Communities.

This is particularly important for AI systems with significant human or societal consequences.

### 15. Identify Risk Sources

### 16. Identify Vulnerabilities

A vulnerability is a weakness that could contribute to a risk event.

Examples:

* Weak authentication.
* Excessive permissions.
* Poor model validation.
* Incomplete training data.
* Missing output filtering.
* Insufficient monitoring.
* Poor change management.
* Lack of human oversight.

Risk identification should actively search for these weaknesses.

---

### 17. Identify Threats

Threats can be:

**Malicious**

* Attackers.
* Insiders.
* Fraudsters.
* Malicious users.

**Accidental**

* Human error.
* Misconfiguration.
* Incorrect deployment.

**Environmental**

* Provider outage.
* Regulatory change.
* Market change.
* Data distribution change.

Not every AI risk requires a malicious threat.

---

### 18. Identify Risk Events

The risk event describes what could happen.

Weak statement:

> "AI security risk."

Better:

> "An unauthorized user may manipulate the AI system through malicious input."

Even better:

> "An externally accessible AI assistant may process malicious instructions that cause it to retrieve information outside the user's authorization boundary."

Specific risk scenarios are easier to assess and treat.

---

### 19. Identify Potential Causes

A risk scenario may have multiple causes.

Example:


Identifying causes helps determine appropriate controls later.

---

### 20. Identify Potential Consequences

Risk identification should consider what happens if the risk materializes.

Potential consequences include:

* Data disclosure.
* Financial loss.
* Service disruption.
* Incorrect decisions.
* Customer harm.
* Regulatory penalties.
* Legal disputes.
* Reputational damage.
* Safety consequences.
* Strategic impact.

The consequences should be linked to organizational objectives.

---

### 23. Identify Security Risks

Security risk identification should consider:

* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Confidentiality.
* Integrity.
* Availability.
* Prompt injection.
* Data poisoning.
* Model manipulation.
* Model theft.
* API security.
* Infrastructure security.
* Supply-chain security.

AI introduces additional attack surfaces that traditional application assessments may not fully capture.

---

### 24. Identify Privacy Risks

Privacy risk identification should consider:

* Personal data collection.
* Purpose of processing.
* Data minimization.
* Data retention.
* Data sharing.
* Profiling.
* Automated processing.
* Data subject rights.
* Cross-border processing.

The assessment should consider the entire information lifecycle.

---

### 25. Identify Data Risks

Data-related questions include:

* Is the data accurate?
* Is it complete?
* Is it representative?
* Is its source known?
* Is its use authorized?
* Is lineage documented?
* Can the data be manipulated?
* Can the data become stale?

Data risk can affect both model performance and compliance.

---

### 26. Identify Model Risks

Model risk identification can examine:

* Accuracy.
* Reliability.
* Robustness.
* Bias.
* Drift.
* Explainability.
* Generalization.
* Failure modes.
* Model assumptions.
* Model limitations.

The organization should identify where the model could behave differently from its intended purpose.

---

### 27. Identify Generative AI Risks

Generative AI introduces additional scenarios such as:

* Hallucination.
* Prompt injection.
* Sensitive-data leakage.
* Harmful content generation.
* Inaccurate advice.
* Insecure code generation.
* Misleading outputs.
* Intellectual-property concerns.

These should be identified according to the actual use case rather than assumed to exist automatically.

---

### 28. Identify AI Agent Risks

AI agents can introduce risks because they can act on behalf of users.

Risk identification should consider:

```text id="y9u5lf"

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓
```

Potential risks include:

* Excessive autonomy.
* Incorrect tool selection.
* Excessive permissions.
* Unauthorized actions.
* Goal manipulation.
* Inadequate human intervention.

---

### 29. Identify Human Oversight Risks

Human involvement does not automatically eliminate AI risk.

Potential weaknesses include:

* Reviewer fatigue.
* Automation bias.
* Insufficient training.
* Lack of authority to override.
* Inadequate escalation.
* Excessive reliance on AI outputs.

Risk identification should therefore examine whether human oversight is actually effective.

---

### 30. Identify Bias and Fairness Risks

Questions may include:

* Could the AI produce systematically different outcomes?
* Is training data representative?
* Are certain groups underrepresented?
* Are proxy variables involved?
* Are error rates materially different across relevant groups?
* Could historical bias be reproduced?

These questions become especially important for high-impact use cases.

---

### 31. Identify Operational Risks

Operational risk identification should consider:

* Availability.
* Reliability.
* Incident response.
* Business continuity.
* Disaster recovery.
* Change management.
* Capacity.
* Monitoring.
* Skills and staffing.

An AI model can be technically accurate but operationally unreliable.

---

### 32. Identify Legal and Regulatory Risks

Questions include:

* What laws apply?
* What regulatory requirements apply?
* Is the use case regulated?
* Are contractual obligations relevant?
* Are intellectual-property issues present?
* Are there sector-specific requirements?
* Does the AI system affect regulated decisions?

Legal and regulatory requirements should be considered during identification, not only after an incident occurs.

---

### 34. Identify Strategic Risks

Strategic risk identification should consider:

* AI dependency.
* Competitive positioning.
* Investment decisions.
* Technology obsolescence.
* Vendor concentration.
* Business-model changes.
* Failure to adopt important AI capabilities.

These risks may not be visible in technical security assessments.

---

### 35. Identify Reputational Risks

Ask:

> "If this AI system failed publicly, how could stakeholders perceive the organization?"

Potential scenarios include:

* Public discriminatory outcomes.
* Customer-facing misinformation.
* AI-related privacy incident.
* Unsafe AI behavior.
* Misleading automated communications.

Reputational risk can be difficult to quantify but should still be identified.

---

### 36. Identify Environmental Risks

Where relevant, consider:

* Computing requirements.
* Energy consumption.
* Infrastructure footprint.
* Hardware lifecycle.
* Resource usage.

The relevance depends on the AI system and organizational objectives.

### 43. Scenario Analysis

Scenario analysis asks:

> "What happens if...?"

Examples:

* What if the model is wrong?
* What if the provider becomes unavailable?
* What if a user sends malicious input?
* What if sensitive data enters the prompt?
* What if the model changes unexpectedly?
* What if the AI takes an unauthorized action?

Scenario analysis is particularly useful for identifying non-obvious risks.

---

### 44. Historical Incident Analysis

Past incidents can reveal recurring risks.

Sources include:

* Internal incidents.
* Security incidents.
* AI failures.
* Vendor incidents.
* Audit findings.
* Near misses.
* Industry incidents.

The objective is not merely to learn from failures but to identify similar exposures before they occur.

---

### 45. Near-Miss Analysis

A near miss is an event that could have caused harm but did not.

Example:

> An AI assistant generated a response containing sensitive information, but the response was blocked by an output filter.

The event reveals an underlying risk even though no actual harm occurred.

Near misses should therefore contribute to risk identification.

Experts may include:

* Data scientists.
* Privacy specialists.
* Legal professionals.
* Domain experts.

However:

> **Expert judgment should supplement systematic identification, not replace it.**

### 51. Risk Identification Should Be Evidence-Based

A risk should ideally be supported by evidence such as:

* Architecture.
* Configuration.
* Data flow.
* Business process.
* Model documentation.
* Testing results.
* Vendor information.
* Incident history.
* Stakeholder interviews.

This reduces speculative risk statements.

---

### 52. Avoid Generic Risk Statements

Weak:

> "AI may have security issues."

Better:

> "The externally accessible AI assistant may accept malicious instructions that bypass intended application controls."

The second statement identifies a concrete scenario that can be analyzed.

---

### 53. Avoid Overly Specific Technical Statements

The opposite problem is also possible.

For example:

> "Prompt injection through a specific tokenizer parsing weakness causes a particular API call."

If the underlying architecture changes, the risk statement becomes obsolete.

A good risk statement should be specific enough to be useful but general enough to remain valid.

### 56. Risk Identification and Shadow AI

Employees may use AI applications outside approved enterprise systems.

Examples:

---

### 60. Core AI Risk Identification Principle

The fundamental principle is:

> **AI risk identification should systematically examine the AI system, its purpose, data, model, technology, users, dependencies, lifecycle, stakeholders, threats, vulnerabilities, and potential consequences to establish a complete set of credible risk scenarios.**

The goal is not to predict every possible failure.

The goal is to ensure that **material and reasonably foreseeable risks are identified before they become unmanaged exposures**.
