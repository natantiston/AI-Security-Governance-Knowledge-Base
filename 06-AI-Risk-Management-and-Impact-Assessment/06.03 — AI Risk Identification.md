# 06.03 — AI Risk Identification

## Part 1 — Foundations and Core Principles

### 1. Definition of AI Risk Identification

AI risk identification is the systematic process of discovering, documenting, and describing potential risks associated with an AI system throughout its lifecycle.

The objective is to answer:

> **What could go wrong, why could it happen, and what could be affected?**

AI risk identification occurs before detailed risk analysis and risk evaluation.

A simplified sequence is:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Business Context
    ↓
Risk Identification
    ↓
Risk Analysis
    ↓
Risk Evaluation
    ↓
Risk Treatment
```

The quality of subsequent risk-management activities depends heavily on whether material risks were identified in the first place.

---

### 2. Purpose of AI Risk Identification

The primary objectives are to:

* Discover potential AI risk scenarios.
* Identify sources of risk.
* Identify vulnerabilities and weaknesses.
* Identify possible threats and triggers.
* Identify affected assets and stakeholders.
* Identify potential consequences.
* Establish a complete risk inventory.
* Support later risk analysis.
* Prevent important risks from being overlooked.

Risk identification should be **systematic rather than dependent on individual intuition**.

---

### 3. Identification Is Not Risk Assessment

A common mistake is to combine identification and assessment.

They are different activities.

**Risk identification:**

> What risks exist?

**Risk analysis:**

> How likely and how severe could they be?

**Risk evaluation:**

> Is the resulting risk acceptable?

For example:

```text
Identification:
AI chatbot may disclose confidential information.

Analysis:
Likelihood = Medium
Impact = High

Evaluation:
Risk exceeds organizational tolerance.
```

The first step identifies the scenario; later steps determine its significance.

---

### 4. AI Risk Identification Begins With the AI System

Before identifying risks, the organization should understand what the AI system actually does.

Important questions include:

* What is the AI system?
* What is its purpose?
* Who uses it?
* What decisions does it support?
* What actions can it perform?
* What data does it process?
* What systems does it connect to?
* Who can be affected?
* How critical is it to the business?

Without understanding the system, risk identification becomes speculative.

---

### 5. AI Business Purpose

The intended business purpose is one of the most important identification inputs.

Consider two systems:

```text
AI System A
Internal document summarization

AI System B
Automated credit decision
```

Both use AI, but their potential risks are substantially different.

The second system may introduce significantly greater:

* Financial risk.
* Regulatory risk.
* Human impact.
* Fairness risk.
* Model risk.

Therefore:

> **AI risk identification must always consider intended use.**

### 16. Identify Vulnerabilities

A vulnerability is a weakness that could contribute to a risk event.

Examples:

* Weak authentication.
* Excessive permissions.
* Poor model validation.
* Incomplete training data.
* Missing output filtering.
* Insufficient monitoring.
* Poor change management.
* Lack of human oversight.

Risk identification should actively search for these weaknesses.

### 17. Identify Threats

Threats can be:

**Malicious**

* Attackers.
* Insiders.
* Fraudsters.
* Malicious users.

**Accidental**

* Human error.
* Misconfiguration.
* Incorrect deployment.

**Environmental**

* Provider outage.
* Regulatory change.
* Market change.
* Data distribution change.

Not every AI risk requires a malicious threat.

---

### 18. Identify Risk Events

The risk event describes what could happen.

Weak statement:

> "AI security risk."

Better:

> "An unauthorized user may manipulate the AI system through malicious input."

Even better:

> "An externally accessible AI assistant may process malicious instructions that cause it to retrieve information outside the user's authorization boundary."

Specific risk scenarios are easier to assess and treat.

---

### 29. Identify Human Oversight Risks

Human involvement does not automatically eliminate AI risk.

Potential weaknesses include:

* Reviewer fatigue.
* Automation bias.
* Insufficient training.
* Lack of authority to override.
* Inadequate escalation.
* Excessive reliance on AI outputs.

Risk identification should therefore examine whether human oversight is actually effective.

### 30. Identify Bias and Fairness Risks

Questions may include:

* Could the AI produce systematically different outcomes?
* Is training data representative?
* Are certain groups underrepresented?
* Are proxy variables involved?
* Are error rates materially different across relevant groups?
* Could historical bias be reproduced?

These questions become especially important for high-impact use cases.

---

### 31. Identify Operational Risks

Operational risk identification should consider:

* Availability.
* Reliability.
* Incident response.
* Business continuity.
* Disaster recovery.
* Change management.
* Capacity.
* Monitoring.
* Skills and staffing.

An AI model can be technically accurate but operationally unreliable.

---

### 32. Identify Legal and Regulatory Risks

Questions include:

* What laws apply?
* What regulatory requirements apply?
* Is the use case regulated?
* Are contractual obligations relevant?
* Are intellectual-property issues present?
* Are there sector-specific requirements?
* Does the AI system affect regulated decisions?

Legal and regulatory requirements should be considered during identification, not only after an incident occurs.

### 44. Historical Incident Analysis

Sources include:

* Internal incidents.
* Security incidents.
* AI failures.
* Vendor incidents.

* Near misses.

The objective is not merely to learn from failures but to identify similar exposures before they occur.

### 45. Near-Miss Analysis

A near miss is an event that could have caused harm but did not.

Example:

> An AI assistant generated a response containing sensitive information, but the response was blocked by an output filter.

The event reveals an underlying risk even though no actual harm occurred.

Near misses should therefore contribute to risk identification.

### 51. Risk Identification Should Be Evidence-Based

A risk should ideally be supported by evidence such as:

* Architecture.
* Configuration.
* Data flow.
* Business process.
* Model documentation.
* Testing results.
* Vendor information.
* Incident history.
* Stakeholder interviews.

This reduces speculative risk statements.

---

### 52. Avoid Generic Risk Statements

Weak:

> "AI may have security issues."

Better:

> "The externally accessible AI assistant may accept malicious instructions that bypass intended application controls."

The second statement identifies a concrete scenario that can be analyzed.

---

### 53. Avoid Overly Specific Technical Statements

The opposite problem is also possible.

For example:

> "Prompt injection through a specific tokenizer parsing weakness causes a particular API call."

If the underlying architecture changes, the risk statement becomes obsolete.

A good risk statement should be specific enough to be useful but general enough to remain valid.

### 60. Core AI Risk Identification Principle

The fundamental principle is:

> **AI risk identification should systematically examine the AI system, its purpose, data, model, technology, users, dependencies, lifecycle, stakeholders, threats, vulnerabilities, and potential consequences to establish a complete set of credible risk scenarios.**

The goal is not to predict every possible failure.

The goal is to ensure that **material and reasonably foreseeable risks are identified before they become unmanaged exposures**.

## Part 2 — AI Risk Identification Methods and Analytical Techniques

### 61. Structured Risk Identification

AI risk identification should use a structured methodology rather than relying solely on brainstorming.

A practical structure is:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Purpose
    ↓
Assets
    ↓
Data
    ↓
Actors
    ↓
Dependencies
    ↓
Threats / Vulnerabilities
    ↓
Risk Scenarios
    ↓
Potential Consequences
```

This ensures that risk identification considers both technical and business dimensions.

---

### 62. Asset-Based Identification

Start by identifying what the AI system depends on or what it creates.

Typical assets include:

* Models.
* Training datasets.
* Prompts.
* System instructions.
* Knowledge bases.
* APIs.
* Credentials.
* Source code.
* AI-generated outputs.
* Customer information.
* Business processes.

For each asset, ask:

> **What could compromise, corrupt, expose, destroy, or misuse this asset?**

---

### 63. Process-Based Identification

AI risk can also be identified by examining the business process in which AI operates.

Example:

```text
Customer Request
      ↓
AI Classification
      ↓
AI Recommendation
      ↓
Human Review
      ↓
Business Decision
      ↓
Customer Notification
```

At every stage, identify possible failures.

For example:

* Incorrect classification.
* Incorrect recommendation.
* Human automation bias.
* Incorrect decision.
* Incorrect communication.

---

### 64. Control-Based Identification

Existing controls can also reveal potential risks.

Suppose an AI application has:

* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Logging.
* Output filtering.
* Human approval.

For each control, ask:

> **What risk is this control intended to address?**

Then ask:

> **What happens if the control fails or is bypassed?**

This can reveal risks that might otherwise remain hidden.

---

### 67. Event-Oriented Identification

This approach asks:

> **What events could cause the AI system to behave outside its intended operation?**

Examples:

* Model failure.
* Data corruption.
* Provider outage.
* Unexpected input.
* Malicious input.
* Model drift.
* Configuration error.
* Software update.

The event becomes the center of the risk scenario.

---

### 68. Scenario-Based Identification

Scenario analysis is particularly effective for AI because AI behavior can be probabilistic and context-dependent.

Example:

> A customer provides an ambiguous request.

Possible outcome:

```text
Ambiguous Input
      ↓
AI Misinterpretation
      ↓
Incorrect Recommendation
      ↓
Customer Action
      ↓
Financial / Operational Consequence
```

The objective is to identify plausible chains of events.

---

### 69. What-If Analysis

A simple but powerful technique is asking:

> **What if this happens?**

Examples:

* What if the AI produces an incorrect answer?
* What if sensitive data enters the prompt?
* What if the model provider is unavailable?
* What if the model is changed?
* What if the user deliberately manipulates the AI?
* What if the AI output is trusted without verification?
* What if the AI takes an unintended action?

Each answer can generate a candidate risk scenario.

---

### 70. Failure Mode Analysis

Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA)-style thinking can be adapted to AI.

For each component, identify:

```text

   ↓
Failure Mode
   ↓
Cause
   ↓
Effect
```

Example:

```text
AI Model
   ↓
Incorrect Classification
   ↓
Biased / Incomplete Data
   ↓
Incorrect Customer Decision
```

This approach is useful for operational and engineering risk identification.

---

### 74. Trust-Boundary Analysis

A trust boundary exists where data, users, or processing move between different trust levels.

Example:

```text
Enterprise Network
        │
        │ Trust Boundary
        ↓
External AI Provider
```

Potential risks include:

* Unauthorized data transfer.
* Provider compromise.
* Insufficient contractual controls.
* Cross-border processing.
* Loss of visibility.

---

### 75. Identity and Access Analysis

AI risk identification should examine:

```text
Who?
 ↓
Can access what?
 ↓
For what purpose?
 ↓
With what privileges?
 ↓
Can perform which actions?
```

This becomes particularly important for AI agents.

Example:

> If an AI agent can read customer data and execute CRM updates, what prevents it from performing an unauthorized action?

That question may identify several risk scenarios.

---

### 76. Privilege Analysis

For AI systems capable of taking action, identify:

* Read permissions.
* Write permissions.
* Delete permissions.
* Administrative permissions.
* API permissions.
* Transaction permissions.

A useful principle is:

> **The greater the AI system's authority, the greater the potential consequence of incorrect or malicious behavior.**

---

### 77. Human-in-the-Loop Analysis

Identify where humans participate.

For example:

```text
AI Recommendation
      ↓
Human Review
      ↓
Approval
      ↓
Action
```

Then ask:

* Can the human override the AI?
* Is the reviewer sufficiently trained?
* Is the reviewer given enough information?
* Can the reviewer detect AI errors?
* Is review mandatory?
* What happens if the reviewer disagrees?

---

### 78. Human-on-the-Loop Analysis

In some systems, humans monitor AI rather than approving every decision.

Example:

```text
AI
 ↓
Automatic Decision
 ↓
Human Monitoring
 ↓
Intervention if Required
```

Risk identification should determine whether monitoring is actually capable of detecting problematic behavior in time.

---

### 79. Human-out-of-the-Loop Analysis

Fully autonomous systems create different risk scenarios.

```text
AI
 ↓
Decision
 ↓
Action
```

Risk identification should pay particular attention to:

* Authorization.
* Safety constraints.
* Monitoring.
* Fail-safe mechanisms.
* Emergency intervention.
* Scope limitations.

---

### 80. Assumption Analysis

AI systems are built around assumptions.

Examples:

* Data remains representative.
* Users behave normally.
* Model performance remains stable.
* External APIs remain available.
* Knowledge remains current.
* Human reviewers detect errors.

Risk identification should ask:

> **What happens if this assumption becomes false?**

---

### 81. Dependency Failure Analysis

Consider each critical dependency.

```text
AI Application
 ↓
Model Provider
 ↓
Cloud
 ↓
Identity Service
 ↓
Database
```

For each dependency:

> What happens if it becomes unavailable, compromised, inaccurate, or unexpectedly changed?

This identifies operational and third-party risks.

---

### 82. Change-Based Identification

AI systems change over time.

Potential changes include:

* Model update.
* Prompt change.
* Dataset change.
* Software update.
* API change.
* Vendor change.
* Configuration change.
* New use case.
* New user population.

Each change can introduce new risks.

---

### 83. Model Drift Analysis

Model performance can degrade because the environment changes.

Example:

```text
Historical Data
      ↓
Model
      ↓
New Population / New Conditions
      ↓
Performance Degradation
      ↓
Incorrect Decisions
```

Risk identification should consider whether model behavior can deteriorate over time.

---

### 84. Data Drift Analysis

Data distribution can also change.

For example:

```text
Training Data
      ↓
Production Data
      ↓
Distribution Changes
      ↓
Model Performance Changes
```

This can create new operational and model risks.

---

### 85. Concept Drift

Concept drift occurs when the relationship between inputs and expected outcomes changes.

Example:

> A fraud-detection model trained on historical transaction behavior becomes less effective because fraud patterns change.

The risk is not necessarily that the model itself was incorrectly developed.

The environment changed.

---

### 86. Misuse Analysis

AI systems may be used outside their intended purpose.

Example:

```text
Approved Use:
Customer FAQ

Actual Use:
Automated Legal Advice
```

Potential risks arise because the AI system is now being used beyond its validated context.

Risk identification should therefore distinguish:

* Intended use.
* Reasonably foreseeable misuse.
* Unauthorized use.

---

### 87. Abuse-Case Analysis

An abuse case describes how a legitimate AI capability could be deliberately misused.

Example:

```text
AI Coding Assistant
       ↓
User requests malware functionality
       ↓
AI generates harmful code
```

The purpose is to identify potential misuse scenarios and associated controls.

---

### 88. Misuse by Internal Users

Employees may intentionally or unintentionally misuse AI.

Examples:

* Uploading confidential documents.
* Using AI for unauthorized decisions.
* Bypassing approval processes.
* Using personal AI accounts.
* Relying on AI for prohibited activities.

Internal-user risk should therefore be part of the identification process.

---

### 89. Prompt-Based Risk Identification

For generative AI, prompts should be examined as a risk interface.

Potential issues include:

* Prompt injection.
* Sensitive information in prompts.
* System-prompt disclosure.
* Instruction manipulation.
* Malicious content.
* Context manipulation.

The prompt layer should not be treated as merely a user-interface feature.

---

### 90. Output-Based Risk Identification

The organization should examine what happens when the AI produces an output.

Questions include:

* Can the output be wrong?
* Can it be harmful?
* Can it disclose information?
* Can it trigger a decision?
* Can it trigger an automated action?
* Can users distinguish fact from generated content?

The risk depends heavily on how the output is consumed.

---

### 91. Tool-Use Risk Identification

For AI systems capable of calling tools:

```text
AI
 ↓
Tool
 ↓
External System
```

Risk identification should examine:

* Which tools are available?
* Which permissions do they have?
* What inputs can the AI provide?
* What actions can be performed?
* Is human approval required?
* Are actions logged?
* Can actions be reversed?

---

### 92. RAG Risk Identification

For Retrieval-Augmented Generation systems:

```text
User
 ↓
Query
 ↓
Retriever
 ↓
Knowledge Base
 ↓
Retrieved Content
 ↓
LLM
 ↓
Response
```

Potential risks include:

* Unauthorized retrieval.
* Poisoned documents.
* Incorrect retrieval.
* Outdated information.
* Sensitive document exposure.
* Access-control bypass.

---

### 93. AI Supply-Chain Identification

AI supply-chain risks may originate from:

* Foundation models.
* Open-source libraries.
* Datasets.
* APIs.
* Cloud providers.
* AI platforms.
* Model hosting providers.
* Software dependencies.

The organization should identify which external components are critical to the AI system.

---

### 94. Third-Party Model Risk Identification

When using an external model, ask:

* Who owns the model?
* Where is it hosted?
* What data does the provider receive?
* Can the provider change the model?
* How are updates communicated?
* What happens if the service is discontinued?
* Can the organization migrate to another provider?

These questions identify dependency and governance risks.

---

### 95. Regulatory Scenario Identification

Risk identification should consider plausible regulatory scenarios.

Examples:

```text

      ↓

      ↓
New Compliance Requirement
      ↓
Potential Non-Compliance
```

The organization should identify regulatory exposure early enough to manage it.

### 96. Stakeholder Scenario Analysis

For each stakeholder:

```text
Stakeholder
     ↓
Potential AI Effect
     ↓
Potential Harm
     ↓

```

Example:

```text
Customer
   ↓
Incorrect AI Decision
   ↓
Financial Harm
   ↓
Complaint / Regulatory Exposure
```

This broadens risk identification beyond technology.

---

### 97. Bow-Tie Thinking

A bow-tie model can connect causes, event, consequences, and controls.

```text
Causes                 Consequences
  \                       /
   \                     /
    →  RISK EVENT  ←
   /                     \
  /                       \
Preventive              Detective /

```

For example:

```text

        ↓

        ↓

```

Controls can then be mapped on both sides.

A practical workshop can follow:

The workshop should focus on identification rather than prematurely debating risk scores.

Useful questions include:

### 101. Avoiding Duplicate Risks

During identification, different teams may describe the same underlying scenario differently.

Example:

```text
Team A:
Unauthorized data access

Team B:
AI information leakage

Team C:
Confidentiality failure
```

The GRC analyst should determine whether these represent:

* The same risk.
* Related risks.
* Different risk scenarios.

Deduplication improves the quality of the risk register.

### 102. Risk Clustering

Related risks can be grouped.

Example:

```text

     ├── Prompt Leakage
     ├── RAG Exposure

     └── API Data Exposure
```

Clustering helps identify common underlying causes.

### 103. Risk Identification Completeness

A risk identification exercise should ask:

> **Have we considered the complete AI lifecycle and all material stakeholders?**

A completeness review can cover:

```text

✓ Technology
✓ Data
✓ Model
✓ Security
✓ Privacy
✓ Human
✓ Operational
✓ Third Party
✓ Legal
✓ Regulatory
✓ Strategic
```

This does not guarantee that every possible risk has been discovered, but it improves systematic coverage.

---

### 104. Risk Identification Quality Criteria

A good identified risk should be:

**Specific**

Clearly describes the scenario.

**Plausible**

Could reasonably occur.

**Relevant**

Connected to the AI system.

**Evidence-supported**

Based on architecture, process, data, threat information, or expert knowledge.

**Actionable**

Can eventually be addressed through risk treatment.

---

### 105. Poor vs Strong Risk Identification

**Poor:**

> AI could be dangerous.

**Better:**

> The AI assistant may generate inaccurate information.

**Strong:**

> The customer-facing AI assistant may generate materially inaccurate responses that users rely upon, resulting in incorrect customer actions and potential financial or reputational consequences.

The strong version provides enough context for subsequent analysis.

### 107. Identification Does Not Mean Every Risk Is Validated

At the identification stage, candidate risks may still require further investigation.

A risk may later be determined to be:

* Not applicable.
* Duplicate.
* Immaterial.
* Already adequately controlled.
* Incorrectly understood.

Therefore, identification should allow uncertainty without prematurely rejecting potential risks.

Re-identification may be triggered by:

For mature organizations:

```text

     ↓

     ↓

     ↓

   /     \
 Yes      No
 ↓         ↓
```

### 111. Part 2 Core Principle

The central principle of this part is:

> **AI risk identification should combine multiple analytical perspectives—including assets, processes, threats, vulnerabilities, data flows, dependencies, human interactions, failure modes, misuse scenarios, and lifecycle changes—to discover credible risk scenarios systematically.**

A mature GRC professional does not ask only:

> "What security risks does this AI system have?"

Instead, they ask:

> **"What could go wrong across the entire AI system, its environment, its users, its dependencies, and its lifecycle—and who or what could be affected?"**

# 06.03 — AI Risk Identification

## Part 3 — Advanced AI Risk Discovery, Scenario Development, and Enterprise Analysis

### 112. Risk Identification Beyond the AI Model

A common mistake is to treat the AI model as the entire risk surface.

In reality, an AI system is an ecosystem:

```text
User
 ↓
Interface
 ↓
Application
 ↓
AI Model
 ↓
Data / RAG
 ↓
Tools / APIs
 ↓
Enterprise Systems
 ↓
Business Process
 ↓
Customer / Stakeholder
```

Risk identification should examine the entire chain.

A technically secure model can still create significant risk because of:

* Poor application design.
* Excessive permissions.
* Inappropriate data.
* Weak human oversight.
* Poor business processes.
* Third-party dependencies.

---

### 113. System Boundary Analysis

Before identifying risks, establish the boundary of the AI system.

Ask:

> **What is inside the organization's control, and what is outside it?**

Example:

```text
┌──────────────── Enterprise ────────────────┐
│                                             │
│ User → Application → AI Gateway             │
│                    ↓                        │
│                 Enterprise Data             │
│                                             │
└───────────────────┬─────────────────────────┘
                    │
               Trust Boundary
                    ↓
             External AI Provider
```

The boundary helps identify where organizational controls end and third-party exposure begins.

---

### 114. Internal vs External Risk Sources

Risk sources can be classified as:

**Internal**

* Employees.
* Administrators.
* Developers.
* Internal processes.
* Internal data.
* Configuration.

**External**

* Customers.
* Attackers.
* AI providers.
* Cloud providers.
* Regulators.
* External data sources.

This classification helps establish appropriate risk scenarios.

---

### 115. Direct vs Indirect AI Risks

Some risks originate directly from AI behavior.

Example:

> The model generates an incorrect prediction.

Other risks arise indirectly.

Example:

```text
AI Output
   ↓
Human Trust
   ↓
Incorrect Decision
   ↓
Business Impact
```

The AI did not directly perform the final action, but its output contributed to the consequence.

Both types should be identified.

---

### 116. First-Order and Second-Order Effects

AI risk identification should consider immediate and downstream effects.

**First-order effect:**

> AI generates an incorrect recommendation.

**Second-order effect:**

> Employee accepts the recommendation.

**Third-order effect:**

> Customer receives an incorrect decision.

**Enterprise consequence:**

> Financial, legal, and reputational exposure.

This is particularly important for decision-support systems.

---

### 117. Cascading Risk Analysis

Some AI risks propagate through interconnected systems.

Example:

```text
Model Error
    ↓
Incorrect API Response
    ↓
Incorrect Business Transaction
    ↓
Customer Impact
    ↓
Regulatory Complaint
    ↓
Reputational Damage
```

The initial risk may appear small when examined in isolation.

Its downstream effects may be significant.

---

### 118. Risk Propagation

Risk can propagate across organizational boundaries.

For example:

```text
Third-Party Model Failure
        ↓
Enterprise AI Failure
        ↓
Business Process Disruption
        ↓
Customer Service Impact
```

Therefore, risk identification should not stop at the immediate AI component.

---

### 119. Risk Interdependency Mapping

Related risks can be represented as a network:

```text
Data Quality
     ↓
Model Accuracy
     ↓
Decision Quality
     ↓
Customer Impact
     ↓
Reputational Risk
```

Another path may be:

```text
Weak IAM
     ↓
Unauthorized Access
     ↓
Data Exposure
     ↓
Privacy Impact
```

Mapping relationships can help identify systemic exposure.

---

### 120. Single Points of Failure

Risk identification should identify AI components whose failure can cause disproportionate consequences.

Examples:

* Single model provider.
* Single AI gateway.
* Single data source.
* Single authentication service.
* Single critical API.

A useful question is:

> **If this component becomes unavailable or compromised, how much of the AI capability is affected?**

---

### 121. Concentration Risk

AI systems can create concentration risk when many business processes depend on the same provider.

Example:

```text
                  AI Provider
                 /     |     \
                /      |      \
        Customer   HR AI    Finance AI
          AI
```

A provider-level incident could therefore affect multiple business functions simultaneously.

---

### 122. Common-Cause Failure

Different AI systems may appear independent but share the same underlying dependency.

Example:

```text
AI System A ─┐
AI System B ─┼──→ Same Cloud Provider
AI System C ─┘
```

A single provider outage can affect all three.

This is a common-cause risk.

---

### 123. Hidden Dependency Identification

Organizations should look beyond documented dependencies.

Examples:

* Shared identity services.
* Shared cloud infrastructure.
* Shared foundation models.
* Shared datasets.
* Shared APIs.
* Shared monitoring platforms.

The objective is to identify dependencies that could create correlated failures.

---

### 124. AI Risk Identification Through Business Process Mapping

Map the AI capability to the actual business process.

Example:

```text
Customer Application
       ↓
AI Fraud Screening
       ↓
Risk Classification
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Approval / Rejection
```

Then ask:

> Where could AI failure change the outcome of the business process?

This often reveals risks that architecture diagrams alone cannot identify.

---

### 125. Decision-Point Analysis

Every AI-supported decision should be examined.

Questions:

* What decision is being influenced?
* How much authority does AI have?
* Can humans override the result?
* What evidence supports the decision?
* What happens if the AI is wrong?
* Is the decision reversible?

The higher the decision consequence, the deeper the risk identification should be.

---

### 126. Irreversible Action Analysis

Some AI actions are easy to reverse.

Others are difficult or impossible to reverse.

Compare:

```text
Low consequence:
Generate a draft email

Higher consequence:
Send the email automatically

Very high consequence:
Execute a financial transaction
```

Risk identification should consider reversibility.

---

### 127. Autonomy Risk Identification

AI autonomy should be treated as an important risk dimension.

A simplified model:

```text
Information
    ↓
Recommendation
    ↓
Decision Support
    ↓
Decision
    ↓
Action
    ↓
Autonomous Action
```

As AI moves toward autonomous action, additional failure scenarios may become possible.

---

### 128. Authorization Boundary Analysis

An AI agent should operate within a clearly defined authorization boundary.

Ask:

> **What is the maximum action this AI is technically capable of performing?**

Then compare:

> **What is the maximum action it should be allowed to perform?**

A gap between the two represents a potential risk.

---

### 129. Privilege Creep in AI Systems

AI systems can accumulate permissions over time.

Example:

```text

Read CRM

Later:
Read CRM
Write CRM
Send Email

Later:
Read CRM
Write CRM
Send Email
Create Customer
Delete Customer
```

Risk identification should consider whether permissions remain aligned with the original purpose.

---

### 130. Prompt-to-Privilege Analysis

For agentic AI, examine the relationship between input and authority.

```text
User Prompt
     ↓
AI Interpretation
     ↓
Tool Selection
     ↓
Permission Check
     ↓
External Action
```

A risk may exist if a relatively unrestricted prompt can indirectly cause a high-impact action.

---

### 131. Tool-Chain Risk

An AI agent may use multiple tools sequentially.

Example:

```text
Search Tool
    ↓
Database Tool
    ↓
Email Tool
    ↓
Transaction Tool
```

Even if each individual tool is appropriately controlled, the combination may create unexpected capabilities.

Therefore:

> **Risk identification should examine the capability created by the tool chain, not only individual tools.**

---

### 132. Emergent Behavior Consideration

Complex AI systems may produce behavior that was not explicitly designed.

For example:

```text
Model
 +
Memory
 +
Tools
 +
Instructions
 +
External Data
      ↓
Unexpected System Behavior
```

Risk identification should consider plausible emergent behavior where system complexity makes it relevant.

---

### 133. Interaction Risk

An AI component may be safe in isolation but risky when combined with another component.

Example:

```text
LLM
 +
RAG
 +
External API
 +
Autonomous Execution
      ↓
New Risk Scenario
```

This is an important distinction between **component-level** and **system-level** risk.

---

### 134. Contextual Risk

The same AI capability can create different risks depending on context.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation
```

In:

```text
Marketing
→ relatively limited consequence
```

In:

```text
Medical Decision Support
→ potentially significant human consequence
```

Therefore, AI risk identification cannot rely solely on technology classification.

---

### 135. Intended vs Actual Use

There can be a gap between what the organization approves and what users actually do.

```text
Intended Use
     ↓
Approved Use Case
     ↓
Actual User Behavior
     ↓
Potential Misuse
```

Risk identification should consider reasonably foreseeable deviations from intended use.

---

### 136. Function Creep

An AI system may gradually acquire additional uses.

Example:

```text
Original:
Document Summarization

Later:
Employee Evaluation

Later:
Performance Ranking
```

Each additional use can create new risks.

Function creep should therefore trigger risk re-identification.

---

### 137. Use-Case Expansion

When an AI system moves into a new business process, reassess:

* Purpose.
* Data.
* Users.
* Stakeholders.
* Decision authority.
* Regulatory context.
* Impact.

A previously acceptable risk profile may no longer apply.

---

### 138. Geographic Expansion

AI deployment across countries can introduce new risk scenarios.

Example:

```text
Country A
   ↓
AI System
   ↓
Country B
   ↓
Different Users
Different Laws
Different Data Requirements
```

Risk identification should account for the new operating environment.

---

### 139. Population Expansion

An AI system initially used by ten employees may later be deployed to:

* 1,000 employees.
* Customers.
* Public users.
* Multiple countries.

The scale of exposure changes even if the underlying technology remains unchanged.

---

### 140. Scale-Based Risk Discovery

Risk identification should ask:

> **What happens if the AI system operates at 10× or 100× its current volume?**

A minor error rate can become material at large scale.

Example:

```text
0.1% error rate
×
1,000 transactions
=
1 error

0.1% error rate
×
1,000,000 transactions
=
1,000 errors
```

Scale can therefore transform operational risk.

---

### 141. Speed-Based Risk Identification

AI can execute decisions much faster than humans.

This can amplify consequences.

Example:

```text
Human Error
→ 10 decisions/hour

AI Error
→ 10,000 decisions/hour
```

The same error probability can create dramatically different exposure.

---

### 142. Automation Amplification

Automation can amplify both benefits and risks.

```text
AI Error
    ↓
Automation
    ↓
Repeated Automatically
    ↓
Large-Scale Impact
```

Risk identification should therefore consider the AI system's execution speed and automation level.

---

### 143. Feedback Loop Risk

Some AI systems influence the data they later receive.

Example:

```text
AI Decision
   ↓
Human Behavior Changes
   ↓
New Data
   ↓
Model Retraining
   ↓
AI Decision
```

This feedback loop can create unexpected changes in system behavior.

---

### 144. Self-Reinforcing Risk

An AI system can potentially reinforce an existing pattern.

Example:

```text
Historical Bias
     ↓
AI Recommendation
     ↓
Operational Decision
     ↓
New Historical Data
     ↓
Future Model Training
     ↓
Bias Reinforced
```

Risk identification should recognize these feedback mechanisms where relevant.

---

### 145. Information Asymmetry

AI can create an information imbalance between the organization and affected users.

For example, users may not know:

* That AI is being used.
* How AI affects them.
* What information the AI uses.
* How to challenge an AI-assisted decision.

These circumstances can create governance and stakeholder risks.

---

### 146. Transparency Risk Identification

Ask:

> **What information should stakeholders know about the AI system?**

Potential concerns include:

* AI involvement.
* Decision logic.
* Limitations.
* Human oversight.
* Data use.
* Appeal mechanisms.

The appropriate level of transparency depends on context and applicable requirements.

---

### 147. Explainability Risk

Some AI decisions may be difficult to explain.

Risk identification should ask:

> Can the organization explain why the AI produced this outcome at the level required for the use case?

This is especially important when AI affects significant decisions.

---

### 148. Traceability Risk

AI systems may require traceability of:

```text
Input
 ↓
Data
 ↓
Model Version
 ↓
Prompt / Configuration
 ↓
Output
 ↓
Human Decision
 ↓
Final Action
```

If this chain cannot be reconstructed, investigations and accountability can become difficult.

---

### 149. Reproducibility Risk

Some AI outputs may not be easily reproduced because of:

* Model changes.
* Randomness.
* Dynamic external data.
* Changing prompts.
* Changing system instructions.
* Provider updates.

Risk identification should consider whether reproducibility is important for the use case.

---

### 150. Version-Change Risk

AI providers may change:

* Model versions.
* Safety mechanisms.
* APIs.
* Performance characteristics.
* Data-processing behavior.

A previously assessed AI system can therefore change without the organization deliberately changing its own application.

---

### 151. Uncontrolled Model Update

A key question is:

> **Can the AI provider change the model without the organization's approval?**

If yes, the organization should identify the possibility of:

```text
Provider Update
      ↓
Model Behavior Change
      ↓
Previously Validated Behavior Changes
      ↓
New Risk
```

---

### 152. Configuration Drift

Risk identification should also consider configuration changes.

Examples:

* Permissions changed.
* Logging disabled.
* Prompt modified.
* Safety filter changed.
* API endpoint changed.
* Data source changed.

Configuration drift can create risks without changing the model itself.

---

### 153. Monitoring Blind Spots

An AI system may have monitoring for infrastructure but not for AI behavior.

Example:

```text
Infrastructure Monitoring
✓ CPU
✓ Memory
✓ Availability

AI Behavior Monitoring
✗ Accuracy
✗ Bias
✗ Drift
✗ Unsafe Output
```

This represents a potential identification gap.

---

### 154. Detection Delay

Risk identification should ask:

> **How quickly would the organization know that something has gone wrong?**

Compare:

```text
Incident detected immediately
        vs.
Incident discovered after six months
```

The latter can produce substantially greater consequences.

---

### 155. Response Capability

Identification should also consider whether the organization can respond.

Questions include:

* Can the AI be disabled?
* Can access be revoked?
* Can outputs be corrected?
* Can transactions be reversed?
* Can the model be rolled back?
* Can affected users be identified?

A system that cannot be quickly contained may have higher potential exposure.

---

### 156. Kill-Switch Risk

For autonomous systems, identify whether there is an effective emergency shutdown mechanism.

Example:

```text
AI Agent
   ↓
Unexpected Behavior
   ↓
Emergency Stop
   ↓
Actions Halted
```

If no practical shutdown exists, this can become a significant risk scenario.

---

### 157. Recovery Analysis

Risk identification should extend beyond the initial failure.

Ask:

> **What happens after the incident?**

Consider:

* Data recovery.
* Model restoration.
* Transaction reversal.
* Customer remediation.
* Regulatory notification.
* Root-cause analysis.
* Evidence preservation.

Recovery limitations can create additional consequences.

---

### 158. Risk Identification Through Incident Simulation

Tabletop exercises can reveal risks that documentation does not identify.

Example scenario:

> "The AI agent begins sending incorrect customer communications to thousands of users."

Participants determine:

```text

 ↓
Containment
 ↓
Investigation
 ↓
Communication
 ↓
Recovery
```

Each difficulty encountered can reveal an additional risk.

---

### 159. Red-Team-Informed Risk Identification

AI red teaming can expose realistic risk scenarios.

Examples:

* Prompt injection.
* Data leakage.
* Jailbreak attempts.
* Unauthorized tool use.
* Malicious document retrieval.
* Unsafe output generation.

The findings should feed back into the enterprise risk identification process.

---

### 160. Security Testing as Risk Discovery

Security tests are not only control-validation exercises.

They can also discover previously unknown risks.

For example:

```text

     ↓
Unexpected AI Behavior
     ↓
New Risk Scenario
     ↓
Risk Register
```

This demonstrates why risk identification should remain iterative.

---

### 161. Privacy Testing as Risk Discovery

Privacy assessments can reveal:

* Unexpected data collection.
* Excessive retention.
* Unauthorized processing.
* Data leakage.
* Unclear data flows.

These findings should feed into the AI risk register.

---

### 162. Model Validation as Risk Discovery

Model validation can reveal:

* Performance weaknesses.
* Data limitations.
* Bias.
* Instability.
* Unexpected failure modes.

These are candidate risks that should be documented before deployment.

---

### 163. User Testing as Risk Discovery

Real users can reveal risks that technical teams may not anticipate.

Examples:

* Users misunderstand AI outputs.
* Users over-trust recommendations.
* Users discover unintended uses.
* Users find confusing workflows.
* Users bypass controls.

User behavior is therefore an important source of risk intelligence.

---

### 164. Red-Flag Conditions

Certain conditions should trigger deeper risk identification.

Examples:

* High autonomy.
* Sensitive data.
* External users.
* Critical business process.
* Regulated decision.
* Financial transactions.
* Safety implications.
* Large-scale deployment.
* Significant third-party dependency.

These conditions indicate that a simple checklist may not be sufficient.

---

### 165. Risk Identification Depth

Not every AI system requires the same level of analysis.

A practical approach is:

```text
Low Impact AI
→ Basic Identification

Moderate Impact AI
→ Structured Assessment

High Impact AI
→ Multidisciplinary Analysis

Critical / Highly Autonomous AI
→ Deep Scenario + Threat + Impact Analysis
```

Risk identification effort should be proportional to potential consequence.

---

### 166. Evidence Triangulation

Strong risk identification can use multiple evidence sources.

For example:

```text

       +
Interview
       +
Security Test
       +
Incident Data
       ↓
Confirmed Risk Scenario
```

When multiple sources point to the same issue, confidence in the identified risk increases.

---

### 167. Risk Identification Confidence

Organizations can optionally record confidence:

```text

Medium
Low
```

For example:

> "Potential data leakage through an AI provider."

If contractual and technical evidence is incomplete, confidence may initially be medium or low.

Further investigation can increase confidence.

---

### 168. Unknown Risk

Some risks cannot be fully understood at the time of identification.

This is especially relevant to emerging AI technology.

Instead of ignoring uncertainty, document it.

Example:

> "Potential unexpected model behavior under previously unseen inputs; behavior not sufficiently characterized."

This allows the organization to manage uncertainty explicitly.

---

### 169. Unknown-Unknowns

No risk-identification methodology can guarantee discovery of every possible AI failure.

Therefore, mature organizations combine:

```text

+
Emerging Risks
+
Threat Intelligence
+
Monitoring
+
Testing
+
Incident Learning
```

This creates resilience against risks that were not initially anticipated.

```text

   ≠

   ≠

   ≠

```

### 181. Part 3 Core Principle

The key principle is:

> **Advanced AI risk identification looks beyond the model itself and examines the complete socio-technical system: architecture, data, users, permissions, dependencies, business processes, automation, human behavior, lifecycle changes, and downstream consequences.**

The most important question is not simply:

> **"Can the AI fail?"**

It is:

> **"If the AI behaves incorrectly, unexpectedly, or maliciously, how can that behavior propagate through the surrounding system and ultimately affect people, assets, business processes, and organizational objectives?"**

# 06.03 — AI Risk Identification

## Part 4 — Enterprise Implementation, Governance, Documentation, and Practical Application

### 182. From Risk Discovery to an Enterprise Risk Record

The final objective of AI risk identification is to transform observations into **structured, traceable, and governable risk records**.

A mature process can be represented as:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Context Collection
    ↓
Risk Discovery
    ↓
Risk Scenario Development
    ↓
Validation
    ↓
Risk Documentation
    ↓
Risk Register
```

The output should be understandable to both technical and business stakeholders.

---

### 183. Establish the AI Risk Identification Scope

Before starting an assessment, formally define the scope.

The scope should identify:

* AI system.
* Business process.
* Organizational unit.
* Geographic scope.
* Data involved.
* Technology components.
* Third-party services.
* Relevant lifecycle stages.
* Assessment period.

For example:

> "The assessment covers the customer-service AI assistant, including its application layer, model provider, enterprise knowledge base, supporting APIs, customer interactions, and associated business processes."

A clearly defined scope prevents important components from being unintentionally excluded.

---

### 184. Establish the Risk Identification Team

AI risk identification should normally be multidisciplinary.

A team may include:

| Role             | Contribution                      |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Business Owner   | Business purpose and consequences |
| AI/ML Engineer   | Model behavior and limitations    |
| Cybersecurity    | Threats and vulnerabilities       |
| Privacy          | Personal-data risks               |
| Legal/Compliance | Regulatory exposure               |
| IT/Cloud         | Infrastructure and dependencies   |
| GRC              | Risk methodology and governance   |
| Domain Expert    | Business-specific consequences    |

The exact composition should be proportional to the AI system's complexity and impact.

---

### 185. Assign Risk Identification Responsibilities

Responsibilities should be clear.

For example:

```text
Business Owner
    → Defines intended use

AI Team
    → Explains model and technical behavior

Security
    → Identifies security threats

Privacy
    → Identifies privacy exposure

GRC
    → Coordinates methodology and documentation
```

This avoids the assumption that cybersecurity alone owns AI risk.

---

### 186. Define the Risk Identification Criteria

Before workshops begin, establish what qualifies as an identifiable risk.

Criteria may include:

* Reasonably foreseeable.
* Relevant to the AI system.
* Supported by evidence or credible assumptions.
* Capable of affecting objectives.
* Connected to a threat, vulnerability, condition, or failure mode.
* Potentially consequential.

This improves consistency between different assessments.

---

### 187. Create an AI Risk Identification Worksheet

A practical worksheet may contain:

| Field           | Description            |
| --------------- | ---------------------- |
| Risk ID         | Unique identifier      |
| AI System       | System being assessed  |
| Use Case        | Intended purpose       |
| Lifecycle Stage | Where the risk occurs  |
| Risk Source     | Origin of the risk     |
| Cause           | Why it could occur     |
| Threat          | Trigger or actor       |
| Vulnerability   | Weakness or condition  |
| Risk Event      | What could happen      |
| Asset           | What could be affected |
| Stakeholder     | Who could be affected  |
| Consequence     | Potential result       |
| Evidence        | Supporting information |
| Owner           | Responsible party      |
| Status          | Identification status  |

At this stage, likelihood and impact scores can remain blank.

---

### 188. Use Unique Risk IDs

Every material risk should receive a unique identifier.

Example:

```text
AI-R-001
AI-R-002
AI-R-003
```

A more structured identifier might be:

```text
AI-SEC-001
AI-PRV-002
AI-OPS-003
AI-MDL-004
```

Unique IDs support:

* Traceability.
* Reporting.
* Audit.
* Treatment tracking.
* Risk aggregation.

---

### 189. Maintain Risk Scenario Traceability

Each risk should be traceable back to its source.

Example:

```text
Architecture Diagram
        ↓
AI-R-004
        ↓
Risk Register
        ↓
Risk Treatment
        ↓
Control
        ↓
Residual Risk
```

This allows an auditor or management reviewer to understand where the risk originated.

---

### 190. Distinguish Risk From Issue

A **risk** represents a potential future event or consequence.

An **issue** represents an existing condition or problem.

Example:

**Risk:**

> The AI may expose confidential information through inappropriate retrieval.

**Issue:**

> Testing has already confirmed that unauthorized users can retrieve confidential documents.

The issue may become evidence supporting the risk.

Keeping the concepts separate improves GRC reporting.

---

### 191. Distinguish Risk From Control Gap

A control gap is not automatically the risk itself.

Example:

**Control gap:**

> AI output monitoring is not implemented.

**Risk:**

> Harmful or materially inaccurate AI outputs may remain undetected and be used in business decisions.

This distinction is important because controls address risks; the control deficiency is not necessarily the risk statement.

---

### 192. Distinguish Risk From Threat

A threat is something capable of causing harm.

Example:

```text
Threat:
Malicious User

Vulnerability:
Weak Prompt Filtering

Risk Event:
Prompt Injection

Consequence:
Unauthorized Data Disclosure
```

These concepts should not be collapsed into one generic statement.

---

### 193. Distinguish Risk From Consequence

A consequence is what happens after the risk event.

Example:

```text
Weak Authorization
       ↓
Unauthorized Retrieval
       ↓
Confidential Data Disclosure
```

The disclosure is the consequence, not the entire risk.

A complete risk scenario captures the causal relationship.

---

### 194. Risk Statement Construction

A useful risk statement can follow:

> **Because of [cause/condition], [risk event] may occur, resulting in [consequence].**

Example:

> Because access controls are insufficiently enforced within the AI retrieval layer, unauthorized users may obtain restricted information, resulting in confidentiality and regulatory exposure.

This creates a clear connection between cause, event, and consequence.

---

### 195. Risk Scenario Quality Test

A documented risk should pass five questions:

1. **What can happen?**
2. **Why can it happen?**
3. **What would be affected?**
4. **Who could be affected?**
5. **What could happen afterward?**

If these cannot be answered, the risk statement may require refinement.

---

### 196. Avoid "Everything Is a Risk"

AI assessments can generate extremely long lists.

Not every theoretical possibility should become a formal enterprise risk.

For example:

> "The AI might someday behave unexpectedly."

This is too broad.

Instead, identify a credible scenario:

> "The AI may generate materially inaccurate customer instructions that are automatically distributed without human verification."

The second scenario is more useful for governance.

---

### 197. Avoid Risk Inflation

Risk identification should not intentionally produce large numbers of risks simply to demonstrate assessment activity.

Risk inflation creates:

* Risk-register overload.
* Duplicate risks.
* Poor prioritization.
* Management fatigue.
* Inefficient treatment.

The objective is **quality and coverage**, not quantity.

---

### 198. Avoid Risk Suppression

The opposite problem is also dangerous.

Teams may avoid documenting risks because:

* They fear management attention.
* They believe the risk is unlikely.
* They assume controls eliminate it.
* They do not want additional remediation work.

A risk should be documented when it is materially credible, even if existing controls reduce its likelihood.

---

### 199. Gross Risk vs Controlled Risk

Risk identification should not confuse the existence of controls with the absence of risk.

Example:

```text
Risk Exists
     ↓
Preventive Controls
     ↓
Detective Controls
     ↓
Corrective Controls
```

Controls influence the eventual risk level.

They do not necessarily mean the underlying risk does not exist.

---

### 200. Capture Existing Controls Separately

During identification, existing controls may be recorded as context.

For example:

```text
Risk:
Unauthorized AI data retrieval

Existing Controls:
- RBAC
- Data classification
- API authorization
- Logging
```

However, the risk should remain distinguishable from its controls.

---

### 201. Identify Control Dependencies

Some risks exist because a control depends on another control.

Example:

```text
AI Authorization
      ↓
Depends on
      ↓
Identity Provider
      ↓
Depends on
      ↓
Directory Service
```

If the dependency fails, the primary control may also fail.

This creates an additional risk dimension.

---

### 202. Identify Control Failure Scenarios

For each critical control, ask:

> **What happens if this control fails?**

Example:

```text
Output Filter
     ↓
Filter Failure
     ↓
Harmful AI Output
     ↓
Customer Exposure
```

This is particularly useful for AI systems that depend on layered controls.

---

### 203. Defense-in-Depth Analysis

AI systems should not rely on a single control.

Example:

```text
User Authentication
       ↓
Authorization
       ↓
Input Validation
       ↓
Model Guardrails
       ↓
Output Filtering
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Monitoring
```

Risk identification should consider the possibility that one or more layers fail.

---

### 204. Identify Control Bypass

Controls can sometimes be bypassed rather than directly defeated.

Example:

```text
Normal Path
User → AI → Approved API

Alternative Path
User → AI → Indirect Tool → Sensitive Data
```

Risk identification should therefore consider alternative paths through the system.

---

### 205. Identify Unintended Data Paths

An AI system may have data paths that were not part of the original design.

Examples:

* Logs containing prompts.
* Debugging records.
* Analytics platforms.
* Backup systems.
* Provider telemetry.
* Error messages.

These paths can introduce additional exposure.

### 206. AI Logging Risk Discovery

* User prompts.

* System instructions.
* Credentials.
* Sensitive business information.

Therefore:

Risk identification should examine what AI-related information is recorded and where it is stored.

### 207. Monitoring Data Risk

AI monitoring may require collecting:

* Prompts.
* Outputs.
* User identifiers.
* Model metrics.
* Behavioral data.

The monitoring system can therefore introduce secondary privacy and security risks.

This demonstrates why risk identification must consider **supporting systems**, not only the AI application.

### 224. Risk Ownership

* Allocate resources.

The AI engineer is not automatically the risk owner.

Risk ownership should normally align with the business responsibility affected by the risk.

### 227. Risk Identification and Lessons Learned

4. Was the risk underestimated?
5. Was there a missing risk?
6. Did a dependency create the event?
7. Does another AI system have the same exposure?

Lessons learned should be applied across the AI portfolio where relevant.

* No business owners.
* No evidence.
* Technical-only assessments.

### 242. GRC Professional's Role

A GRC professional does not need to personally perform every technical test.

The GRC role is to ensure that:

* The methodology is defined.
* Relevant stakeholders participate.
* Risk scenarios are documented consistently.
* Evidence exists.
* Risk ownership is established.
* Material risks are escalated.
* Risk identification connects to enterprise governance.
* The process is repeatable and auditable.

This is an important distinction between **technical execution** and **risk governance**.

### 243. Executive-Level Risk Communication

Executives generally do not need the full technical attack path.

Instead, communicate:

```text

   ↓
Business Consequence
   ↓
Exposure
   ↓
Current Position
   ↓
Required Decision
```

Example:

This translates technical findings into business language.

### 248. Part 4 Core Principle

> **Enterprise AI risk identification transforms technical observations, business-process analysis, stakeholder concerns, threat intelligence, testing results, and lifecycle information into structured, evidence-based risk scenarios that can be governed through the organization's formal risk-management framework.**

The goal is not to produce the longest possible risk register.

The goal is to produce a **complete, credible, traceable, and actionable representation of material AI risks**.

AI risk identification answers the foundational question:

A comprehensive approach examines:

```text
    ↓

    ↓
Data
    ↓

    ↓
Users
    ↓
Stakeholders
    ↓
Threats
    ↓
Vulnerabilities
    ↓

    ↓

    ↓
Misuse
    ↓
Human Interaction
    ↓
Business Processes
    ↓
```
