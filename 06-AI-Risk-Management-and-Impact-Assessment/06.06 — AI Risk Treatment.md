# 06.06 — AI Risk Treatment

## Part 1 — Foundations, Purpose, Principles, and Treatment Context

### 459. Introduction to AI Risk Treatment

AI risk treatment is the process of **selecting and implementing measures to modify AI risk**.

Once an AI risk has been identified, analyzed, and evaluated, the organization needs to determine what should be done with that risk.

The fundamental question is:

> **"What action should we take to modify this AI risk to an acceptable level?"**

Risk treatment may involve:

* Reducing likelihood.
* Reducing consequences.
* Changing the AI system.
* Changing the way the AI system is used.
* Sharing the risk.
* Avoiding the activity.
* Accepting the remaining risk.

The treatment decision should be based on the organization's risk criteria and governance requirements.

---

### 460. Purpose of AI Risk Treatment

The primary purpose of risk treatment is to bring risk to a level that is **consistent with organizational requirements and decision-making boundaries**.

Conceptually:

```text id="x3d8k2"
Identified Risk
      ↓
Risk Analysis
      ↓
Risk Evaluation
      ↓
Treatment Decision
      ↓
Modified Risk
```

Treatment therefore connects risk evaluation with practical action.

---

### 461. Risk Treatment Is Not Risk Elimination

A common misconception is:

> "Risk treatment means eliminating the risk."

That is not necessarily true.

Many AI risks cannot be completely eliminated.

For example:

> An AI model may always have some probability of generating an incorrect output.

Treatment may instead reduce the probability or consequences to an acceptable level.

```text id="g3f7k9"
Initial Risk
     ↓
Treatment
     ↓
Lower Residual Risk
```

---

### 462. Inherent Risk and Residual Risk

Risk treatment operates between inherent and residual risk.

```text id="2y9j8v"
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Risk Treatment
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

**Inherent risk**

> The level of risk before considering controls or treatment.

**Residual risk**

> The risk remaining after treatment and controls are considered.

The treatment process should therefore demonstrate how the organization moves from the initial exposure toward the desired risk level.

---

### 463. Treatment Objective

The objective should be explicit.

For example:

> "Reduce the risk of unauthorized disclosure of confidential information from High to Moderate."

This is more useful than:

> "Improve security."

A good treatment objective is:

* Specific.
* Risk-focused.
* Measurable where possible.
* Linked to the risk evaluation.

---

### 464. Risk Treatment Options

At a high level, organizations can choose among several approaches:

```text id="p8x6u3"
        AI Risk
           ↓
 ┌─────────┼─────────┐
 ↓         ↓         ↓
Modify    Share     Avoid
Risk      Risk      Risk
 ↓
Accept Remaining Risk
```

The exact terminology can vary by framework, but the fundamental idea is to deliberately determine how the risk will be handled.

---

### 465. Risk Modification

Risk modification means changing the risk characteristics.

This may involve:

* Reducing likelihood.
* Reducing consequence.
* Increasing detectability.
* Increasing containment capability.
* Reducing exposure.
* Limiting scale.
* Reducing autonomy.

For AI systems, risk modification is often the most practical treatment approach.

---

### 466. Example of Risk Modification

Suppose:

> An AI agent can independently execute financial transactions.

Potential treatment:

> Require human approval for transactions above a defined threshold.

The AI system still exists, but its risk characteristics change.

```text id="d3k6s1"
Unrestricted AI Action
        ↓
Treatment
        ↓
Human Approval Threshold
        ↓
Reduced Consequence / Exposure
```

---

### 467. Preventive Treatment

Preventive treatment attempts to reduce the probability that a risk event will occur.

Examples include:

* Strong authentication.
* Authorization.
* Input validation.
* Data filtering.
* Secure configuration.
* Model restrictions.
* Prompt controls.
* Tool restrictions.
* Segmentation.

---

### 468. Detective Treatment

Detective measures identify harmful behavior or outcomes.

Examples:

* AI activity monitoring.
* Anomaly detection.
* Output monitoring.
* Security logging.
* Model-performance monitoring.
* Alerting.
* User reporting.

Detection does not necessarily prevent the initial event, but it can reduce the duration or consequences.

---

### 469. Corrective Treatment

Corrective treatment addresses consequences after an event.

Examples:

* AI service shutdown.
* Model rollback.
* Transaction reversal.
* Account suspension.
* Data restoration.
* Incident response.
* Corrective model configuration.

---

### 470. Treatment Can Address Likelihood or Consequence

Risk treatment does not always need to prevent the event.

Consider:

> AI generates an incorrect recommendation.

Treatment could:

**Reduce likelihood**

> Improve model validation.

or:

**Reduce consequence**

> Require human approval before the recommendation becomes an actual decision.

This distinction is important.

---

### 471. Layered Risk Treatment

For significant AI risks, multiple treatments may be required.

Example:

```text id="h6s2s9"
Access Control
      ↓
Data Filtering
      ↓
Output Validation
      ↓
Human Review
      ↓
Monitoring
      ↓
Incident Response
```

This creates layered protection rather than depending on a single control.

---

### 472. Defense in Depth

Defense in depth is particularly important when AI can:

* Access sensitive information.
* Execute actions.
* Interact with external systems.
* Affect customers.
* Make consequential recommendations.

A single control failure should not automatically produce a catastrophic outcome where practical.

---

### 473. Risk Treatment Hierarchy

Organizations can consider treatment options in a logical order:

```text id="7x5v2q"
Can the risky activity be eliminated?
          ↓
If not,
Can the risk be reduced?
          ↓
If not,
Can exposure be limited?
          ↓
Can consequences be reduced?
          ↓
Can the risk be shared?
          ↓
Can the remaining risk be accepted?
```

The appropriate approach depends on context.

---

### 474. Treatment Selection

Treatment selection should consider:

* Risk significance.
* Risk appetite.
* Treatment effectiveness.
* Cost.
* Feasibility.
* Implementation time.
* Business impact.
* Technical constraints.
* Legal requirements.
* Operational consequences.
* Residual risk.

The cheapest treatment is not automatically the best treatment.

---

### 475. Cost-Benefit Consideration

Suppose:

> Treatment A costs €10,000 and reduces risk substantially.

> Treatment B costs €500,000 and provides only marginal additional risk reduction.

The organization may prefer Treatment A if it brings the risk within acceptable boundaries.

However, cost should not be used to justify violating mandatory requirements.

---

### 476. Treatment Effectiveness

A treatment should be evaluated according to how much risk it actually reduces.

Example:

```text id="b9k4s0"
Before Treatment
Likelihood = 4
Impact = 5

After Treatment
Likelihood = 2
Impact = 5
```

The treatment primarily reduced likelihood.

Another treatment might reduce consequence instead:

```text id="j8z1x5"
Before
Likelihood = 4
Impact = 5

After
Likelihood = 4
Impact = 3
```

Understanding which dimension changes is important.

---

### 477. Expected Treatment Outcome

Before implementing a treatment, the organization can define the expected outcome.

Example:

> "Implementing mandatory human approval is expected to reduce the potential impact of unauthorized AI actions from Severe to Moderate."

This creates a measurable treatment objective.

---

### 478. Treatment Feasibility

A theoretically effective treatment may not be practical.

Consider:

> "Have a human review every AI transaction."

If the AI processes:

> 100,000 transactions per hour,

manual review may not be operationally feasible.

Therefore, treatment selection should consider actual operating scale.

---

### 479. Automation and Treatment

AI treatment can itself involve automation.

For example:

> Automatically block AI requests that attempt to access restricted data.

Automation can provide:

* Speed.
* Consistency.
* Scalability.

But automated controls can also fail, so high-impact environments may require additional safeguards.

---

### 480. Human Oversight as Treatment

Human oversight can reduce AI risk when it is meaningful.

Effective oversight requires that the human can:

* Understand the relevant information.
* Challenge the AI output.
* Reject the recommendation.
* Modify the decision.
* Stop the process.
* Escalate when necessary.

Simply placing a person in an approval workflow does not automatically constitute effective risk treatment.

---

### 481. Limiting AI Autonomy

Reducing autonomy can be a powerful treatment.

Example:

```text id="x4q6u2"
Autonomous Action
       ↓
Treatment
       ↓
Recommendation Only
```

Instead of allowing AI to execute an action directly, the organization may require a human to approve the action.

---

### 482. Limiting Scope

Another treatment is reducing what the AI system can access or affect.

Examples:

* Restrict users.
* Restrict departments.
* Restrict data.
* Restrict geographic deployment.
* Restrict transaction values.
* Restrict available tools.
* Restrict operating hours.

This reduces the potential blast radius.

---

### 483. Reducing Blast Radius

Suppose an AI system can modify:

> 1 million customer records.

Treatment could limit it to:

> 10,000 records per batch.

If the AI makes an error, the maximum immediate impact is reduced.

```text id="p4n7y2"
Large Exposure
     ↓
Treatment
     ↓
Limited Exposure
     ↓
Smaller Potential Impact
```

---

### 484. Rate Limiting

Rate limiting can reduce the speed and scale of AI actions.

Examples:

* Maximum requests per minute.
* Maximum transactions per hour.
* Maximum records processed.
* Maximum automated actions.

This is particularly useful where rapid propagation is a major risk driver.

---

### 485. Permission Reduction

AI agents should receive only the permissions necessary for their intended function.

For example:

> A customer-service AI may need read access to customer information but not permission to delete accounts.

This is an application of least privilege to AI-enabled systems.

---

### 486. Tool Restriction

An AI agent may have access to tools such as:

* Databases.
* APIs.
* Email.
* File systems.
* Financial systems.
* Ticketing platforms.

Treatment can restrict which tools the AI can invoke.

Example:

```text id="6q4d9x"
AI Agent
   ↓
Allowed Tools
   ├── Knowledge Search ✓
   ├── Ticket Creation ✓
   ├── Account Deletion ✗
   └── Payment Execution ✗
```

---

### 487. Data Minimization as Treatment

Reducing the amount of data available to an AI system can reduce exposure.

Instead of:

> Full customer database.

Provide:

> Only the fields necessary for the specific task.

This can reduce the potential consequence of:

* Unauthorized access.
* Data leakage.
* Model misuse.

---

### 488. Data Segmentation

Data can be separated based on sensitivity.

Example:

```text id="o8q0r4"
Public Data
    ↓
Internal Data
    ↓
Confidential Data
    ↓
Highly Sensitive Data
```

AI access can then be restricted according to classification.

---

### 489. Environment Segmentation

AI systems can also be separated by environment.

Example:

```text id="m7x9u1"
Development
     ↓
Testing
     ↓
Pilot
     ↓
Production
```

High-risk AI capabilities should not automatically be available across all environments.

---

### 490. Model Restriction

Treatment can involve limiting the model itself.

Examples:

* Use a less autonomous model.
* Use a model with appropriate capabilities.
* Restrict model functionality.
* Disable unnecessary tools.
* Restrict context length where appropriate.
* Restrict external connectivity.

The objective is to align capability with the required business purpose.

---

### 491. Architecture Modification

Sometimes the best treatment is architectural.

Example:

Instead of:

```text id="p1d9g8"
User → AI → Production Database
```

use:

```text id="7k4f2m"
User
 ↓
AI
 ↓
Policy Enforcement Layer
 ↓
Validated API
 ↓
Production Database
```

The additional enforcement layer can reduce unauthorized or unsafe actions.

---

### 492. Guardrails

AI guardrails can constrain system behavior.

Examples:

* Input restrictions.
* Output restrictions.
* Tool authorization.
* Data access policies.
* Content filtering.
* Transaction thresholds.
* Human approval requirements.

Guardrails should be tested rather than assumed to be effective.

---

### 493. Validation as Treatment

Validation can occur at multiple stages:

```text id="z4u1t5"
Input Validation
      ↓
Model Processing
      ↓
Output Validation
      ↓
Action Validation
```

Each stage can address different risk drivers.

---

### 494. Output Validation

If AI output can create significant consequences, the organization may require validation before use.

Examples:

* Schema validation.
* Business-rule validation.
* Human review.
* Data consistency checks.
* Policy checks.

This is particularly useful when AI output feeds automated workflows.

---

### 495. Action Validation

A higher-risk AI agent may require validation before executing an action.

Example:

```text id="6y2h3k"
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Policy Check
       ↓
Authorization Check
       ↓
Human Approval
       ↓
Execution
```

This creates multiple decision gates.

---

### 496. Monitoring as Treatment

Monitoring can reduce risk by improving:

* Detection speed.
* Visibility.
* Incident response.
* Accountability.
* Control verification.

Examples:

* AI activity monitoring.
* Model performance monitoring.
* Security monitoring.
* Data-access monitoring.
* Anomaly detection.

---

### 497. Treatment and Detectability

Consider two identical AI systems.

**System A**

> Errors are detected immediately.

**System B**

> Errors may remain undetected for six months.

Even if the probability of failure is identical, the consequences may differ substantially.

Improving detectability can therefore be a meaningful treatment.

---

### 498. Incident Response as Treatment

AI-specific incident response should address scenarios such as:

* Prompt injection.
* Unauthorized AI actions.
* Data leakage.
* Model compromise.
* Model failure.
* Harmful outputs.
* Provider outage.

Treatment should consider whether the organization can respond quickly enough to limit consequences.

---

### 499. Kill Switch

For sufficiently autonomous systems, an emergency shutdown mechanism may be appropriate.

Conceptually:

```text id="j2t7s9"
AI Agent
   ↓
Unexpected Behavior
   ↓
Detection
   ↓
Emergency Stop
   ↓
Containment
```

The mechanism should be tested to ensure that it actually works.

---

### 500. Rollback Capability

If a model or configuration causes problems, the organization may need to revert to a previously validated state.

```text id="n3m5p8"
Current Model
     ↓
Problem
     ↓
Rollback
     ↓
Known-Good Configuration
```

Rollback capability can reduce recovery time and operational impact.

---

### 501. Resilience as Risk Treatment

For critical AI systems, resilience measures can include:

* Redundancy.
* Failover.
* Backup systems.
* Alternate providers.
* Manual fallback.
* Disaster recovery.

The treatment objective is to reduce the consequence of AI unavailability or failure.

---

### 502. Manual Fallback

A manual fallback process can reduce operational risk.

Example:

> If the AI claims-processing system becomes unavailable, trained employees can process critical claims manually.

However, the fallback must be realistic.

Questions include:

* Is sufficient staff available?
* Can they handle the volume?
* Are procedures documented?
* Has the process been tested?

---

### 503. Treatment Through Process Change

Sometimes technology does not need to change.

The organization can modify the business process.

Example:

Instead of:

> AI automatically rejects applications.

Change to:

> AI identifies potentially problematic applications for human review.

The technology remains similar, but the consequence of AI error is reduced.

---

### 504. Treatment Through Use-Case Restriction

An organization may restrict the AI system to lower-risk uses.

Example:

```text id="l3u8v2"
AI Capability
     ↓
Approved Uses
     ├── Internal Drafting ✓
     ├── Summarization ✓
     ├── Customer Decisions ✗
     └── Autonomous Transactions ✗
```

This can be an effective way to manage risk.

---

### 505. Treatment Through Deployment Phasing

Instead of full deployment:

```text id="a8v4j1"
Pilot
  ↓
Limited Production
  ↓
Expanded Production
```

The organization can gradually increase exposure as evidence and confidence improve.

---

### 506. Pilot as Risk Treatment

A controlled pilot can limit:

* Number of users.
* Data exposure.
* Transaction volume.
* AI autonomy.
* Geographic scope.

It also provides operational evidence before broader deployment.

---

### 507. Treatment and Business Objectives

Treatment should not unnecessarily destroy the business value of AI.

The objective is:

> **Reduce unacceptable risk while preserving as much legitimate business value as reasonably possible.**

For example:

> Rather than eliminating an AI assistant because it can access sensitive data, restrict it to approved datasets and approved users.

This is often more practical than complete prohibition.

---

### 508. Proportionality

Treatment should be proportionate to risk.

A low-risk internal AI tool may require:

* Basic access control.
* Standard monitoring.
* User guidance.

A high-impact AI system may require:

* Extensive testing.
* Multiple controls.
* Independent validation.
* Human oversight.
* Formal approval.
* Continuous monitoring.

---

### 509. Treatment Priority

Treatment priority can be based on:

* Risk severity.
* Regulatory importance.
* Potential human impact.
* Likelihood.
* Business criticality.
* Exposure.
* Treatment feasibility.
* Time sensitivity.

High-risk issues should generally receive greater urgency.

---

### 510. Treatment Effectiveness vs Cost

A useful decision principle is:

> **Select treatment that achieves the required risk reduction while remaining proportionate and feasible.**

However:

> Cost should not be the sole basis for ignoring a mandatory control or unacceptable risk.

---

### 511. Treatment Constraints

Treatment may be constrained by:

* Legacy architecture.
* Budget.
* Skills.
* Technology limitations.
* Vendor capabilities.
* Time.
* Business dependencies.
* Regulatory requirements.

These constraints should be documented rather than hidden.

---

### 512. Treatment Plan

A treatment plan should identify:

```text id="k6r4t2"
Risk
 ↓
Treatment Objective
 ↓
Treatment Action
 ↓
Control / Measure
 ↓
Owner
 ↓
Deadline
 ↓
Expected Risk Reduction
 ↓
Validation
```

This turns a governance decision into an executable plan.

---

### 513. Treatment Owner

Each treatment action should have a clearly assigned owner.

For example:

| Treatment                 | Owner               |
| ------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Implement authorization   | IAM Team            |
| Configure AI guardrails   | AI Engineering      |
| Define review process     | Business Owner      |
| Validate privacy controls | Privacy Team        |
| Implement monitoring      | Security Operations |

Accountability should be explicit.

---

### 514. Treatment Deadline

A treatment plan should include:

* Target completion date.
* Priority.
* Dependencies.
* Milestones.
* Validation date.

Without deadlines, treatment actions can remain indefinitely open.

---

### 515. Treatment Verification

Implementation is not the same as effectiveness.

Example:

> "Monitoring enabled."

This only proves implementation.

A stronger verification is:

> "Monitoring was tested using defined scenarios and successfully detected the specified AI-risk events."

The organization should verify that treatment actually reduces the intended risk.

---

### 516. Treatment Acceptance Criteria

Before implementation, define what success looks like.

Example:

> "AI transactions above €1,000 must require explicit human approval."

Testing can then determine whether the control works.

```text id="k7v3p1"
Treatment Requirement
       ↓
Control Implementation
       ↓
Control Testing
       ↓
Pass / Fail
```

---

### 517. Treatment and Residual Risk

After treatment:

```text id="q4t8w2"
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Treatment
     ↓
Residual Risk
     ↓
Risk Evaluation
```

The residual risk must be reassessed.

If it remains above acceptable boundaries, additional action may be required.

---

### 518. Treatment Iteration

Risk treatment is often iterative.

```text id="f2q8w6"
Treatment 1
   ↓
Residual Risk
   ↓
Still Too High
   ↓
Treatment 2
   ↓
Residual Risk
   ↓
Acceptable
```

This prevents organizations from assuming that one treatment will always be sufficient.

---

### 519. Treatment and Continuous Improvement

AI systems evolve.

Therefore, treatment should be reviewed when:

* The model changes.
* The data changes.
* The user population changes.
* Autonomy increases.
* New threats emerge.
* Controls fail.
* Incidents occur.
* Business use changes.

Risk treatment should remain aligned with the current operating environment.

---

### 520. Core Principle of AI Risk Treatment

The central principle is:

> **AI risk treatment is the deliberate selection and implementation of measures that modify AI risk so that the resulting exposure is consistent with organizational objectives, risk criteria, applicable requirements, and approved risk boundaries.**

Remember the basic relationship:

```text id="p6z4k1"
INHERENT RISK
      ↓
TREATMENT
      ↓
CONTROLS / PROCESS CHANGES
      ↓
RESIDUAL RISK
      ↓
RE-EVALUATION
```

A strong GRC professional does not simply ask:

> **"What control can we implement?"**

The better question is:

> **"What treatment will address the actual risk driver, how much risk reduction is expected, and how will we verify that the residual risk is acceptable?"**
