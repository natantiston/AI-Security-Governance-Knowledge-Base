# 06.06 — AI Risk Treatment

## Part 1 — Foundations, Purpose, Principles, and Treatment Context

### 459. Introduction to AI Risk Treatment

AI risk treatment is the process of **selecting and implementing measures to modify AI risk**.

Once an AI risk has been identified, analyzed, and evaluated, the organization needs to determine what should be done with that risk.

The fundamental question is:

> **"What action should we take to modify this AI risk to an acceptable level?"**

Risk treatment may involve:

* Reducing likelihood.
* Reducing consequences.
* Changing the AI system.
* Changing the way the AI system is used.
* Sharing the risk.
* Avoiding the activity.
* Accepting the remaining risk.

The treatment decision should be based on the organization's risk criteria and governance requirements.

---

### 460. Purpose of AI Risk Treatment

The primary purpose of risk treatment is to bring risk to a level that is **consistent with organizational requirements and decision-making boundaries**.

Conceptually:

```text id="x3d8k2"
Identified Risk
      ↓
Risk Analysis
      ↓
Risk Evaluation
      ↓
Treatment Decision
      ↓
Modified Risk
```

Treatment therefore connects risk evaluation with practical action.

---

### 461. Risk Treatment Is Not Risk Elimination

A common misconception is:

> "Risk treatment means eliminating the risk."

That is not necessarily true.

Many AI risks cannot be completely eliminated.

For example:

> An AI model may always have some probability of generating an incorrect output.

Treatment may instead reduce the probability or consequences to an acceptable level.

```text id="g3f7k9"
Initial Risk
     ↓
Treatment
     ↓
Lower Residual Risk
```

---

### 462. Inherent Risk and Residual Risk

Risk treatment operates between inherent and residual risk.

```text id="2y9j8v"
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Risk Treatment
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

**Inherent risk**

> The level of risk before considering controls or treatment.

**Residual risk**

> The risk remaining after treatment and controls are considered.

The treatment process should therefore demonstrate how the organization moves from the initial exposure toward the desired risk level.

---

### 463. Treatment Objective

The objective should be explicit.

For example:

> "Reduce the risk of unauthorized disclosure of confidential information from High to Moderate."

This is more useful than:

> "Improve security."

A good treatment objective is:

* Specific.
* Risk-focused.
* Measurable where possible.
* Linked to the risk evaluation.

---

### 464. Risk Treatment Options

At a high level, organizations can choose among several approaches:

```text id="p8x6u3"
        AI Risk
           ↓
 ┌─────────┼─────────┐
 ↓         ↓         ↓
Modify    Share     Avoid
Risk      Risk      Risk
 ↓
Accept Remaining Risk
```

The exact terminology can vary by framework, but the fundamental idea is to deliberately determine how the risk will be handled.

---

### 465. Risk Modification

Risk modification means changing the risk characteristics.

This may involve:

* Reducing likelihood.
* Reducing consequence.
* Increasing detectability.
* Increasing containment capability.
* Reducing exposure.
* Limiting scale.
* Reducing autonomy.

For AI systems, risk modification is often the most practical treatment approach.

---

### 466. Example of Risk Modification

Suppose:

> An AI agent can independently execute financial transactions.

Potential treatment:

> Require human approval for transactions above a defined threshold.

The AI system still exists, but its risk characteristics change.

```text id="d3k6s1"
Unrestricted AI Action
        ↓
Treatment
        ↓
Human Approval Threshold
        ↓
Reduced Consequence / Exposure
```

---

### 467. Preventive Treatment

Preventive treatment attempts to reduce the probability that a risk event will occur.

Examples include:

* Strong authentication.
* Authorization.
* Input validation.
* Data filtering.
* Secure configuration.
* Model restrictions.
* Prompt controls.
* Tool restrictions.
* Segmentation.

---

### 468. Detective Treatment

Detective measures identify harmful behavior or outcomes.

Examples:

* AI activity monitoring.
* Anomaly detection.
* Output monitoring.
* Security logging.
* Model-performance monitoring.
* Alerting.
* User reporting.

Detection does not necessarily prevent the initial event, but it can reduce the duration or consequences.

---

### 469. Corrective Treatment

Corrective treatment addresses consequences after an event.

Examples:

* AI service shutdown.
* Model rollback.
* Transaction reversal.
* Account suspension.
* Data restoration.
* Incident response.
* Corrective model configuration.

---

### 470. Treatment Can Address Likelihood or Consequence

Risk treatment does not always need to prevent the event.

Consider:

> AI generates an incorrect recommendation.

Treatment could:

**Reduce likelihood**

> Improve model validation.

or:

**Reduce consequence**

> Require human approval before the recommendation becomes an actual decision.

This distinction is important.

---

### 471. Layered Risk Treatment

For significant AI risks, multiple treatments may be required.

Example:

```text id="h6s2s9"
Access Control
      ↓
Data Filtering
      ↓
Output Validation
      ↓
Human Review
      ↓
Monitoring
      ↓
Incident Response
```

This creates layered protection rather than depending on a single control.

---

### 472. Defense in Depth

Defense in depth is particularly important when AI can:

* Access sensitive information.
* Execute actions.
* Interact with external systems.
* Affect customers.
* Make consequential recommendations.

A single control failure should not automatically produce a catastrophic outcome where practical.

---

### 473. Risk Treatment Hierarchy

Organizations can consider treatment options in a logical order:

```text id="7x5v2q"
Can the risky activity be eliminated?
          ↓
If not,
Can the risk be reduced?
          ↓
If not,
Can exposure be limited?
          ↓
Can consequences be reduced?
          ↓
Can the risk be shared?
          ↓
Can the remaining risk be accepted?
```

The appropriate approach depends on context.

---

### 474. Treatment Selection

Treatment selection should consider:

* Risk significance.
* Risk appetite.
* Treatment effectiveness.
* Cost.
* Feasibility.
* Implementation time.
* Business impact.
* Technical constraints.
* Legal requirements.
* Operational consequences.
* Residual risk.

The cheapest treatment is not automatically the best treatment.

---

### 475. Cost-Benefit Consideration

Suppose:

> Treatment A costs €10,000 and reduces risk substantially.

> Treatment B costs €500,000 and provides only marginal additional risk reduction.

The organization may prefer Treatment A if it brings the risk within acceptable boundaries.

However, cost should not be used to justify violating mandatory requirements.

---

### 476. Treatment Effectiveness

A treatment should be evaluated according to how much risk it actually reduces.

Example:

```text id="b9k4s0"
Before Treatment
Likelihood = 4
Impact = 5

After Treatment
Likelihood = 2
Impact = 5
```

The treatment primarily reduced likelihood.

Another treatment might reduce consequence instead:

```text id="j8z1x5"
Before
Likelihood = 4
Impact = 5

After
Likelihood = 4
Impact = 3
```

Understanding which dimension changes is important.

---

### 477. Expected Treatment Outcome

Before implementing a treatment, the organization can define the expected outcome.

Example:

> "Implementing mandatory human approval is expected to reduce the potential impact of unauthorized AI actions from Severe to Moderate."

This creates a measurable treatment objective.

---

### 478. Treatment Feasibility

A theoretically effective treatment may not be practical.

Consider:

> "Have a human review every AI transaction."

If the AI processes:

> 100,000 transactions per hour,

manual review may not be operationally feasible.

Therefore, treatment selection should consider actual operating scale.

---

### 479. Automation and Treatment

AI treatment can itself involve automation.

For example:

> Automatically block AI requests that attempt to access restricted data.

Automation can provide:

* Speed.
* Consistency.
* Scalability.

But automated controls can also fail, so high-impact environments may require additional safeguards.

---

### 480. Human Oversight as Treatment

Human oversight can reduce AI risk when it is meaningful.

Effective oversight requires that the human can:

* Understand the relevant information.
* Challenge the AI output.
* Reject the recommendation.
* Modify the decision.
* Stop the process.
* Escalate when necessary.

Simply placing a person in an approval workflow does not automatically constitute effective risk treatment.

---

### 481. Limiting AI Autonomy

Reducing autonomy can be a powerful treatment.

Example:

```text id="x4q6u2"
Autonomous Action
       ↓
Treatment
       ↓
Recommendation Only
```

Instead of allowing AI to execute an action directly, the organization may require a human to approve the action.

---

### 482. Limiting Scope

Another treatment is reducing what the AI system can access or affect.

Examples:

* Restrict users.
* Restrict departments.
* Restrict data.
* Restrict geographic deployment.
* Restrict transaction values.
* Restrict available tools.
* Restrict operating hours.

This reduces the potential blast radius.

---

### 483. Reducing Blast Radius

Suppose an AI system can modify:

> 1 million customer records.

Treatment could limit it to:

> 10,000 records per batch.

If the AI makes an error, the maximum immediate impact is reduced.

```text id="p4n7y2"
Large Exposure
     ↓
Treatment
     ↓
Limited Exposure
     ↓
Smaller Potential Impact
```

---

### 484. Rate Limiting

Rate limiting can reduce the speed and scale of AI actions.

Examples:

* Maximum requests per minute.
* Maximum transactions per hour.
* Maximum records processed.
* Maximum automated actions.

This is particularly useful where rapid propagation is a major risk driver.

---

### 485. Permission Reduction

AI agents should receive only the permissions necessary for their intended function.

For example:

> A customer-service AI may need read access to customer information but not permission to delete accounts.

This is an application of least privilege to AI-enabled systems.

---

### 486. Tool Restriction

An AI agent may have access to tools such as:

* Databases.
* APIs.
* Email.
* File systems.
* Financial systems.
* Ticketing platforms.

Treatment can restrict which tools the AI can invoke.

Example:

```text id="6q4d9x"
AI Agent
   ↓
Allowed Tools
   ├── Knowledge Search ✓
   ├── Ticket Creation ✓
   ├── Account Deletion ✗
   └── Payment Execution ✗
```

---

### 487. Data Minimization as Treatment

Reducing the amount of data available to an AI system can reduce exposure.

Instead of:

> Full customer database.

Provide:

> Only the fields necessary for the specific task.

This can reduce the potential consequence of:

* Unauthorized access.
* Data leakage.
* Model misuse.

---

### 488. Data Segmentation

Data can be separated based on sensitivity.

Example:

```text id="o8q0r4"
Public Data
    ↓
Internal Data
    ↓
Confidential Data
    ↓
Highly Sensitive Data
```

AI access can then be restricted according to classification.

---

### 489. Environment Segmentation

AI systems can also be separated by environment.

Example:

```text id="m7x9u1"
Development
     ↓
Testing
     ↓
Pilot
     ↓
Production
```

High-risk AI capabilities should not automatically be available across all environments.

---

### 490. Model Restriction

Treatment can involve limiting the model itself.

Examples:

* Use a less autonomous model.
* Use a model with appropriate capabilities.
* Restrict model functionality.
* Disable unnecessary tools.
* Restrict context length where appropriate.
* Restrict external connectivity.

The objective is to align capability with the required business purpose.

---

### 491. Architecture Modification

Sometimes the best treatment is architectural.

Example:

Instead of:

```text id="p1d9g8"
User → AI → Production Database
```

use:

```text id="7k4f2m"
User
 ↓
AI
 ↓
Policy Enforcement Layer
 ↓
Validated API
 ↓
Production Database
```

The additional enforcement layer can reduce unauthorized or unsafe actions.

---

### 492. Guardrails

AI guardrails can constrain system behavior.

Examples:

* Input restrictions.
* Output restrictions.
* Tool authorization.
* Data access policies.
* Content filtering.
* Transaction thresholds.
* Human approval requirements.

Guardrails should be tested rather than assumed to be effective.

---

### 493. Validation as Treatment

Validation can occur at multiple stages:

```text id="z4u1t5"
Input Validation
      ↓
Model Processing
      ↓
Output Validation
      ↓
Action Validation
```

Each stage can address different risk drivers.

---

### 494. Output Validation

If AI output can create significant consequences, the organization may require validation before use.

Examples:

* Schema validation.
* Business-rule validation.
* Human review.
* Data consistency checks.
* Policy checks.

This is particularly useful when AI output feeds automated workflows.

---

### 495. Action Validation

A higher-risk AI agent may require validation before executing an action.

Example:

```text id="6y2h3k"
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Policy Check
       ↓
Authorization Check
       ↓
Human Approval
       ↓
Execution
```

This creates multiple decision gates.

---

### 496. Monitoring as Treatment

Monitoring can reduce risk by improving:

* Detection speed.
* Visibility.
* Incident response.
* Accountability.
* Control verification.

Examples:

* AI activity monitoring.
* Model performance monitoring.
* Security monitoring.
* Data-access monitoring.
* Anomaly detection.

---

### 497. Treatment and Detectability

Consider two identical AI systems.

**System A**

> Errors are detected immediately.

**System B**

> Errors may remain undetected for six months.

Even if the probability of failure is identical, the consequences may differ substantially.

Improving detectability can therefore be a meaningful treatment.

---

### 498. Incident Response as Treatment

AI-specific incident response should address scenarios such as:

* Prompt injection.
* Unauthorized AI actions.
* Data leakage.
* Model compromise.
* Model failure.
* Harmful outputs.
* Provider outage.

Treatment should consider whether the organization can respond quickly enough to limit consequences.

---

### 499. Kill Switch

For sufficiently autonomous systems, an emergency shutdown mechanism may be appropriate.

Conceptually:

```text id="j2t7s9"
AI Agent
   ↓
Unexpected Behavior
   ↓
Detection
   ↓
Emergency Stop
   ↓
Containment
```

The mechanism should be tested to ensure that it actually works.

---

### 500. Rollback Capability

If a model or configuration causes problems, the organization may need to revert to a previously validated state.

```text id="n3m5p8"
Current Model
     ↓
Problem
     ↓
Rollback
     ↓
Known-Good Configuration
```

Rollback capability can reduce recovery time and operational impact.

---

### 501. Resilience as Risk Treatment

For critical AI systems, resilience measures can include:

* Redundancy.
* Failover.
* Backup systems.
* Alternate providers.
* Manual fallback.
* Disaster recovery.

The treatment objective is to reduce the consequence of AI unavailability or failure.

---

### 502. Manual Fallback

A manual fallback process can reduce operational risk.

Example:

> If the AI claims-processing system becomes unavailable, trained employees can process critical claims manually.

However, the fallback must be realistic.

Questions include:

* Is sufficient staff available?
* Can they handle the volume?
* Are procedures documented?
* Has the process been tested?

---

### 503. Treatment Through Process Change

Sometimes technology does not need to change.

The organization can modify the business process.

Example:

Instead of:

> AI automatically rejects applications.

Change to:

> AI identifies potentially problematic applications for human review.

The technology remains similar, but the consequence of AI error is reduced.

---

### 504. Treatment Through Use-Case Restriction

An organization may restrict the AI system to lower-risk uses.

Example:

```text id="l3u8v2"
AI Capability
     ↓
Approved Uses
     ├── Internal Drafting ✓
     ├── Summarization ✓
     ├── Customer Decisions ✗
     └── Autonomous Transactions ✗
```

This can be an effective way to manage risk.

---

### 505. Treatment Through Deployment Phasing

Instead of full deployment:

```text id="a8v4j1"
Pilot
  ↓
Limited Production
  ↓
Expanded Production
```

The organization can gradually increase exposure as evidence and confidence improve.

---

### 506. Pilot as Risk Treatment

A controlled pilot can limit:

* Number of users.
* Data exposure.
* Transaction volume.
* AI autonomy.
* Geographic scope.

It also provides operational evidence before broader deployment.

---

### 507. Treatment and Business Objectives

Treatment should not unnecessarily destroy the business value of AI.

The objective is:

> **Reduce unacceptable risk while preserving as much legitimate business value as reasonably possible.**

For example:

> Rather than eliminating an AI assistant because it can access sensitive data, restrict it to approved datasets and approved users.

This is often more practical than complete prohibition.

---

### 508. Proportionality

Treatment should be proportionate to risk.

A low-risk internal AI tool may require:

* Basic access control.
* Standard monitoring.
* User guidance.

A high-impact AI system may require:

* Extensive testing.
* Multiple controls.
* Independent validation.
* Human oversight.
* Formal approval.
* Continuous monitoring.

---

### 509. Treatment Priority

Treatment priority can be based on:

* Risk severity.
* Regulatory importance.
* Potential human impact.
* Likelihood.
* Business criticality.
* Exposure.
* Treatment feasibility.
* Time sensitivity.

High-risk issues should generally receive greater urgency.

---

### 510. Treatment Effectiveness vs Cost

A useful decision principle is:

> **Select treatment that achieves the required risk reduction while remaining proportionate and feasible.**

However:

> Cost should not be the sole basis for ignoring a mandatory control or unacceptable risk.

---

### 511. Treatment Constraints

Treatment may be constrained by:

* Legacy architecture.
* Budget.
* Skills.
* Technology limitations.
* Vendor capabilities.
* Time.
* Business dependencies.
* Regulatory requirements.

These constraints should be documented rather than hidden.

---

### 512. Treatment Plan

A treatment plan should identify:

```text id="k6r4t2"
Risk
 ↓
Treatment Objective
 ↓
Treatment Action
 ↓
Control / Measure
 ↓
Owner
 ↓
Deadline
 ↓
Expected Risk Reduction
 ↓
Validation
```

This turns a governance decision into an executable plan.

---

### 513. Treatment Owner

Each treatment action should have a clearly assigned owner.

For example:

| Treatment                 | Owner               |
| ------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Implement authorization   | IAM Team            |
| Configure AI guardrails   | AI Engineering      |
| Define review process     | Business Owner      |
| Validate privacy controls | Privacy Team        |
| Implement monitoring      | Security Operations |

Accountability should be explicit.

---

### 514. Treatment Deadline

A treatment plan should include:

* Target completion date.
* Priority.
* Dependencies.
* Milestones.
* Validation date.

Without deadlines, treatment actions can remain indefinitely open.

---

### 515. Treatment Verification

Implementation is not the same as effectiveness.

Example:

> "Monitoring enabled."

This only proves implementation.

A stronger verification is:

> "Monitoring was tested using defined scenarios and successfully detected the specified AI-risk events."

The organization should verify that treatment actually reduces the intended risk.

---

### 516. Treatment Acceptance Criteria

Before implementation, define what success looks like.

Example:

> "AI transactions above €1,000 must require explicit human approval."

Testing can then determine whether the control works.

```text id="k7v3p1"
Treatment Requirement
       ↓
Control Implementation
       ↓
Control Testing
       ↓
Pass / Fail
```

---

### 517. Treatment and Residual Risk

After treatment:

```text id="q4t8w2"
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Treatment
     ↓
Residual Risk
     ↓
Risk Evaluation
```

The residual risk must be reassessed.

If it remains above acceptable boundaries, additional action may be required.

---

### 518. Treatment Iteration

Risk treatment is often iterative.

```text id="f2q8w6"
Treatment 1
   ↓
Residual Risk
   ↓
Still Too High
   ↓
Treatment 2
   ↓
Residual Risk
   ↓
Acceptable
```

This prevents organizations from assuming that one treatment will always be sufficient.

---

### 519. Treatment and Continuous Improvement

AI systems evolve.

Therefore, treatment should be reviewed when:

* The model changes.
* The data changes.
* The user population changes.
* Autonomy increases.
* New threats emerge.
* Controls fail.
* Incidents occur.
* Business use changes.

Risk treatment should remain aligned with the current operating environment.

---

### 520. Core Principle of AI Risk Treatment

The central principle is:

> **AI risk treatment is the deliberate selection and implementation of measures that modify AI risk so that the resulting exposure is consistent with organizational objectives, risk criteria, applicable requirements, and approved risk boundaries.**

Remember the basic relationship:

```text id="p6z4k1"
INHERENT RISK
      ↓
TREATMENT
      ↓
CONTROLS / PROCESS CHANGES
      ↓
RESIDUAL RISK
      ↓
RE-EVALUATION
```

A strong GRC professional does not simply ask:

> **"What control can we implement?"**

The better question is:

> **"What treatment will address the actual risk driver, how much risk reduction is expected, and how will we verify that the residual risk is acceptable?"**

# 06.06 — AI Risk Treatment

## Part 2 — AI Risk Treatment Strategy and Treatment Design

### 521. Strategic Approach to AI Risk Treatment

AI risk treatment should begin with the **risk itself**, not with a predetermined security control.

The organization should first understand:

> What is causing the risk, what could happen, and what level of risk needs to be changed?

Then treatment can be designed around the actual risk drivers.

```text
Risk Driver
     ↓
Risk Event
     ↓
Potential Consequence
     ↓
Treatment Objective
     ↓
Treatment Measure
```

This prevents organizations from implementing controls simply because they are available or commonly used.

---

### 522. Treatment Objective Before Treatment Action

Before selecting a control, define what the treatment must achieve.

For example:

**Risk:**

> AI agent may disclose confidential information.

**Treatment objective:**

> Reduce the likelihood that unauthorized users can obtain confidential information through the AI system.

Only after defining the objective should the organization select appropriate measures.

---

### 523. Risk Driver Analysis

A risk may have several underlying drivers.

Example:

```text id="8f3k2m"
Weak Access Control
       +
Excessive AI Permissions
       +
Poor Data Segmentation
       ↓
Unauthorized Data Access
       ↓
Confidentiality Risk
```

Treatment should address the drivers rather than only the final consequence.

---

### 524. Treating the Root Cause

Suppose:

> AI generates incorrect financial recommendations.

A superficial treatment might be:

> Tell users to be careful.

A stronger treatment could address:

* Training data quality.
* Model validation.
* Business-rule validation.
* Output verification.
* Human approval.

The stronger treatment targets the mechanisms producing the risk.

---

### 525. Treatment Selection Criteria

Treatment options should be assessed against several criteria:

* Effectiveness.
* Feasibility.
* Cost.
* Implementation time.
* Scalability.
* Reliability.
* Maintainability.
* Business impact.
* Regulatory suitability.
* Residual risk.

A treatment that works technically but cannot operate reliably at enterprise scale may not be an effective treatment.

---

### 526. Treatment Alternatives

For a single AI risk, several treatment options may exist.

Example:

**Risk:**

> AI-generated recommendations cause incorrect customer decisions.

Possible treatments:

1. Improve model validation.
2. Restrict AI to recommendations.
3. Require human approval.
4. Add business-rule validation.
5. Reduce AI scope.
6. Stop the use case.

The organization can compare the alternatives rather than immediately selecting one.

---

### 527. Treatment Option Analysis

A simple comparison could be:

| Treatment                | Risk Reduction | Cost                 | Complexity |
| ------------------------ | -------------- | -------------------- | ---------- |
| Model validation         | High           | Medium               | Medium     |
| Human approval           | High           | High                 | High       |
| Scope restriction        | Medium         | Low                  | Low        |
| Business-rule validation | High           | Medium               | Medium     |
| Use-case withdrawal      | Very High      | High business impact | Low        |

The best option depends on the organization's objectives and risk boundaries.

---

### 528. Multiple Treatment Options

Sometimes a combination is stronger than any individual measure.

Example:

```text id="4w7n2s"
Model Validation
       +
Output Validation
       +
Human Approval
       +
Monitoring
       ↓
Lower Residual Risk
```

This is especially relevant for high-impact AI systems.

---

### 529. Treatment Portfolio

An enterprise should not view treatment as isolated controls.

It may have:

```text id="r5h8c1"
AI Security Controls
AI Privacy Controls
AI Governance Controls
AI Operational Controls
AI Human Oversight
AI Resilience Controls
        ↓
Enterprise Treatment Portfolio
```

This provides a broader view of how AI risk is managed.

---

### 530. Treatment Prioritization

When many AI risks exist, treatment must be prioritized.

Possible priority factors:

* Risk severity.
* Risk above appetite.
* Human impact.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Business criticality.
* Number of affected users.
* Potential financial impact.
* Treatment feasibility.
* Time sensitivity.

---

### 531. Prioritizing Above-Appetite Risks

A practical rule is:

> Risks above the organization's approved appetite should receive priority for treatment or formal escalation.

For example:

```text id="f9p4m7"
Residual Risk
      ↓
Within Appetite → Monitor
      ↓
Above Appetite → Treat / Escalate
```

The exact governance response depends on policy.

---

### 532. Treatment Urgency

Two risks can have the same rating but different urgency.

Example:

**Risk A**

> High risk with a potential impact in five years.

**Risk B**

> High risk capable of material impact within hours.

Risk B may require more immediate treatment.

Therefore, **time to impact** can be an important treatment consideration.

---

### 533. Speed of Propagation

AI can rapidly propagate errors.

For example:

```text id="u6m2r8"
AI Error
   ↓
Automated Workflow
   ↓
10,000 Transactions
   ↓
Large Impact
```

Treatment can reduce propagation through:

* Transaction limits.
* Approval thresholds.
* Rate limits.
* Batch processing.
* Monitoring.
* Circuit breakers.

---

### 534. Reversibility as Treatment Consideration

A highly reversible AI action may have lower practical consequences than an irreversible action.

Example:

```text id="t3j8w1"
AI Drafts Email
     ↓
Human Can Edit
     ↓
Reversible
```

Compared with:

```text id="a7f2m5"
AI Deletes Customer Data
     ↓
Difficult to Restore
     ↓
Irreversible / High Consequence
```

Treatment should therefore consider reversibility.

---

### 535. Treatment Through Reversibility

Where possible, design AI workflows so actions can be reversed.

Examples:

* Soft delete instead of permanent deletion.
* Transaction cancellation.
* Version rollback.
* Approval before irreversible action.
* Backup before automated modification.

Increasing reversibility can reduce consequences.

---

### 536. Treatment Through Transaction Limits

AI agents performing actions can be restricted by:

* Monetary value.
* Number of transactions.
* Number of records.
* Frequency.
* User scope.

Example:

```text id="b5d7x4"
AI Agent
   ↓
Maximum €1,000 per transaction
   ↓
Human approval above €1,000
```

This can significantly reduce potential impact.

---

### 537. Circuit Breakers

A circuit breaker can automatically stop AI activity when predefined conditions are exceeded.

Example:

```text id="w8p1q3"
AI Activity
     ↓
Error Rate > Threshold
     ↓
Circuit Breaker
     ↓
AI Action Stopped
```

This can reduce rapid propagation of failures.

---

### 538. Fail-Safe Design

Where practical, AI systems should fail in a manner that limits harm.

Example:

> If the AI cannot confidently classify a transaction, send it for human review rather than automatically approving it.

This can be described as:

```text id="x4m7k2"
AI Uncertainty
      ↓
Safe Default
      ↓
Human Review
```

---

### 539. Fail-Open vs Fail-Closed

The appropriate failure behavior depends on the risk.

**Fail-open**

> System continues operating when a control fails.

**Fail-closed**

> System blocks the action when the control cannot verify safety or authorization.

For sensitive AI actions, fail-closed behavior may be preferable, but the operational consequences must also be evaluated.

---

### 540. Treatment Through Confidence Thresholds

AI outputs can be subjected to confidence or quality thresholds.

Example:

```text id="j2k5p8"
AI Output
   ↓
Confidence / Validation Check
   ↓
High Confidence → Continue
Low Confidence → Human Review
```

However, model confidence should not automatically be interpreted as actual correctness.

---

### 541. Treatment Through Independent Validation

Critical AI outputs can be checked by an independent mechanism.

Examples:

* Rule-based validation.
* Separate model.
* Deterministic calculation.
* Human expert.
* External verification.

This can reduce dependence on the AI model itself.

---

### 542. Deterministic Controls

Where possible, deterministic rules can be placed around probabilistic AI behavior.

Example:

```text id="n5k8v1"
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Business Rules
       ↓
Allowed / Rejected
```

This can be particularly useful for:

* Financial limits.
* Access permissions.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Transaction thresholds.

---

### 543. Policy Enforcement Layer

A policy enforcement layer can separate AI reasoning from actual authorization.

```text id="x9q4k7"
AI
 ↓
Policy Engine
 ↓
Authorization
 ↓
Action
```

The AI may propose an action, but the policy layer determines whether the action is permitted.

---

### 544. Treatment Through Least Privilege

AI agents should receive only the permissions necessary for their purpose.

For example:

> A support AI may retrieve customer information but should not have unrestricted administrative privileges.

This limits the potential consequence if the AI is manipulated or behaves unexpectedly.

---

### 545. Privilege Separation

High-impact actions can require different authorities.

Example:

```text id="c4g8m2"
AI Requests Action
       ↓
Authorization Service
       ↓
Human Approval
       ↓
Execution
```

This reduces the possibility of an AI independently performing sensitive actions.

---

### 546. Segregation of AI Functions

Different AI functions can be separated.

For example:

* One component analyzes.
* Another validates.
* Another executes.

This reduces the possibility that a single compromised AI component controls the entire workflow.

---

### 547. Treatment Through Isolation

AI systems can be isolated from critical systems.

Example:

```text id="v3q6h8"
AI Environment
      ↓
Controlled Interface
      ↓
Critical System
```

Instead of:

```text
AI
 ↓
Direct unrestricted access
 ↓
Critical System
```

Isolation reduces potential blast radius.

---

### 548. Sandboxing

An AI agent can operate within a restricted environment.

For example:

> The AI can execute code only inside a temporary isolated environment with no access to production systems.

This can reduce the consequence of malicious or erroneous AI-generated actions.

---

### 549. Network Controls

Network segmentation can restrict AI connectivity.

Examples:

* Egress filtering.
* Firewall rules.
* API allowlists.
* Private network access.
* Restricted DNS.
* Service-to-service authorization.

The objective is to prevent unnecessary external communication.

---

### 550. Data Loss Prevention

DLP mechanisms can reduce the probability of sensitive information leaving the controlled environment.

Example:

```text id="p7s4j2"
AI Output
   ↓
DLP Inspection
   ↓
Sensitive Data Detected?
   ├── No → Release
   └── Yes → Block / Redact / Review
```

---

### 551. Redaction

Sensitive information can be removed before it reaches users or external systems.

Examples:

* Personal identifiers.
* Payment information.
* Credentials.
* Confidential business information.

Redaction can reduce the consequence of accidental disclosure.

---

### 552. Tokenization and Masking

Where appropriate, sensitive information can be transformed before AI processing.

Example:

```text id="q8r1v4"
Customer Data
     ↓
Masking / Tokenization
     ↓
AI Processing
```

This may reduce exposure while preserving some functionality.

---

### 553. Treatment Through Data Quality

Poor data can produce poor AI outcomes.

Treatment may include:

* Data validation.
* Data cleansing.
* Data provenance.
* Quality checks.
* Duplicate removal.
* Missing-value handling.
* Dataset version control.

The objective is to reduce risks originating from unreliable inputs.

---

### 554. Treatment Through Model Validation

Model validation may examine:

* Accuracy.
* Robustness.
* Performance.
* Security.
* Failure conditions.
* Generalization.
* Known limitations.

Validation should be appropriate to the AI use case and risk level.

---

### 555. Adversarial Testing

Security testing can deliberately attempt to cause undesirable AI behavior.

Examples include:

* Prompt manipulation.
* Malicious inputs.
* Data poisoning scenarios.
* Unauthorized tool invocation.
* Evasion attempts.

Findings can then inform risk treatment.

---

### 556. Red-Team Testing

For higher-risk AI systems, red-team exercises can test whether controls withstand realistic attack scenarios.

Example:

```text id="r6m2x8"
Attack Scenario
      ↓
AI System
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Observed Behavior
      ↓
Treatment Improvements
```

The purpose is not merely to find vulnerabilities but to determine whether the treatment is effective.

---

### 557. Treatment Through User Controls

Users can introduce AI risk through:

* Unsafe prompts.
* Unauthorized data uploads.
* Misuse.
* Circumvention.
* Excessive reliance on AI.

Treatment can therefore include:

* User permissions.
* Acceptable-use policies.
* Training.
* Technical restrictions.
* Monitoring.

---

### 558. User Training as a Supporting Treatment

Training can reduce human-related AI risks.

Examples:

* Recognizing AI hallucinations.
* Protecting confidential information.
* Understanding AI limitations.
* Recognizing prompt-injection attempts.
* Reporting AI incidents.

Training should normally complement technical controls rather than replace them for significant risks.

---

### 559. Treatment Through Awareness

Users should understand:

> AI output is not automatically authoritative.

For example:

```text id="x2c5j8"
AI Output
    ↓
Human Critical Review
    ↓
Business Decision
```

This can reduce automation bias and inappropriate reliance.

---

### 560. Treatment Through Governance

Some AI risks require governance controls rather than technical controls.

Examples:

* Approval committees.
* AI policies.
* Risk acceptance procedures.
* Restricted use cases.
* Documentation requirements.
* Periodic reviews.
* Independent assessments.

AI risk treatment should therefore combine technical and governance measures.

---

### 561. Treatment Through Contractual Controls

For third-party AI services, treatment may include contractual requirements such as:

* Security obligations.
* Privacy requirements.
* Incident notification.
* Audit rights.
* Data-use restrictions.
* Subprocessor controls.
* Service-level commitments.
* Change notification.
* Exit requirements.

Contracts can reduce certain third-party risks but should not be treated as a substitute for technical assurance.

---

### 562. Treatment Through Vendor Assurance

Organizations may require evidence from AI suppliers, such as:

* Independent assurance reports.
* Security assessments.
* Penetration testing summaries.
* Privacy documentation.
* Business continuity information.

The organization should assess the quality and relevance of the evidence.

---

### 563. Treatment Through Supplier Diversification

If excessive dependence on one AI provider creates concentration risk, treatment could involve:

* Multiple providers.
* Alternative models.
* Portable architecture.
* Backup providers.
* Internal fallback capability.

This improves resilience.

---

### 564. Treatment Through Portability

An organization can reduce provider lock-in by designing systems around:

* Standard interfaces.
* Portable data.
* Modular architecture.
* Abstraction layers.
* Multiple model options.

Portability can reduce dependency risk.

---

### 565. Treatment Through Architectural Abstraction

Instead of embedding one model throughout the enterprise:

```text id="k5s2n8"
Applications
     ↓
AI Abstraction Layer
     ↓
Model A / Model B / Model C
```

This can make model replacement easier.

---

### 566. Treatment Through Deployment Controls

AI deployment should be controlled through defined gates.

Example:

```text id="s4m7p2"
Development
    ↓
Security Review
    ↓
Risk Evaluation
    ↓
Approval
    ↓
Production
```

This prevents unassessed AI systems from being deployed directly into production.

---

### 567. Treatment Through Change Control

Changes to AI systems should be evaluated according to their materiality.

Examples:

* Model replacement.
* New data source.
* New tool.
* Increased permissions.
* Increased autonomy.
* New user population.

Material changes may require reassessment before deployment.

---

### 568. Treatment Through Configuration Management

Organizations should maintain visibility into:

* Model version.
* System version.
* Configuration.
* Prompts or instructions where governed.
* Tools.
* Integrations.
* Permissions.

This supports repeatability and incident investigation.

---

### 569. Treatment Through Version Control

A model or AI configuration should be traceable to a known version.

Example:

```text id="u1r8m3"
AI Version 1
     ↓
Validated
     ↓
Production

AI Version 2
     ↓
Testing
     ↓
Approval
     ↓
Production
```

This prevents unvalidated changes from silently entering production.

---

### 570. Treatment Through Deployment Segmentation

Not every user needs the same AI capability.

Organizations can create:

* Development access.
* Pilot access.
* Standard access.
* Privileged access.

This limits exposure while the system matures.

---

### 571. Treatment Through Monitoring Thresholds

Monitoring becomes more effective when thresholds are predefined.

Examples:

* Error rate.
* Unauthorized access attempts.
* Sensitive-data detections.
* Tool invocation failures.
* Unusual transaction volume.

A threshold breach can trigger treatment escalation.

---

### 572. Treatment Through Alerting

An alert should lead to an actionable response.

Weak:

> "AI anomaly detected."

Stronger:

> "AI agent exceeded transaction threshold; automated execution suspended and security review initiated."

The second approach connects detection directly to treatment.

---

### 573. Treatment Through Automated Containment

For certain risks, automated containment may reduce the time between detection and response.

Example:

```text id="c7f2m9"
Anomaly
  ↓
Detection
  ↓
Automated Containment
  ↓
Human Investigation
```

This can be useful when AI can act at machine speed.

---

### 574. Treatment Through Incident Playbooks

AI-specific playbooks should define:

* Trigger.
* Initial response.
* Containment.
* Investigation.
* Escalation.
* Recovery.
* Communication.
* Re-evaluation.

This makes treatment operational rather than purely theoretical.

---

### 575. Treatment Through Business Continuity

For critical AI services, treatment may include:

* Manual fallback.
* Alternative systems.
* Backup providers.
* Recovery procedures.
* Disaster recovery testing.

The treatment objective is to prevent AI failure from becoming a business-wide failure.

---

### 576. Treatment Through Recovery Objectives

Organizations can define:

* Maximum tolerable downtime.
* Recovery time objective.
* Recovery point objective.
* Maximum acceptable data loss.

These requirements can influence AI architecture and treatment decisions.

---

### 577. Treatment Through Limiting Consequence

Consider:

> AI can make incorrect credit recommendations.

Instead of trying only to eliminate every incorrect recommendation, treatment may limit the consequences:

```text id="g6x3r2"
AI Recommendation
      ↓
Human Review
      ↓
Final Decision
```

The AI remains useful while its output does not automatically become the final decision.

---

### 578. Treatment Through Progressive Autonomy

AI autonomy can be increased gradually.

```text id="h4n8s1"
Level 1 → Suggest
Level 2 → Recommend
Level 3 → Act with Approval
Level 4 → Limited Autonomous Action
Level 5 → Broad Autonomous Action
```

Organizations can increase autonomy only after sufficient evidence demonstrates acceptable performance and control effectiveness.

---

### 579. Treatment Through Pilot Controls

A pilot can include:

* Restricted users.
* Restricted data.
* Limited duration.
* Limited transactions.
* Enhanced monitoring.
* Mandatory feedback.
* Explicit exit criteria.

This reduces exposure while evidence is gathered.

---

### 580. Treatment Exit Criteria

A pilot should define conditions for:

**Expansion**

> Risk remains within acceptable boundaries.

**Extension**

> More evidence is required.

**Termination**

> Risk exceeds acceptable boundaries.

This prevents pilots from continuing indefinitely without governance decisions.

---

### 581. Treatment Through Use-Case Redesign

Sometimes the safest treatment is to change the business process itself.

Original:

> AI autonomously approves employee expenses.

Redesigned:

> AI reviews expenses and identifies exceptions for human approval.

The business still receives AI efficiency while reducing the consequences of AI error.

---

### 582. Treatment Through Restricting High-Impact Decisions

An organization may prohibit AI from making certain decisions autonomously.

Examples:

* Employment decisions.
* Significant financial decisions.
* Safety-critical decisions.
* Certain access decisions.

Instead, AI may provide decision support.

This can materially change the risk profile.

---

### 583. Treatment Through Human Escalation

AI systems can be designed to escalate uncertain or high-impact cases.

```text id="n2k7v4"
AI Processing
     ↓
Routine Case → Automated Path
     ↓
Uncertain / High Impact
     ↓
Human Review
```

This allows automation for lower-risk cases while retaining stronger oversight for exceptional cases.

---

### 584. Treatment Through Risk-Based Human Review

Not every AI output necessarily requires identical review.

A risk-based model can apply:

```text id="e8q3m1"
Low Impact → Automated
Medium Impact → Sampling / Review
High Impact → Mandatory Human Review
Critical Impact → Specialized Approval
```

This can make human oversight more scalable.

---

### 585. Sampling as Treatment

Where full review is impractical, statistical or risk-based sampling may provide additional assurance.

For example:

> Review 10% of low-risk AI outputs and 100% of high-impact outputs.

The sampling design should be appropriate to the risk.

---

### 586. Treatment Through Quality Assurance

Periodic QA can test whether:

* AI outputs remain accurate.
* Controls remain effective.
* Users follow procedures.
* Exceptions are handled correctly.
* Risk assumptions remain valid.

QA provides evidence that treatment continues to work.

---

### 587. Treatment Through Independent Review

Higher-risk AI systems may require independent review by:

* Security.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Compliance.
* Risk.
* Internal audit.

The objective is to challenge assumptions and reduce conflicts of interest.

---

### 588. Treatment Through Documentation

Documentation is itself a governance measure.

Important records include:

* Treatment decision.
* Rationale.
* Selected controls.
* Treatment owner.
* Expected outcome.
* Implementation date.
* Validation evidence.
* Residual risk.
* Approval.

This creates accountability.

---

### 589. Treatment Decision Record

A concise treatment decision can be documented as:

> **Risk:** Unauthorized disclosure through AI assistant
> **Treatment Objective:** Reduce likelihood and potential exposure
> **Treatment:** Restrict data access, enforce authorization, implement DLP, and require monitoring
> **Owner:** AI Platform Team
> **Target:** Before production
> **Expected Residual Risk:** Moderate
> **Validation:** Security testing and control verification

---

### 590. Treatment Dependencies

Treatment actions may depend on other projects.

Example:

```text id="s5k9x2"
AI Access Control
       ↓
Depends on IAM Upgrade
       ↓
IAM Upgrade Delayed
       ↓
AI Risk Treatment Delayed
```

Dependencies should be visible to management.

---

### 591. Treatment Roadmap

For complex AI programs, treatment may be phased:

```text id="x7q2m5"

Basic Controls

      ↓

Phase 2
Enhanced Monitoring

      ↓

Phase 3
Advanced Validation

      ↓

Phase 4
Continuous Assurance
```

This provides a realistic path toward mature risk management.

---

### 592. Treatment Debt

Organizations can accumulate **AI risk treatment debt** when known risk-reduction actions remain unresolved.

Examples:

* Unimplemented controls.
* Outdated models.
* Unresolved vulnerabilities.
* Missing monitoring.
* Expired exceptions.
* Incomplete testing.

Treatment debt should be tracked similarly to technical debt.

---

### 593. Treatment Backlog

A GRC system may maintain:

| Risk   | Treatment          | Owner    | Priority | Status      |
| ------ | ------------------ | -------- | -------- | ----------- |
| AI-R01 | DLP                | Security | Critical | In Progress |
| AI-R02 | Human review       | Business | High     | Planned     |
| AI-R03 | Model validation   | AI Team  | High     | Testing     |
| AI-R04 | Access restriction | IAM      | Medium   | Complete    |

This creates operational visibility.

---

### 594. Treatment Closure

A treatment should not be marked complete simply because a ticket is closed.

Closure should require evidence that:

1. The treatment was implemented.
2. It operates as intended.
3. Expected risk reduction was achieved.
4. Residual risk was reassessed.
5. Appropriate approval was obtained.

---

### 595. Treatment Validation

A practical validation sequence is:

```text id="n6p3r8"
Treatment Implemented
       ↓
Control Tested
       ↓
Evidence Collected
       ↓
Risk Reassessed
       ↓
Residual Risk Confirmed
       ↓
Treatment Closed / Continued
```

---

### 596. Treatment Failure

If the treatment does not produce the expected reduction:

```text id="z8w4q2"
Treatment
   ↓
Testing
   ↓
Insufficient Effectiveness
   ↓
Risk Remains High
   ↓
Additional Treatment
```

The organization should not close the risk merely because the planned control was implemented.

---

### 597. Treatment Effectiveness Metrics

Possible metrics include:

* Percentage of treatment actions completed.
* Percentage completed on time.
* Percentage validated successfully.
* Average risk reduction.
* Number of failed treatments.
* Number of reopened treatments.
* Number of risks remaining above appetite.

---

### 598. Treatment Governance Principle

The core governance principle is:

> **Treatment should modify risk, not merely produce documentation.**

A mature organization therefore asks:

> "What measurable change in risk did this treatment create?"

rather than:

> "Did we create a control document?"

---

### 599. Advanced Treatment Mental Model

Use this mental model:

```text id="c2m8r5"
RISK
 ↓
ROOT CAUSE
 ↓
TREATMENT OBJECTIVE
 ↓
OPTIONS
 ↓
SELECT TREATMENT
 ↓
IMPLEMENT
 ↓
TEST
 ↓
MEASURE EFFECTIVENESS
 ↓
REASSESS RESIDUAL RISK
 ↓
ACCEPT / ADD TREATMENT / ESCALATE
```

This is the practical lifecycle of AI risk treatment.

---

### 600. Part 2 Summary

AI risk treatment should be **risk-driven, proportionate, measurable, and verifiable**.

The key principles are:

1. Start with the risk driver.
2. Define the treatment objective.
3. Consider multiple treatment alternatives.
4. Address likelihood, consequence, or both.
5. Reduce AI scope and blast radius where appropriate.
6. Control AI permissions and autonomy.
7. Use layered technical and governance controls.
8. Consider human oversight where appropriate.
9. Use monitoring and containment for dynamic AI risks.
10. Validate that treatment actually reduces risk.
11. Reassess residual risk after implementation.
12. Track treatment ownership, deadlines, dependencies, and evidence.

The fundamental professional question is:

> **"What specific change are we making to the AI system, process, people, or environment, and how will we demonstrate that this change has reduced the risk to the required level?"**

# 06.06 — AI Risk Treatment

## Part 3 — Advanced Treatment Design, Optimization, and Enterprise Application

### 601. Treatment as a Risk-Reduction Strategy

At an advanced level, AI risk treatment should be viewed as a **risk-reduction strategy**, not merely a list of controls.

The treatment should establish a clear relationship between:

```text id="r2k8m4"
Risk Driver
     ↓
Risk Scenario
     ↓
Treatment Objective
     ↓
Treatment Mechanism
     ↓
Expected Risk Reduction
     ↓
Residual Risk
```

This allows the organization to determine whether the treatment actually addresses the problem.

---

### 602. Treatment Mechanism

A treatment mechanism explains **how** a treatment reduces risk.

Example:

> **Risk:** AI agent sends confidential information externally.

> **Treatment:** Restrict outbound communication and inspect AI-generated content before transmission.

> **Mechanism:** Reduce the probability that unauthorized sensitive information leaves the controlled environment.

This is more rigorous than simply recording:

> "Implement DLP."

---

### 603. Treatment-to-Risk Mapping

Every major treatment should be mapped to the risk it addresses.

| Risk                | Treatment           | Risk Dimension           |
| ------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Unauthorized access | Least privilege     | Likelihood               |
| Data leakage        | DLP                 | Likelihood / Consequence |
| Incorrect decision  | Human approval      | Consequence              |
| AI outage           | Failover            | Consequence              |
| Excessive autonomy  | Action restrictions | Consequence              |

This helps prevent irrelevant or redundant controls.

---

### 604. Treatment-to-Control Mapping

Treatment may require multiple controls.

Example:

```text id="q8f3m1"
Risk:
Unauthorized AI Action
        ↓
Treatment Objective:
Reduce unauthorized execution
        ↓
Controls:
Authentication
Authorization
Tool Restrictions
Transaction Limits
Human Approval
Monitoring
```

The treatment is the strategy; the controls are mechanisms used to execute it.

---

### 605. Control Coverage

A treatment plan should identify whether the controls collectively cover the relevant risk drivers.

Example:

```text id="k4r7x2"
Risk Driver A → Covered ✓
Risk Driver B → Covered ✓
Risk Driver C → Partially Covered ⚠
Risk Driver D → Not Covered ✗
```

This exposes treatment gaps.

---

### 606. Treatment Gaps

A treatment gap exists when:

> A significant risk driver is not sufficiently addressed by existing or planned measures.

Example:

```text id="m9w5p2"
AI Data Leakage Risk

Access Control       ✓
DLP                  ✓
Monitoring           ✓
Third-Party Sharing  ✗
```

The treatment may therefore remain incomplete.

---

### 607. Treatment Overlap

Multiple controls may address the same risk.

Example:

```text id="h2q7s5"
Control A
Control B
Control C
      ↓
Same Risk Driver
```

Some overlap can provide resilience, but excessive duplication can create unnecessary cost and complexity.

---

### 608. Treatment Optimization

Treatment optimization asks:

> **"Are we achieving sufficient risk reduction with an efficient combination of measures?"**

This involves balancing:

* Effectiveness.
* Cost.
* Complexity.
* Operational burden.
* User experience.
* Reliability.
* Maintainability.

---

### 609. Treatment Efficiency

Two treatments may produce similar risk reduction.

Example:

| Treatment | Risk Reduction |  Cost |
| --------- | -------------: | ----: |
| Control A |            70% | €100K |
| Control B |            68% |  €20K |

If both satisfy organizational requirements, Control B may be more efficient.

However, the comparison should consider more than initial cost.

---

### 610. Total Cost of Treatment

Treatment cost may include:

* Technology.
* Licensing.
* Engineering.
* Integration.
* Operations.
* Monitoring.
* Training.
* Maintenance.
* Compliance.
* Future scaling.

A low-cost control can become expensive if operational overhead is high.

---

### 611. Treatment Trade-Offs

Every treatment may introduce trade-offs.

Example:

> Stronger human review reduces AI decision risk.

But:

* Processing becomes slower.
* Staffing requirements increase.
* User experience may decline.

The organization must evaluate both **risk reduction and operational consequence**.

---

### 612. Security vs Usability

A highly restrictive AI environment may reduce security risk but make the system difficult to use.

Example:

```text id="v6x2n9"
Maximum Restrictions
       ↓
Very Low Risk
       ↓
Low Business Usability
```

The goal is not necessarily maximum restriction.

The goal is **appropriate risk reduction consistent with business objectives**.

---

### 613. Treatment Proportionality

Treatment should generally be proportional to:

* Potential consequence.
* Likelihood.
* Exposure.
* Criticality.
* Stakeholder impact.

A low-risk internal AI assistant should not necessarily require the same controls as a safety-critical autonomous AI system.

---

### 614. Risk-Based Treatment Tiers

An enterprise can define treatment tiers.

```text id="s3k8m2"
Tier 1 — Low Risk
Standard Controls

Tier 2 — Moderate Risk
Enhanced Controls

Tier 3 — High Risk
Strong Controls + Formal Approval

Tier 4 — Critical Risk
Enhanced Governance + Executive Oversight
```

This improves consistency and scalability.

---

### 615. Standard Treatment Catalog

A GRC function can maintain a treatment catalog.

Examples:

* Access control.
* Data minimization.
* Data segmentation.
* DLP.
* Human approval.
* Output validation.
* Model validation.
* Monitoring.
* Logging.
* Rate limiting.
* Transaction limits.
* Network isolation.
* Incident response.
* Business continuity.

The catalog accelerates risk treatment design while still requiring contextual judgment.

---

### 616. Control Baselines

The organization can establish baseline controls for different AI risk categories.

For example:

**Internal low-risk AI**

> Authentication + basic logging.

**Sensitive-data AI**

> Authentication + authorization + DLP + monitoring.

**High-impact AI**

> Risk assessment + validation + human oversight + monitoring + formal approval.

Baselines create consistency.

---

### 617. Tailoring the Baseline

A baseline should not become a rigid checklist.

The evaluator should determine whether:

* Controls are relevant.
* Additional controls are needed.
* Certain controls are unnecessary.
* Alternative controls provide equivalent protection.

This is **risk-based tailoring**.

---

### 618. Compensating Controls

When a standard treatment cannot be implemented, an alternative may be considered.

Example:

**Standard control**

> Automated data classification.

**Alternative**

> Manual classification by trained reviewers.

The organization should evaluate whether the alternative provides sufficient risk reduction.

---

### 619. Compensating Control Requirements

A compensating control should generally be:

* Documented.
* Risk-based.
* Effective.
* Testable.
* Approved.
* Time-bound where appropriate.

The organization should avoid treating compensating controls as permanent shortcuts without justification.

---

### 620. Treatment Sequencing

Some treatments must occur before others.

Example:

```text id="x7m3q9"
Identity Foundation
       ↓
Authorization
       ↓
AI Tool Restrictions
       ↓
Monitoring
       ↓
Autonomous Actions
```

Allowing AI autonomy before establishing reliable authorization can create unnecessary exposure.

---

### 621. Treatment Dependencies

Treatment plans should explicitly identify dependencies.

Example:

> AI transaction restrictions depend on the implementation of a centralized authorization service.

If the authorization project is delayed, the AI treatment may also be delayed.

This should be visible in the risk register.

---

### 622. Treatment Critical Path

For high-risk AI systems, some controls may form a critical path.

```text id="j5q2v8"
Identity
  ↓
Authorization
  ↓
Tool Control
  ↓
Human Approval
  ↓
Production
```

If one critical control is incomplete, production deployment may need to be delayed.

---

### 623. Treatment Gates

Governance gates can prevent progression until required risk treatments are complete.

Example:

```text id="n8f4k1"
Development
     ↓
Gate 1 — Security
     ↓
Gate 2 — Risk
     ↓
Gate 3 — Privacy
     ↓
Gate 4 — Approval
     ↓
Production
```

The gates should be proportional to the AI system's risk.

---

### 624. Treatment Prioritization Matrix

Treatment can be prioritized using:

| Risk Reduction | Implementation Effort | Priority      |
| -------------- | --------------------- | ------------- |
| High           | Low                   | Immediate     |
| High           | High                  | Strategic     |
| Low            | Low                   | Opportunistic |
| Low            | High                  | Reconsider    |

This helps allocate resources.

---

### 625. Quick Wins

Some treatments provide substantial risk reduction with limited effort.

Examples:

* Removing unnecessary AI permissions.
* Restricting unused tools.
* Enabling logging.
* Applying transaction limits.
* Restricting sensitive datasets.

These can often be implemented quickly while larger treatments are being developed.

---

### 626. Strategic Treatments

Other treatments require significant investment.

Examples:

* New AI security architecture.
* Centralized AI governance platform.
* Enterprise AI monitoring.
* Multi-provider architecture.
* Comprehensive model validation infrastructure.

These should be managed as strategic initiatives.

---

### 627. Treatment Roadmap

A mature roadmap can contain:

```text id="f4x8k2"
Immediate
  ↓
Quick Risk Reduction

Short Term
  ↓
Control Improvements

Medium Term
  ↓
Architecture Changes

Long Term
  ↓
Continuous Assurance
```

This allows risk reduction to begin while larger initiatives are underway.

---

### 628. Interim Risk Treatment

If the final treatment will take six months, an interim control may be required.

Example:

> Until automated DLP is deployed, all external AI usage is restricted to approved users and approved datasets.

This prevents the organization from remaining unnecessarily exposed while waiting for the permanent solution.

---

### 629. Treatment Sunset

Temporary controls should have a planned expiration.

Example:

```text id="p3k7x1"
Temporary Control
      ↓
Permanent Control Implemented
      ↓
Validation
      ↓
Temporary Control Retired
```

Otherwise, temporary measures can become uncontrolled permanent processes.

---

### 630. Treatment Lifecycle

A complete treatment lifecycle can therefore be:

```text id="m5q9r3"

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

```

### 631. Treatment Performance

Treatment performance should be measurable where practical.

Example:

**Objective:**

> Reduce unauthorized AI data access.

**Measure:**

> Number of unauthorized access events.

**Target:**

> Zero confirmed unauthorized disclosures.

The measurement should reflect the actual risk objective.

---

### 632. Leading and Lagging Indicators

**Leading indicators** show whether treatment is being implemented.

Examples:

* Percentage of systems with DLP enabled.
* Percentage of AI agents using least privilege.
* Percentage of high-risk AI systems with human approval.

**Lagging indicators** show outcomes.

Examples:

* Number of AI-related incidents.
* Number of unauthorized disclosures.
* Number of harmful AI actions.

Both can be useful.

---

### 633. Treatment Assurance

Assurance asks:

> **"Can we demonstrate that the treatment works?"**

Evidence may include:

* Control testing.
* Security assessments.
* Penetration testing.
* Simulation.
* Audit evidence.
* Operational monitoring.
* Incident data.

---

### 634. Control Testing

A control should be tested against realistic scenarios.

Example:

> Attempt to make the AI access restricted customer data.

Expected result:

> Access denied and event logged.

The test provides evidence of treatment effectiveness.

---

### 635. Negative Testing

Testing should not only confirm expected behavior.

It should also deliberately test failure conditions.

Example:

```text id="r8x2m6"
Unauthorized Request
       ↓
AI System
       ↓
Expected: Block
```

Then test:

* Invalid credentials.
* Privilege escalation.
* Malicious instructions.
* Unexpected inputs.
* Tool misuse.

---

### 636. Treatment Under Adversarial Conditions

AI controls should be evaluated against realistic attack conditions.

Examples:

* Prompt injection.
* Malicious files.
* Data poisoning.
* Tool manipulation.
* Privilege abuse.
* Adversarial inputs.

The treatment should remain effective under plausible attack scenarios.

---

### 637. Treatment Robustness

A robust treatment remains effective despite reasonable variations in conditions.

For example:

> A data protection control should not work only for one known prompt but fail when the same sensitive data is requested using a different phrasing.

Robustness is particularly important for AI because behavior can be variable.

---

### 638. Treatment Reliability

A control that works 99% of the time may still be inadequate for a highly consequential action.

Treatment reliability should therefore be evaluated relative to the risk.

Example:

```text id="n7q3k5"
Low Consequence
99% Control Effectiveness
May Be Acceptable

Critical Consequence
99% Control Effectiveness
May Be Insufficient
```

---

### 639. Treatment Availability

Security controls themselves can become unavailable.

Example:

> AI authorization service becomes unavailable.

The organization must decide whether the AI:

* Stops.
* Continues in restricted mode.
* Falls back to manual processing.

This is part of treatment design.

---

### 640. Treatment Failure Modes

Every significant treatment should consider:

> **"What happens if this treatment fails?"**

Example:

```text id="c6m4p8"
AI Monitoring
      ↓
Monitoring Failure
      ↓
Can AI Continue?
      ↓
Restricted Mode / Stop / Manual Review
```

This creates resilience around the control.

---

### 641. Treatment Independence

Where possible, critical controls should not all depend on the same component.

Example:

```text id="v2k6s9"
AI Control A ──┐
AI Control B ──┼── Same Dependency
AI Control C ──┘
```

If that dependency fails, all controls may fail simultaneously.

---

### 642. Common-Cause Treatment Failure

Examples include:

* Shared identity provider failure.
* Shared logging infrastructure.
* Shared cloud provider.
* Shared AI model.
* Shared data source.

Treatment design should consider these common dependencies.

---

### 643. Treatment Resilience

For critical AI systems, treatment may require redundancy.

Example:

```text id="x4q8m2"
Primary AI Service
      ↓
Failure
      ↓
Alternative Service
      ↓
Business Continues
```

This can reduce operational consequence.

---

### 644. Treatment and Business Continuity

The organization should determine:

* How long the AI can be unavailable.
* Whether a manual alternative exists.
* How quickly the alternative can be activated.
* Whether staff are trained.
* Whether the alternative can handle required volume.

### 645. Treatment and Recovery

Recovery controls can include:

Examples:

* Disable one tool.
* Restrict one account.
* Isolate one environment.
* Stop one transaction class.

* Environment isolation.
* Data restrictions.

These measures are especially important for autonomous AI.

### 667. Treatment and Risk Acceptance

Treatment and acceptance are related but different.

Treatment asks:

> **"How can we modify the risk?"**

Acceptance asks:

> **"Are we willing to retain the remaining risk?"**

Therefore:

```text id="u5k8m3"
Treatment
    ↓
Residual Risk
    ↓
Acceptance Decision
```

The organization should not confuse implementing a treatment with accepting the residual risk.

* Operational consequences.
* Customer impact.

Possible treatment:

> Restrict the AI to administrative support rather than decision-making.

This changes the risk by changing the use case.

> Remove only the high-risk functionality.

Example:

```text id="v8m3q5"
 ├── Summarization ✓
 ├── Search ✓
 ├── Drafting ✓
 └── Autonomous Account Changes ✗
```

This can preserve lower-risk business value.

### 674. Treatment Effectiveness Review

After implementation, the organization should ask:

1. Was the treatment implemented?
2. Does it operate as intended?
3. Did likelihood decrease?
4. Did consequence decrease?
5. Did detectability improve?
6. Did the blast radius decrease?
7. Is residual risk now acceptable?

This is the practical test of treatment success.

### 675. Treatment Success Criteria

A treatment is successful when:

> The intended risk reduction has been achieved and the resulting residual risk is within the organization's approved boundaries, subject to applicable requirements.

Implementation alone does not establish success.

### 676. Treatment Failure Criteria

Treatment should be reconsidered when:

* Controls fail testing.
* Risk remains above appetite.
* Incidents demonstrate insufficient protection.
* Assumptions prove incorrect.
* The AI system changes materially.
* New threats emerge.

### 679. Executive-Level Treatment View

Executives generally need to know:

* What is the risk?
* What is being done?
* How much risk reduction is expected?
* What does treatment cost?
* What risk remains?
* Is the residual risk acceptable?
* What decision is required?

They generally do not need every technical implementation detail unless it affects the decision.

### 680. Senior GRC Treatment Perspective

A senior GRC professional should be able to explain:

> **"I do not start with a control. I start with the risk scenario and its drivers. I define the treatment objective, compare feasible treatment options, assess their expected risk reduction and operational impact, select proportionate measures, assign ownership and deadlines, validate effectiveness, and reassess the residual risk. If the remaining risk is still outside appetite, I escalate or recommend further treatment rather than treating implementation of a control as proof that the risk is resolved."**

### 682. Part 3 Summary

Advanced AI risk treatment is about **designing the right intervention for the actual risk**.

The most important principles are:

1. Treat the risk driver, not merely the symptom.
2. Define the treatment objective before selecting controls.
3. Map treatments to specific risks.
4. Evaluate multiple treatment options.
5. Consider effectiveness, cost, complexity, and business impact.
6. Use layered controls where consequences justify them.
7. Reduce AI autonomy and blast radius where appropriate.
8. Apply least privilege and controlled tool access.
9. Use validation, monitoring, containment, and recovery.
10. Consider control dependencies and common-cause failures.
11. Use interim treatments when permanent controls take time.
12. Validate that implemented treatments actually reduce risk.
13. Reassess residual risk after treatment.
14. Continuously improve treatment based on evidence and incidents.

The central professional principle is:

> **A treatment is not successful because a control was implemented; it is successful when there is sufficient evidence that the treatment has modified the risk as intended and the resulting residual risk is within the organization's approved boundaries.**

### 684. Treatment Ownership

Every significant treatment should have an accountable owner.

Possible owners include:

* AI Product Owner.
* Business Process Owner.
* CISO.
* Security Engineering.
* Privacy Officer.
* Data Owner.
* IT Operations.
* Third-Party Manager.

### 685. Risk Owner vs Treatment Owner

**Risk Owner**

> Accountable for the risk and the decision regarding its management.

**Treatment Owner**

> Responsible for implementing the agreed risk treatment.

```text
      ↓
```

### 694. Design Effectiveness vs Operating Effectiveness

**Operating effectiveness**

> Did the control actually operate as intended?

> Testing shows that sensitive data was consistently blocked.

That provides stronger evidence of operating effectiveness.

Each can have different risk profiles and treatment requirements.

Therefore, a single enterprise-wide treatment approach may be insufficient.

### 709. Common Treatment Baseline

An organization can still establish common requirements such as:

* Identity.
* Access control.
* Logging.
* Data protection.
* Security testing.
* Incident management.
* Governance approval.

These form the baseline.

Higher-risk systems receive additional measures.

Retirement itself can create risks.

### 718. Treatment Across the AI Supply Chain

### 719. Shared Responsibility

For third-party AI:

> The provider may secure the underlying service, but the customer may remain responsible for configuration, access, data, users, and business use.

Treatment should therefore identify which party controls each risk.

---

### 720. Responsibility Matrix

A simple model:

| Risk Area                  | Organization | Provider |
| -------------------------- | ------------ | -------- |
| User access                | R            | C        |
| Application configuration  | R            | C        |
| Infrastructure security    | C            | R        |
| Customer data              | R            | C        |
| Model service availability | C            | R        |
| Business decision          | R            | I        |

**R = Responsible, C = Consulted, I = Informed**

The exact allocation depends on the service arrangement.

---

### 721. Treatment and Regulatory Requirements

Where requirements are mandatory, treatment must meet the applicable obligation.

A treatment should therefore distinguish:

> **Risk-driven controls**

from:

> **Mandatory compliance requirements**

A control may be required even if the organization's internal risk assessment rates the risk differently.

---

### 722. Treatment and Risk Appetite

Risk treatment should ultimately support the organization's approved risk appetite.

Example:

```text
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Treatment
     ↓
Residual Risk
     ↓
Risk Appetite
     ↓
Decision
```

If residual risk exceeds appetite, additional action or formal acceptance may be required.

---

### 723. Treatment and Risk Capacity

Risk appetite describes the amount of risk the organization is willing to accept.

Risk capacity describes the organization's ability to absorb risk without unacceptable consequences.

A treatment decision should consider both.

A financially expensive AI failure may exceed the organization's practical capacity even if management considers the probability relatively low.

---

### 724. Treatment Cost-Benefit Analysis

Treatment decisions can compare:

```text
Cost of Treatment
        VS
Expected Risk Reduction
        VS
Potential Loss
```

This supports rational allocation of resources.

However, financial optimization alone is not sufficient where legal, safety, human-rights, or other mandatory considerations apply.

---

### 725. Treatment Effectiveness vs Treatment Efficiency

These concepts should be distinguished.

**Effectiveness**

> Did the treatment reduce the intended risk?

**Efficiency**

> Did it achieve the risk reduction using resources appropriately?

A treatment can be effective but inefficient.

Another can be efficient but insufficiently effective.

---

### 726. Treatment Maturity

A simple maturity model:

**Level 1 — Reactive**

> Treatment occurs after incidents.

**Level 2 — Defined**

> Treatment processes exist.

**Level 3 — Risk-Based**

> Treatment is prioritized based on risk.

**Level 4 — Measured**

> Effectiveness is measured.

**Level 5 — Adaptive**

> Treatment continuously evolves using risk intelligence and operational evidence.

### 737. Treatment Design Checklist

Before approving treatment, ask:

**Risk**

* What risk are we treating?
* What causes the risk?
* What consequence are we trying to reduce?

**Treatment**

* What is the treatment objective?
* Why was this treatment selected?
* Are alternatives available?

**Implementation**

* Who owns it?
* What dependencies exist?
* What is the deadline?

**Effectiveness**

* How will it be tested?
* What evidence will demonstrate effectiveness?
* What risk reduction is expected?

**Residual Risk**

* What risk remains?
* Is it within appetite?
* Who approves the remaining risk?

### 738. Senior GRC Treatment Questions

During a professional review, ask:

> "What risk driver does this control address?"

> "What is the expected risk reduction?"

> "What evidence will prove effectiveness?"

> "What happens if the control fails?"

> "Is there a compensating control?"

> "What residual risk remains?"

> "Who has authority to accept that residual risk?"

These questions demonstrate mature AI risk-management thinking.

### 739. Common Treatment Mistakes

#### Mistake 1 — Control-first thinking

> "We need DLP."

Better:

> "We need to reduce the risk of unauthorized sensitive-data disclosure; DLP is one potential treatment."

---

#### Mistake 2 — Treating implementation as success

> "The control is deployed, so the risk is closed."

Better:

> Validate whether the control actually reduced the risk.

---

#### Mistake 3 — No owner

> "Security will handle it."

Better:

> Identify a named accountable owner and responsible implementation team.

---

#### Mistake 4 — No deadline

> "We will implement it later."

Better:

> Establish a risk-based target date.

---

#### Mistake 5 — No residual-risk assessment

> "Treatment completed."

Better:

> Reassess the remaining risk after treatment.

---

#### Mistake 6 — Ignoring operational impact

> "More controls are always better."

Better:

> Consider security, business continuity, cost, usability, and operational consequences.

---

### 740. Common AI Treatment Anti-Patterns

Avoid:

* Treating every AI risk identically.
* Implementing controls without understanding the risk.
* Relying exclusively on user training.
* Assuming vendor responsibility eliminates organizational responsibility.
* Ignoring AI autonomy.
* Ignoring third-party dependencies.
* Failing to validate treatment.
* Leaving temporary controls indefinitely.
* Closing risks because tickets are closed.
* Accepting residual risk without proper authority.

### 743. Treatment as an Executive Decision

For significant AI risks, treatment is ultimately a management decision.

Technical teams can recommend:

> "Implement control X."

But management must understand:

> "This treatment costs X, reduces the risk by approximately Y, requires Z months, and leaves residual risk at level A."

This allows informed decision-making.

### 744. Treatment Communication

A concise executive communication can be:

> **Risk:** Unauthorized AI transactions
> **Current Risk:** High
> **Treatment:** Least privilege, transaction limits, human approval
> **Investment:** €X
> **Expected Result:** Reduce risk to Moderate
> **Target:** Q4
> **Residual Risk:** Moderate
> **Decision Required:** Approve treatment funding

This is the language of enterprise risk management.

The complete AI risk treatment model can be remembered as:

```text

    ↓

    ↓
DESIGN
    ↓
PRIORITIZE
    ↓

    ↓

    ↓

    ↓

    ↓
```

The purpose is not to eliminate every AI risk.

## 746. Part 4 — Key Takeaways

The most important principles to retain are:

1. **Every significant AI treatment needs ownership.**
2. **Risk ownership and treatment ownership can be different.**
3. **Treatment status is not the same as risk status.**
4. **Control implementation does not automatically mean risk reduction.**
5. **Treatment effectiveness requires evidence.**
6. **Temporary treatments should have expiration or transition plans.**
7. **Treatment exceptions require controlled governance.**
8. **AI treatment should cover the AI lifecycle.**
9. **Third-party AI requires shared-responsibility analysis.**
10. **Treatment should be proportional to risk and impact.**
11. **High-autonomy AI requires stronger action boundaries.**
12. **Treatment should reduce likelihood, consequence, exposure, or a combination of these.**
13. **Residual risk must be reassessed after treatment.**
14. **Risks remaining above appetite require additional treatment, escalation, or authorized acceptance.**
15. **A mature GRC function can demonstrate the complete chain from risk → treatment → evidence → residual risk → management decision.**

### The core professional statement

> **"AI risk treatment is the structured process of selecting, implementing, and validating proportionate measures that modify identified AI risks. As a GRC professional, I would begin with the risk scenario and its drivers, define the treatment objective, evaluate treatment alternatives, assign ownership and timelines, validate implementation and operating effectiveness, measure the resulting risk reduction, and reassess residual risk. Treatment is not considered successful merely because a control has been implemented; it is successful when there is sufficient evidence that the intended risk reduction has been achieved and the remaining risk is appropriately managed within the organization's governance and risk boundaries."**
