# 06.18 — AI Risk Registers and Risk Treatment Plans

## Part 1 — Risk Register Foundations and AI Risk Documentation

### 1. Introduction

An **AI Risk Register** is a structured record used to identify, assess, prioritize, treat, monitor, and report risks associated with artificial intelligence systems and use cases.

A **Risk Treatment Plan** defines what the organization will do about those risks.

Together, they provide the operational bridge between:

```text
AI Risk Governance
        ↓
Risk Identification
        ↓
Risk Assessment
        ↓
Risk Treatment
        ↓
Control Implementation
        ↓
Monitoring
        ↓
Management Decision
```

The risk register answers:

> **"What AI risks do we have?"**

The treatment plan answers:

> **"What are we going to do about them?"**

---

# 2. Purpose of an AI Risk Register

The AI risk register provides a centralized view of organizational AI exposure.

It helps management understand:

* Which AI risks exist.
* Which systems create those risks.
* How significant the risks are.
* Who owns them.
* What controls are in place.
* What remediation is required.
* What residual risk remains.
* Which risks require escalation or acceptance.

The register therefore becomes a key governance instrument.

---

# 3. Risk Register Is More Than a Spreadsheet

A common misconception is:

> "The risk register is just a list of risks."

A mature risk register is a **management control mechanism**.

It connects:

```text
Risk
 ↓
Owner
 ↓
Control
 ↓
Treatment
 ↓
Evidence
 ↓
Residual Risk
 ↓
Decision
 ↓
Monitoring
```

If the register contains risks but nobody acts on them, it has little governance value.

---

# 4. AI Risk Register Scope

An AI risk register can cover risks associated with:

* AI applications.
* Machine-learning models.
* Generative AI.
* AI agents.
* AI vendors.
* AI data.
* AI infrastructure.
* AI integrations.
* AI development.
* AI deployment.
* AI operations.
* AI retirement.

The scope should align with the organization's AI governance framework.

---

# 5. Risk Register Versus Asset Inventory

These are different.

### AI inventory

Answers:

> **What AI systems do we have?**

### Risk register

Answers:

> **What risks do those systems create?**

For example:

```text
AI Inventory
     ↓
Customer Chatbot
     ↓
Risk Register
     ↓
Data leakage risk
Incorrect response risk
Availability risk
Prompt injection risk
```

One AI system can therefore generate multiple risks.

---

# 6. Risk Register Versus Issue Register

Another important distinction:

### Risk

> Something that **may happen** and could cause harm.

### Issue

> Something that **has already happened or currently exists**.

Example:

**Risk:**

> AI may expose confidential customer information.

**Issue:**

> A customer record was already exposed through the AI system.

The incident or issue may create a new risk requiring reassessment.

---

# 7. Risk Register Versus Control Register

A **risk register** focuses on exposure.

A **control register** focuses on safeguards.

Example:

```text
Risk
Confidential data disclosure
        ↓
Controls
DLP
Access control
Data classification
Logging
        ↓
Control Register
Documents each control
```

The two registers should be linked.

---

# 8. Risk Register Versus Treatment Plan

The distinction is important.

### Risk Register

Describes the risk.

### Treatment Plan

Describes the actions required to address it.

Example:

| Risk                   | Treatment                  |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Sensitive data leakage | Implement DLP              |
| Unauthorized AI access | Strengthen IAM             |
| Model bias             | Perform fairness testing   |
| Vendor risk            | Add contractual safeguards |

---

# 9. Minimum Risk Register Fields

A practical AI risk register may contain:

| Field                 | Purpose                            |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Risk ID               | Unique identification              |
| AI System             | System associated with risk        |
| Risk Description      | What could happen                  |
| Risk Category         | Type of risk                       |
| Cause                 | Why it could occur                 |
| Impact                | Potential consequence              |
| Likelihood            | Probability                        |
| Inherent Risk         | Risk before controls               |
| Existing Controls     | Current safeguards                 |
| Control Effectiveness | How well controls operate          |
| Residual Risk         | Remaining exposure                 |
| Treatment             | Response strategy                  |
| Risk Owner            | Accountable owner                  |
| Action Owner          | Person responsible for remediation |
| Due Date              | Target completion                  |
| Status                | Current treatment state            |
| Acceptance Authority  | Authorized decision-maker          |
| Review Date           | Next reassessment                  |
| Evidence              | Supporting documentation           |

---

# 10. Unique Risk ID

Every risk should have a unique identifier.

Example:

```text
AI-RISK-001
AI-RISK-002
AI-RISK-003
```

The identifier allows the risk to be tracked across:

* Risk assessments.
* Treatment plans.
* Audit findings.
* Governance meetings.
* Reports.
* Exceptions.
* Control testing.

---

# 11. Risk Statement

A risk statement should be clear and actionable.

A useful structure is:

> **Because of [cause], [event] may occur, resulting in [impact].**

Example:

> **Because employees can submit confidential information to unauthorized external AI services, sensitive organizational data may be disclosed, resulting in regulatory, financial and reputational impact.**

This is much stronger than:

> "AI data risk."

---

# 12. Risk Cause

The cause explains **why the risk could occur**.

Examples:

* Excessive privileges.
* Inadequate security controls.
* Poor data governance.
* Weak vendor controls.
* Insufficient validation.
* Lack of user awareness.
* Model limitations.
* Inadequate monitoring.

---

# 13. Risk Event

The event describes what could actually happen.

Examples:

* Sensitive information is disclosed.
* AI generates incorrect information.
* Unauthorized action is executed.
* Model performance deteriorates.
* Vendor suffers a security breach.
* AI produces discriminatory outcomes.

---

# 14. Risk Impact

Impact describes the consequences.

Possible impact categories:

* Financial.
* Operational.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Regulatory.
* Legal.
* Reputational.
* Customer.
* Safety.
* Strategic.

---

# 15. Example Risk Construction

```text
CAUSE
Weak access controls
        ↓
EVENT
Unauthorized AI access
        ↓
IMPACT
Sensitive data exposure
```

Complete risk statement:

> **Weak access controls may allow unauthorized users to access AI systems containing sensitive information, resulting in potential data exposure, regulatory consequences and reputational damage.**

Possible categories include:

# 17. Why Taxonomy Matters

Without a consistent taxonomy, organizations may classify similar risks differently.

For example:

```text
Team A → "AI Security"
Team B → "Data Risk"
Team C → "Technology Risk"
```

A standardized taxonomy allows management to identify patterns across the enterprise.

Development-stage risks may include:

* Insecure code.

* Inadequate documentation.

These should be recorded and treated before production where appropriate.

# 22. Risk Register During Deployment

Deployment may introduce:

* New integrations.
* New users.
* Production data.
* External exposure.

Therefore:

> Deployment should trigger a review of the existing risk register.

# 23. Risk Register During Operations

Operational monitoring can identify:

* Model drift.
* Security incidents.
* Performance degradation.
* Increasing exceptions.
* New attack techniques.
* Vendor changes.
* New use cases.

These may require new risks or changes to existing risks.

---

# 24. Dynamic Risk Register

AI risk registers should not be treated as static documents.

A mature model is:

```text
Risk Register
      ↓
Monitor
      ↓
New Information
      ↓
Reassess
      ↓
Update Risk
      ↓
Update Treatment
```

This is particularly important for rapidly evolving AI technologies.

---

# 25. Inherent Risk

**Inherent risk** represents exposure before considering the effectiveness of existing controls.

Example:

```text
AI Data Leakage
Likelihood = High
Impact = High

Inherent Risk = Critical
```

The exact scoring methodology depends on organizational policy.

---

# 26. Existing Controls

The register should document controls already implemented.

Examples:

* IAM.
* Encryption.
* DLP.
* Network segmentation.
* Human approval.
* Model validation.
* Monitoring.
* Logging.
* Security testing.

This prevents treatment plans from ignoring existing safeguards.

---

# 27. Control Effectiveness

The existence of a control does not automatically mean the risk has been adequately reduced.

For example:

> DLP is deployed.

But testing shows:

> DLP can be bypassed.

Therefore, the control may be only partially effective.

The register should reflect that reality.

---

# 28. Residual Risk

Residual risk is the remaining exposure after controls and treatment are considered.

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Existing Controls
      ↓
Control Effectiveness
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

Example:

```text
Inherent = Critical
Controls = Strong
Residual = Moderate
```

The organization then determines whether the residual risk is acceptable.

---

# 29. Target Risk

A treatment plan can also establish a **target risk level**.

Example:

```text
Current Residual Risk = High
Target Risk = Moderate
```

This provides a measurable treatment objective.

The plan should explain how the organization intends to reach that target.

---

# 30. Risk Treatment Strategy

The risk register should identify the chosen treatment.

Common options are:

1. **Avoid**
2. **Reduce**
3. **Share / Transfer**
4. **Accept**

For AI, these can be applied to individual risks or entire use cases.

---

# 31. Treatment Action

A treatment action should be specific.

Weak:

> "Improve AI security."

Strong:

> "Implement DLP controls on the enterprise AI gateway to prevent transmission of restricted data to external AI services."

The second statement is measurable.

---

# 32. Action Owner

The **risk owner** and **action owner** may be different.

Example:

```text
Risk Owner
Chief Data Officer
       ↓
Action Owner
Security Engineering Manager
       ↓
Action
Implement DLP
```

This distinction creates clear accountability.

---

# 33. Treatment Due Date

Each treatment action should have a target date where appropriate.

Example:

```text
Action: Implement AI Gateway DLP
Owner: Security Engineering
Due: 30 September
Status: In Progress
```

Overdue actions should be visible to management.

---

# 34. Treatment Status

Common statuses include:

* Not Started.
* Planned.
* In Progress.
* Blocked.
* Completed.
* Validating.
* Closed.

A mature process should distinguish:

> **Implemented**

from:

> **Validated as effective.**

# 39. What Makes a Good Risk Register?

A good register is:

* Accurate.
* Current.
* Consistent.
* Actionable.
* Traceable.
* Owned.
* Evidence-based.
* Linked to controls.
* Linked to treatment.
* Connected to governance decisions.

The objective is not to create the largest possible register.

The objective is to create a **useful decision-making tool**.

---

# 40. What Makes a Poor Risk Register?

Common problems include:

* Vague risk descriptions.
* No risk owner.
* No treatment owner.
* No due dates.
* No residual risk.
* No evidence.
* Outdated information.
* Duplicate risks.
* Risks copied from templates without analysis.
* Risks marked "accepted" without authorization.

These create the appearance of governance without effective governance.

---

# 41. Risk Register Quality Test

Ask five questions:

```text
What is the risk?
      ↓
Who owns it?
      ↓
What are we doing about it?
      ↓
What evidence proves it?
      ↓
What residual risk remains?
```

If the register cannot answer these questions, it needs improvement.

# 06.18 — AI Risk Registers and Risk Treatment Plans

## Part 2 — Risk Scoring, Prioritization, Treatment Planning, and Risk Relationships

### 45. From Risk Identification to Prioritization

Once an AI risk has been documented, the next question is:

> **How serious is the risk, and which risks should receive attention first?**

This is where **risk scoring and prioritization** become important.

A practical sequence is:

```text
Identify
   ↓
Analyze
   ↓
Score
   ↓
Prioritize
   ↓
Treat
   ↓
Monitor
```

The objective is not simply to assign a number.

The objective is to help management determine **where resources and attention are needed most**.

---

# 46. Likelihood and Impact

A common risk methodology evaluates:

* **Likelihood** — how likely the risk is to occur.
* **Impact** — how severe the consequences would be.

A simple model is:

> **Risk = Likelihood × Impact**

For example:

```text id="1r7q5k"
Likelihood = 4
Impact     = 5

Risk Score = 20
```

The organization's approved methodology should determine what the numbers mean.

---

# 47. Example 5×5 Risk Matrix

A common model uses five levels for each dimension:

| Score | Likelihood     | Impact        |
| ----: | -------------- | ------------- |
|     1 | Rare           | Insignificant |
|     2 | Unlikely       | Minor         |
|     3 | Possible       | Moderate      |
|     4 | Likely         | Major         |
|     5 | Almost Certain | Severe        |

The resulting score may then be mapped to:

* Low.
* Moderate.
* High.
* Critical.

The exact thresholds should come from the organization's risk methodology rather than being invented for each assessment.

---

# 48. AI Risk Example

Suppose an AI chatbot processes confidential customer information.

Assessment:

```text id="k0v1pf"
Likelihood = 4
Impact     = 5

Inherent Risk = 20
```

Management may classify this as:

> **Critical**

depending on the approved scoring matrix.

The important point is that the rating should be **defensible and consistently applied**.

---

# 49. Risk Scoring Is Not Risk Judgment

A numerical score is useful, but it should not replace professional judgment.

For example:

> A low-probability event could have catastrophic consequences.

Therefore, GRC should consider:

* Severity.
* Regulatory obligations.
* Human impact.
* Risk velocity.
* Concentration.
* Strategic importance.

---

# 50. Risk Velocity

**Risk velocity** asks:

> **How quickly could the risk cause harm once triggered?**

Example:

### Risk A

AI model slowly deteriorates over six months.

### Risk B

AI agent can execute a destructive action within seconds.

Both may have similar likelihood and impact, but Risk B may require stronger immediate controls.

---

# 51. Risk Proximity

Risk proximity asks:

> **How close is the organization to experiencing the risk?**

For example:

> A known vulnerability already exists in a production AI system.

That is different from:

> A theoretical vulnerability might exist in a future AI system.

Proximity can influence prioritization.

---

# 52. Risk Exposure

Risk exposure can be influenced by:

* Number of affected systems.
* Number of users.
* Data sensitivity.
* Transaction volume.
* Degree of autonomy.
* External accessibility.
* Business criticality.

Therefore, two systems using the same AI model may have different risk levels.

---

# 53. AI Risk Prioritization

A practical prioritization sequence:

```text id="e7m1s4"
Critical
   ↓
Immediate attention

High
   ↓
Priority treatment

Moderate
   ↓
Planned treatment

Low
   ↓
Monitor / manage
```

The actual response should follow organizational policy.

---

# 54. Risk Appetite

Risk prioritization must be connected to **risk appetite**.

Risk appetite represents the amount and type of risk an organization is willing to pursue or retain in achieving its objectives.

Example:

```text id="w2q5m8"
Residual Risk
     ↓
Within Appetite?
   ↙        ↘
 YES        NO
 ↓           ↓
Accept      Treat /
Monitor     Escalate
```

---

# 55. Risk Tolerance

**Risk appetite** and **risk tolerance** are related but different.

### Appetite

Broad willingness to take risk.

### Tolerance

Specific acceptable variation or boundary around an objective.

Example:

> Organization has low appetite for unauthorized disclosure of customer data.

A tolerance threshold might establish specific measurable limits.

---

# 56. Risk Capacity

**Risk capacity** represents the maximum amount of risk the organization can absorb before its viability or objectives are seriously threatened.

Conceptually:

```text id="5i7q0v"
Risk Appetite
      ↓
Risk Tolerance
      ↓
Risk Capacity
```

These concepts should not be treated as interchangeable.

---

# 57. Treatment Planning

Once a risk has been prioritized, the organization develops a treatment plan.

A strong treatment plan answers:

1. What needs to change?
2. Why is it needed?
3. Who owns it?
4. What control will be implemented?
5. When will it be completed?
6. What evidence will demonstrate completion?
7. What residual risk is expected afterward?

---

# 58. Treatment Plan Structure

Example:

| Field        | Example                    |
| ------------ | -------------------------- |
| Risk         | AI data leakage            |
| Current Risk | Critical                   |
| Treatment    | Reduce                     |
| Action       | Implement AI gateway + DLP |
| Owner        | Security Engineering       |
| Due Date     | 30 Sept                    |
| Target Risk  | Moderate                   |
| Evidence     | DLP test results           |
| Status       | In Progress                |

---

# 59. Treatment Objective

A treatment plan should define the desired outcome.

Weak:

> "Improve security."

Strong:

> "Reduce unauthorized transmission of restricted information through external AI services to an acceptable residual risk level."

The second statement gives the treatment a measurable objective.

---

# 60. Treatment Actions

Actions should be:

* Specific.
* Assigned.
* Measurable.
* Time-bound.
* Evidence-based.

Example:

> Implement DLP policies that detect and block defined confidential-data patterns before information is transmitted to external AI services.

---

# 61. Action Dependencies

Some treatment actions depend on other actions.

Example:

```text id="x5v8n1"
Data Classification
      ↓
DLP Policy
      ↓
AI Gateway Enforcement
      ↓
Testing
```

If data classification is incomplete, the DLP implementation may not work effectively.

Dependencies should therefore be documented.

---

# 62. Treatment Milestones

For large AI programs, use milestones.

Example:

```text id="w8m4r1"
Milestone 1
Policy approved
      ↓
Milestone 2
Architecture implemented
      ↓
Milestone 3
Controls configured
      ↓
Milestone 4
Testing completed
      ↓
Milestone 5
Production validation
```

This provides better visibility than one large "remediation complete" task.

---

# 63. Treatment Priority

Not every remediation action has equal urgency.

Prioritize based on:

* Risk severity.
* Risk velocity.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Business criticality.
* Exploitability.
* Exposure.
* Dependencies.

A critical exploitable weakness should generally receive more attention than a minor documentation gap.

---

# 64. Quick Wins

Some risks can be reduced rapidly.

Example:

> Employees are using unauthorized AI tools.

Immediate actions could include:

* Publish interim guidance.
* Restrict known high-risk services.
* Establish approved AI tools.
* Communicate prohibited data types.

These can reduce exposure while longer-term controls are developed.

---

# 65. Strategic Treatment

Other risks require larger programs.

Example:

> Enterprise-wide AI governance.

Treatment may involve:

```text id="5z1x8u"
AI Policy
+
AI Inventory
+
Risk Framework
+
Vendor Governance
+
Security Architecture
+
Monitoring
+
Training
```

This becomes a strategic workstream rather than a single remediation task.

---

# 66. Compensating Controls

When the preferred control cannot immediately be implemented, compensating controls may temporarily reduce exposure.

Example:

Preferred:

> Automated DLP enforcement.

Temporary:

> Restrict AI access to approved users + manual review + restricted data.

The compensating control should still be assessed for effectiveness.

---

# 67. Treatment Trade-Offs

Controls can introduce:

* Cost.
* Complexity.
* Performance impact.
* User friction.
* Operational overhead.

GRC should consider whether the treatment provides proportionate risk reduction.

The goal is:

> **Appropriate risk reduction, not uncontrolled accumulation of controls.**

---

# 68. Risk Reduction

Treatment effectiveness can be demonstrated by comparing risk before and after treatment.

Example:

```text id="m7x4k2"
Inherent Risk = Critical
        ↓
Treatment
        ↓
Residual Risk = Moderate
```

The reduction should be supported by evidence.

---

# 69. Risk Treatment Does Not Always Mean Lowering the Score

Sometimes treatment changes the nature of the risk.

Example:

Instead of allowing:

> Fully autonomous AI decisions.

The organization changes the architecture to:

> AI recommendation + mandatory human approval.

This may fundamentally change:

* Impact.
* Likelihood.
* Exposure.
* Control requirements.

Therefore, reassessment is necessary.

---

# 70. Risk Relationships

AI risks rarely exist independently.

One risk may create another.

Example:

```text id="3x7v2a"
Poor Data Quality
      ↓
Incorrect Model Output
      ↓
Bad Business Decision
      ↓
Customer Impact
```

These are **related risks**.

---

# 71. Parent and Child Risks

An enterprise AI risk may have supporting risks.

Example:

### Parent Risk

> AI governance failure.

### Child risks

* No AI inventory.
* Unclear ownership.
* Inadequate risk assessment.
* Weak vendor governance.
* Poor monitoring.

This structure can help management understand systemic exposure.

---

# 72. Risk Aggregation

Suppose a company has:

```text id="0m8p4e"
20 AI applications
+
3 common AI vendors
+
1 shared AI gateway
```

A weakness in the common infrastructure could affect many applications.

Therefore, GRC should consider **aggregated exposure**, not only application-level risks.

---

# 73. Common-Cause Risk

A common-cause risk affects multiple systems because they depend on the same component.

Examples:

* Same AI provider.
* Same cloud platform.
* Same model.
* Same identity system.
* Same data pipeline.

This creates concentration risk.

---

# 74. Third-Party Risk Relationship

Consider:

```text id="4f6m2p"
AI Application
      ↓
AI Platform
      ↓
Cloud Provider
      ↓
Infrastructure
```

A failure at one layer can affect the layers above it.

The risk register should identify these dependencies where material.

# 94. Interview Anchor

If asked:

> **"How do you prioritize AI risks and develop a treatment plan?"**

Answer:

> **"I prioritize AI risks using the organization's approved methodology, normally considering likelihood, impact and where appropriate factors such as risk velocity, proximity, concentration and business criticality. I don't rely solely on the numerical score; I also consider risk appetite, regulatory obligations and the potential consequences of the risk. Once prioritized, I define a treatment strategy and convert it into specific actions with clear owners, deadlines, dependencies and measurable target outcomes.**
>
> **For each treatment, I identify the relevant controls and determine how their effectiveness will be demonstrated. Once implementation is complete, I validate the controls using evidence and reassess the residual risk. If the residual exposure is within appetite and delegated authority, I document the decision and monitor it. If it remains outside appetite or exceeds the owner's authority, I escalate it for further treatment or formal acceptance. I also maintain traceability between risks, controls, policies, requirements, exceptions and incidents so that management and auditors can see how the risk is being governed."**

## Part 3 — Risk Ownership, Accountability, Escalation, Monitoring, and Reporting

### 96. Risk Ownership

A risk register is only effective when every material risk has clear accountability.

The first question should be:

> **Who is accountable for the risk?**

This person is the **Risk Owner**.

The risk owner is responsible for ensuring that the risk is appropriately:

* Understood.
* Assessed.
* Treated.
* Accepted or escalated.
* Monitored.

---

# 97. Risk Owner vs. Action Owner

These roles should not be confused.

### Risk Owner

Accountable for the **risk decision**.

### Action Owner

Responsible for completing a **specific treatment action**.

Example:

```text
Risk
AI data leakage
      ↓
Risk Owner
Chief Data Officer
      ↓
Treatment
Implement DLP
      ↓
Action Owner
Security Engineering Manager
```

The security manager implements the control, but the risk owner remains accountable for the overall risk.

---

# 98. Control Owner

There may also be a **Control Owner**.

The control owner is accountable for ensuring that a specific control is:

* Designed.
* Implemented.
* Maintained.
* Tested.
* Operating effectively.

Example:

```text
Risk Owner
     ↓
AI Data Leakage

Control Owner
     ↓
Security Engineering

Action Owner
     ↓
DLP Project Manager
```

In smaller organizations, one person may perform multiple roles.

---

# 99. Business Owner

The **Business Owner** owns the business process or AI use case.

For example:

> Customer Service Director owns the AI chatbot business process.

The security team may own security controls, but the business owner remains accountable for the business use case.

---

# 100. AI System Owner

The AI System Owner is responsible for the AI system itself.

Typical responsibilities may include:

* System lifecycle.
* Configuration.
* Performance.
* Documentation.
* Change management.
* Operational monitoring.

Example:

```text
Business Owner
      ↓
Why are we using AI?

AI System Owner
      ↓
How is the system operated?

Risk Owner
      ↓
How is the risk governed?
```

# 102. The Three Lines Model

AI risk governance can also be understood using the **Three Lines** model.

### First Line

Business and operational teams.

They:

* Own the risks.
* Operate the AI systems.
* Implement controls.

### Second Line

Risk, compliance, security governance and similar functions.

They:

* Establish frameworks.
* Provide guidance.
* Challenge risk decisions.
* Monitor compliance.

### Third Line

Internal Audit.

They:

* Provide independent assurance.

# 103. Important GRC Principle

Second-line GRC should not take ownership of a business risk that belongs to the first line.

Instead:

> **GRC provides challenge, oversight and guidance while management retains accountability for the risk.**

This distinction is important in senior GRC interviews.

# 105. Risk Escalation

Escalation is required when the risk cannot appropriately be managed at the current level.

Triggers may include:

* Risk exceeds appetite.
* Risk exceeds delegated authority.
* Treatment is overdue.
* Control has failed.
* Risk is increasing.
* Regulatory exposure exists.
* Business impact becomes material.

---

# 106. Escalation Is Not Failure

A common misconception is:

> "Escalation means GRC failed."

Actually:

> **Escalation is an important governance mechanism.**

It ensures that decisions are made by someone with the appropriate authority.

---

# 107. Example of Proper Escalation

Suppose:

```text
Residual Risk = Critical
Risk Appetite = Moderate
```

The business owner says:

> "I accept the risk."

If the business owner does not have authority to accept Critical risk:

> GRC should escalate the decision.

The correct response is not to override the business.

It is to ensure the decision reaches the appropriate authority.

# 115. Key Risk Indicators

# 116. KRI vs KPI

### KRI

# 118. Risk Metrics Should Drive Decisions

Weak metric:

> "We have 500 controls."

Better:

> "97% of critical AI controls passed effectiveness testing."

Even better:

> "Three critical AI systems have failed control testing and remain above risk appetite."

The last one is directly actionable.

# 154. End-to-End Example — Customer-Service GenAI

### Business Objective

The organization wants to deploy a generative-AI chatbot to answer customer questions.

The chatbot will:

* Interact with customers.
* Access approved knowledge bases.
* Retrieve customer account information.
* Create service tickets.
* Escalate complex cases to employees.

# 155. Step 1 — Define the AI Use Case

Before assessing risk, understand:

* What the AI does.
* Why the organization uses it.
* What data it processes.
* Who uses it.
* What systems it connects to.
* What decisions it makes.
* What actions it can perform.

Example:

> **AI Customer-Service Assistant — external-facing generative-AI chatbot supporting customer service operations.**

---

# 156. Step 2 — Identify Risks

Potential risks include:

### R1 — Data Leakage

Customer information may be disclosed to unauthorized parties.

### R2 — Hallucination

The AI may provide inaccurate information.

### R3 — Prompt Injection

An attacker may manipulate the AI to bypass intended restrictions.

### R4 — Unauthorized Action

The AI may initiate an inappropriate customer transaction.

### R5 — Availability

Failure of the AI service may disrupt customer support.

### R6 — Third-Party Risk

The external AI provider may experience a security or service failure.

---

# 157. Step 3 — Write Proper Risk Statements

Avoid:

> "Chatbot security."

Instead:

> **"Because the chatbot processes customer information and interacts with external users, malicious or unintended inputs may cause restricted information to be disclosed, resulting in privacy, regulatory, financial and reputational impact."**

This identifies:

```text

 ↓

 ↓
Impact
```

| ------------------- | ---------: | -----: | -------: |

The exact score classifications must follow the organization's approved methodology.

# 159. Step 5 — Identify Existing Controls

Suppose the organization already has:

* Identity management.
* Encryption.
* Network controls.
* Logging.
* Security monitoring.
* Vendor assessments.
* Customer authentication.
* Human escalation.

Document these controls before designing additional treatment.

# 160. Step 6 — Evaluate Control Effectiveness

Example:

### Control

Human approval for high-risk transactions.

### Testing

Security team tests 100 high-risk requests.

### Result

98 correctly required human approval.

Two requests bypassed the approval process.

Therefore:

> The control exists but requires remediation.

This is more meaningful than simply writing:

> "Human approval implemented."

| Unauthorized action | High     | Approval controls    | Moderate |

| Vendor failure      | High     | Vendor governance    | Moderate |

The risk may still be above appetite.

> **Reduce**

* Conduct adversarial testing.
* Implement input filtering.
* Restrict tool access.
* Apply output validation.
* Establish human approval for sensitive actions.
* Monitor suspicious prompts.

# 200. The 06.18 Master Answer

If an interviewer gives you almost any AI GRC risk scenario, you can structure your response like this:

> **"First, I would understand the AI use case, its business purpose, the data involved, the users, integrations and level of autonomy. I would then identify the relevant risks and document them using a clear cause-event-impact structure. I would assess inherent risk using the organization's approved methodology, identify existing controls and evaluate their effectiveness.**
>
> **Based on the residual exposure, I would determine the appropriate treatment strategy—avoid, reduce, share or accept—and translate that into specific actions with accountable owners, deadlines, dependencies, target risk levels and evidence requirements. Once controls are implemented, I would validate their effectiveness rather than assuming implementation means the risk has been addressed.**
>
> **I would then reassess residual risk against the organization's risk appetite and delegated authority. If the risk is within appetite, I would document the decision and establish monitoring. If it remains outside appetite or exceeds the owner's authority, I would escalate it with a clear recommendation and available treatment options. Finally, I would establish monitoring and reassessment triggers so that incidents, control failures, model changes, vendor changes or other material changes can update the risk profile."**
