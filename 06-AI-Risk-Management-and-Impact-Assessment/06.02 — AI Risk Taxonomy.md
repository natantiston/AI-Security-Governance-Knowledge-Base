# 06.02 — AI Risk Taxonomy

## Part 1 — Foundations and Core Categories

### 1. Definition of an AI Risk Taxonomy

An AI risk taxonomy is a structured classification system used to organize the different types of risks that can arise from the development, deployment, operation, and use of AI systems.

Its purpose is to provide a **common language for identifying, assessing, reporting, and managing AI risk**.

Without a taxonomy, organizations may describe similar risks differently:

```text
Security Team → Model Attack
Privacy Team  → Data Exposure
Legal Team    → Compliance Concern
Business Team → Operational Risk
```

A taxonomy provides a common structure:

```text
                    AI RISK
                       │
        ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
        ↓              ↓              ↓
     Security        Privacy       Operational
        │              │              │
     Attacks       Data Use       Reliability
     Access        Disclosure     Availability
     Integrity     Retention      Continuity
```

---

### 2. Purpose of an AI Risk Taxonomy

A taxonomy helps an organization:

* Identify risks consistently.
* Avoid major risk categories being overlooked.
* Assign risk ownership.
* Standardize assessments.
* Develop appropriate controls.
* Compare risks across AI systems.
* Aggregate risks at enterprise level.
* Create meaningful management reports.
* Support audits and assurance.
* Establish a common GRC language.

The taxonomy is therefore a **foundation for structured AI risk management**.

---

### 3. Taxonomy vs Risk Register

These concepts should not be confused.

A **risk taxonomy** defines the categories of risk.

A **risk register** records individual risks.

For example:

```text
AI Risk Taxonomy
      ↓
Security Risk
      ↓
Data Security
      ↓
Unauthorized Disclosure
```

The risk register might then contain:

> R-024 — Customer-support AI may disclose restricted customer information through an unauthorized response.

The taxonomy classifies the risk; the register records the actual risk scenario.

---

### 4. Taxonomy vs Risk Assessment

The taxonomy answers:

> **What type of risk is this?**

The risk assessment answers:

> **How significant is this risk?**

Example:

```text
Taxonomy:
AI Security → Access Control

Risk Assessment:
Likelihood = Medium
Impact = High
Risk = High
```

Classification and assessment are therefore complementary.

---

### 5. Taxonomy vs Risk Framework

A taxonomy is also different from a risk framework.

A **taxonomy** organizes risk categories.

A **framework** defines how the organization manages those risks.

```text
AI Risk Taxonomy
       ↓
What types of risks exist?
       ↓
AI Risk Framework
       ↓
How do we manage them?
       ↓
Risk Assessment
       ↓
How significant are they?
```

---

### 6. Why AI Requires a Specialized Taxonomy

Traditional enterprise risk categories remain relevant to AI.

However, AI introduces additional characteristics such as:

* Model behavior.
* Training-data dependency.
* Generative output.
* Hallucination.
* Prompt manipulation.
* Model drift.
* AI autonomy.
* Human-AI interaction.
* Algorithmic bias.

A useful AI taxonomy therefore combines **traditional enterprise risk categories with AI-specific risk dimensions**.

---

A practical enterprise taxonomy can include:


The exact taxonomy should be adapted to organizational context.

---

### 8. AI Security Risk

AI security risk concerns threats that could compromise the:

* Confidentiality.
* Integrity.
* Availability.
* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Resilience.

Examples include:

* Prompt injection.
* Model manipulation.
* Data poisoning.
* Model theft.
* Unauthorized access.
* AI-enabled attacks.
* Insecure APIs.
* Excessive agent privileges.

Security risk is one of the most important components of the AI risk taxonomy.

---

### 9. AI Privacy Risk

Privacy risk concerns inappropriate or unlawful processing of personal information by AI systems.

Examples include:

* Excessive data collection.
* Unauthorized processing.
* Data leakage.
* Improper retention.
* Inappropriate secondary use.
* Inadequate transparency.
* Inappropriate profiling.

Privacy risk can exist even when there is no cybersecurity attack.

---

### 10. AI Data Risk

Data risk concerns the quality, integrity, availability, provenance, and suitability of data used by AI.

Examples include:

* Poor-quality training data.
* Incomplete data.
* Incorrect data.
* Unrepresentative data.
* Unauthorized data.
* Corrupted data.
* Missing provenance.
* Inadequate data lineage.

Data quality can directly influence AI output quality.

---

### 11. AI Model Risk

Model risk concerns the possibility that an AI model does not perform as intended.

Examples include:

* Incorrect predictions.
* Hallucination.
* Model instability.
* Poor generalization.
* Model drift.
* Unexpected behavior.
* Inappropriate model selection.
* Inadequate validation.

Model risk is particularly important where AI outputs influence material decisions.

---

### 12. AI Performance and Reliability Risk

Performance and reliability risk concerns whether an AI system consistently performs its intended function.

Potential problems include:

* High error rates.
* Unstable outputs.
* Latency.
* Availability problems.
* Model degradation.
* Inconsistent responses.
* Failure under unusual conditions.

A model can be secure but still unreliable.

---

### 13. AI Operational Risk

Operational risk concerns failures in the processes, systems, people, or procedures surrounding AI.

Examples:

* Poor deployment processes.
* Inadequate change management.
* Weak incident response.
* Incorrect configuration.
* Insufficient monitoring.
* Operational dependency on AI.

Operational risk focuses on how AI functions within the organization.

---

### 14. Legal and Regulatory Risk

AI systems may create legal or regulatory exposure.

Examples include:

* Non-compliance with applicable AI regulations.
* Privacy violations.
* Intellectual-property disputes.
* Contractual breaches.
* Consumer-protection issues.
* Employment-related legal exposure.

The applicable requirements depend on jurisdiction, sector, and use case.

---

### 15. Compliance Risk

Compliance risk concerns failure to satisfy:

* Internal policies.
* External regulations.
* Industry standards.
* Contractual requirements.
* Governance requirements.

For example:

> An organization may have a policy requiring high-risk AI systems to undergo formal approval. Deploying such a system without approval creates compliance risk.

---

### 16. Ethical and Responsible AI Risk

Ethical and responsible AI risk concerns whether AI is used in ways inconsistent with organizational principles or accepted responsible-AI expectations.

Potential issues include:

* Unfair outcomes.
* Lack of transparency.
* Lack of accountability.
* Manipulative behavior.
* Inappropriate automation.
* Misuse of AI capabilities.

Ethical risk may overlap with legal risk but is not necessarily identical to it.

---

### 17. Human and Social Risk

Human and social risk concerns consequences for individuals and society.

Examples include:

* Discrimination.
* Social exclusion.
* Loss of opportunity.
* Psychological harm.
* Manipulation.
* Over-reliance on automation.
* Unsafe decisions.
* Negative societal effects.

This category expands risk management beyond the organization's immediate interests.

---

### 18. Financial Risk

AI can create direct and indirect financial exposure.

Examples:

* Incorrect financial decisions.
* Fraud.
* Unauthorized transactions.
* AI service costs.
* Business interruption.
* Regulatory penalties.
* Litigation costs.

Financial risk should consider both probability and potential magnitude.

---

### 19. Strategic Risk

Strategic AI risk concerns whether AI affects the organization's long-term objectives.

Examples include:

* Excessive dependence on one AI provider.
* Failure to adopt strategically important AI.
* Poor AI investment decisions.
* Competitive disadvantage.
* AI capability misalignment.
* Rapid technology changes.

Strategic risk is generally considered at an enterprise level.

---

### 20. Third-Party AI Risk

Organizations frequently depend on external AI providers.

Risks may arise from:

* Provider security weaknesses.
* Provider outages.
* Model changes.
* Data-processing practices.
* Subprocessors.
* Contractual limitations.
* Vendor concentration.

Third-party risk should therefore be represented explicitly in the AI taxonomy.

---

### 21. AI Supply Chain Risk

The AI supply chain can include:

```text
Data Provider
      ↓
Model Developer
      ↓
Model Repository
      ↓
AI Framework
      ↓
Cloud Provider
      ↓
AI Platform
      ↓
Enterprise Application
      ↓
End User
```

A weakness anywhere in this chain can potentially affect the final AI system.

---

### 22. Reputational Risk

AI failures can damage organizational reputation.

Examples include:

* Public AI failures.
* Biased outcomes.
* Customer harm.
* Data exposure.
* Inappropriate AI-generated content.
* Misleading automated communications.

Reputational impact may extend beyond direct financial losses.

---

### 23. Environmental Risk

AI systems can have environmental implications.

Potential considerations include:

* Energy consumption.
* Computing resource requirements.
* Data-center impact.
* Hardware lifecycle.
* Resource consumption associated with large-scale model development.

Environmental risk may be particularly relevant for organizations with sustainability commitments or reporting obligations.

---

### 24. AI Governance Risk

Governance risk concerns weaknesses in organizational oversight.

Examples include:

* No AI inventory.
* Unclear ownership.
* Missing policies.
* Inadequate approval processes.
* Weak risk acceptance.
* Poor documentation.
* Insufficient monitoring.
* Lack of independent assurance.

A technically secure AI system can still have significant governance risk.

---

### 25. AI Risk Categories Are Interconnected

Risk categories should not be treated as isolated boxes.

For example:

```text
Poor Data
    ↓
Model Error
    ↓
Incorrect AI Output
    ↓
Operational Failure
    ↓
Customer Harm
    ↓
Legal Exposure
    ↓
Reputational Damage
```

One AI event can therefore produce several risk categories simultaneously.

---

### 26. Primary Risk vs Secondary Risk

A risk assessment can distinguish between:

**Primary risk**

The immediate risk created by a condition.

**Secondary risk**

Additional risks resulting from the primary risk.

Example:

```text
Primary:
AI model manipulated

        ↓

Secondary:
Incorrect outputs

        ↓

Secondary:
Business decision failure

        ↓

Secondary:
Financial / regulatory impact
```

This helps organizations understand risk propagation.

---

### 27. Risk Category Hierarchy

A mature taxonomy should have multiple levels.

For example:

```text
Level 1: AI Risk
    ↓
Level 2: Security Risk
    ↓
Level 3: AI Application Security
    ↓
Level 4: Prompt Injection
    ↓
Level 5: Specific Risk Scenario
```

This hierarchical structure improves consistency and reporting.

### 31. Risk Taxonomy and Assessment Questions

Each category can have specialized assessment questions.

**Security**

> Can the AI system be compromised or abused?

**Privacy**

> Does the AI process personal information appropriately?

**Data**

> Is the data accurate, representative, authorized, and traceable?

**Model**

> Does the model perform reliably for its intended purpose?

**Operational**

> Can the organization operate and recover the AI system effectively?

**Governance**

> Are ownership, approval, monitoring, and accountability established?

### 34. AI Risk Taxonomy and Risk Appetite

Different risk categories may have different organizational tolerances.

For example:

```text

```

### 35. AI Risk Taxonomy and Risk Aggregation

A taxonomy allows risks to be aggregated by category.

For example:

```text
Enterprise AI Risk
       │
       ├── Security: 18 risks
       ├── Privacy: 9 risks
       ├── Model: 14 risks
       ├── Operational: 11 risks
```

---

### 36. Taxonomy and Risk Concentration

If many AI systems share the same weakness, the taxonomy can reveal systemic exposure.

Example:

```text
20 AI Systems
      ↓
Same Third-Party Provider
      ↓
Third-Party Risk
      ↓
Enterprise Concentration
```

This is more useful than treating every system independently.

---

### 37. Taxonomy and Emerging AI Risks

The taxonomy should evolve.

New AI capabilities may introduce new risks such as:

* Autonomous agents.
* Multimodal AI.
* AI-generated code.
* AI-to-AI interaction.
* Tool-using systems.
* Autonomous workflows.

A taxonomy should therefore be periodically reviewed.

---

### 38. Taxonomy Governance

The organization should establish ownership of the taxonomy itself.

Responsibilities may include:

* Maintaining definitions.
* Adding new categories.
* Removing obsolete categories.
* Reviewing overlaps.
* Aligning with regulations.
* Aligning with standards.
* Communicating changes.

Without taxonomy governance, classification can become inconsistent.

---

### 39. Taxonomy Version Control

Changes to the taxonomy should be controlled.

For example:

```text

```

Version control supports traceability and auditability.

### 41. Practical GRC Principle

A good AI risk taxonomy should be:

**Comprehensive**

It should cover the major dimensions of AI risk.

**Consistent**

Different teams should classify similar risks similarly.

**Actionable**

Categories should help determine ownership and controls.

**Flexible**

The taxonomy should adapt to new AI technologies.

**Non-overlapping where possible**

Categories should minimize unnecessary duplication.

**Business-relevant**

The taxonomy should support meaningful management decisions.

---

### 42. Taxonomy Quality Test

An organization can test its taxonomy by asking:

1. Can every material AI risk be classified?
2. Can similar risks be classified consistently?
3. Can each category have an accountable owner?
4. Can controls be mapped to categories?
5. Can risks be aggregated for management reporting?
6. Can the taxonomy support different AI use cases?
7. Can new AI risks be incorporated without redesigning the entire structure?

If the answer is yes, the taxonomy is likely useful operationally.

### 49. Avoiding an Overly Complex Taxonomy

A taxonomy can become counterproductive if it contains too many categories.

For example:

```text

```

This may create classification confusion.

The objective should be:

> **Enough structure to create consistency without creating unnecessary complexity.**

---

### 50. Final AI Risk Taxonomy Principle

The most important principle is:

> **An AI risk taxonomy provides the organizational language for describing where AI risk originates and what type of exposure it creates.**

It should allow the organization to move from:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Risk Scenario
    ↓
Risk Category
    ↓
Risk Owner
    ↓
Control
    ↓
Risk Assessment
    ↓
Management Decision
```

A strong taxonomy does not eliminate AI risk.

It makes AI risk **visible, consistently classified, assignable, measurable, and manageable**.

# 06.02 — AI Risk Taxonomy

## Part 2 — Detailed AI Risk Categories and Taxonomy Structure

### 51. The Multi-Dimensional Nature of AI Risk

AI risk should not be treated as a single category.

A mature taxonomy recognizes that an AI system can create risk through multiple dimensions simultaneously:

```text
                         AI SYSTEM
                             │
       ┌─────────────┬───────┼───────┬──────────────┐
       ↓             ↓       ↓       ↓              ↓
      Data         Model   Security Privacy      Human
       │             │       │       │              │
       └─────────────┴───────┼───────┴──────────────┘
                             ↓
                     Business Impact
```

This means a single AI use case may need several risk classifications.

---

### 52. Source-Based AI Risk Taxonomy

One useful way to organize AI risk is according to **where the risk originates**.

```text
AI Risk Sources
│
├── Data
├── Model
├── Technology
├── People
├── Process
├── Vendor
├── Environment
└── Governance
```

This approach helps answer:

> **"What is causing or contributing to the risk?"**

---

### 53. Outcome-Based AI Risk Taxonomy

Another approach is to classify risk according to the **type of consequence**.

```text
AI Risk Outcomes
│
├── Security Harm
├── Privacy Harm
├── Financial Harm
├── Operational Harm
├── Legal Harm
├── Human Harm
├── Social Harm
├── Environmental Harm
└── Reputational Harm
```

This answers:

> **"What could happen if the risk materializes?"**

Both source-based and outcome-based views can be useful.

---

### 54. Source vs Outcome

These two perspectives should not be confused.

Example:

```text
Source:
Poor training data

        ↓

Mechanism:
Model produces inaccurate results

        ↓

Outcome:
Incorrect customer decision

        ↓

Potential Impact:
Financial + Regulatory + Reputational
```

The **source**, **risk event**, and **impact** are different elements.

---

### 55. Data Risk Taxonomy

Data risk can be decomposed into several subcategories:

```text
Data Risk
│
├── Data Quality
├── Data Integrity
├── Data Availability
├── Data Provenance
├── Data Lineage
├── Data Representativeness
├── Data Governance
├── Data Security
└── Data Usage
```

This allows an organization to identify the specific data-related weakness.

---

### 56. Data Quality Risk

Data quality risk occurs when data is:

* Inaccurate.
* Incomplete.
* Inconsistent.
* Outdated.
* Duplicated.
* Irrelevant.

Poor data quality can produce unreliable AI outcomes.

```text
Poor Data
   ↓
Poor Learning / Retrieval
   ↓
Poor Output
   ↓
Poor Decision
```

---

### 57. Data Representativeness Risk

Data may be technically accurate but still not adequately represent the population or environment in which the AI operates.

For example:

```text
Training Population
        ↓
Limited Representation
        ↓
Deployment to Broader Population
        ↓
Unequal Performance
```

This can contribute to fairness and performance risks.

---

### 58. Data Provenance Risk

Data provenance concerns the ability to determine:

* Where data came from.
* Who supplied it.
* When it was collected.
* How it was modified.
* Whether its use is authorized.

Weak provenance makes it difficult to establish whether data is trustworthy and appropriately used.

---

### 59. Data Lineage Risk

Data lineage tracks how information moves through the AI lifecycle.

```text
Source
  ↓
Collection
  ↓
Transformation
  ↓
Storage
  ↓
Training / Retrieval
  ↓
AI Processing
  ↓
Output
```

Weak lineage can make investigation and accountability difficult.

---

### 60. Model Risk Taxonomy

Model risk can be divided into:

```text
Model Risk
│
├── Model Selection
├── Model Design
├── Model Training
├── Model Validation
├── Model Performance
├── Model Drift
├── Model Robustness
├── Model Explainability
└── Model Change
```

Different models can therefore fail in different ways.

---

### 61. Model Selection Risk

An organization may choose an inappropriate model for the intended use case.

For example:

> A lightweight model may be adequate for document classification but inappropriate for a high-impact decision requiring much greater accuracy and robustness.

Model selection should therefore consider the intended purpose.

---

### 62. Model Validation Risk

A model may appear effective during development but fail under real-world conditions.

Validation should consider:

* Intended population.
* Expected data.
* Edge cases.
* Error rates.
* Security conditions.
* Operational environment.

Validation should provide evidence that the model is appropriate for its intended use.

---

### 63. Model Drift Risk

Model performance can change over time.

```text
Initial Deployment
      ↓
Good Performance
      ↓
Environment Changes
      ↓
Data Distribution Changes
      ↓
Performance Degrades
```

This creates the need for ongoing monitoring.

---

### 64. Model Robustness Risk

Robustness concerns how well the AI behaves when conditions differ from normal expectations.

Examples include:

* Unexpected inputs.
* Noisy data.
* Adversarial inputs.
* Unusual user behavior.
* Edge cases.

A robust AI system should maintain acceptable behavior under reasonably foreseeable conditions.

---

### 65. Model Explainability Risk

Some AI systems can be difficult to explain.

This becomes important when users need to understand:

* Why a result was produced.
* What information influenced the result.
* What limitations exist.
* How to challenge or review the output.

Explainability requirements should be proportionate to the use case and applicable requirements.

---

### 66. AI Security Risk Taxonomy

Security risk can be decomposed into:

```text
AI Security
│
├── Identity & Access
├── Confidentiality
├── Integrity
├── Availability
├── Prompt / Input Security
├── Output Security
├── Model Security
├── Application Security
├── Infrastructure Security
└── Supply Chain Security
```

This connects AI risk management with traditional cybersecurity while recognizing AI-specific attack surfaces.

---

### 67. Identity and Access Risk

AI systems may have identities and permissions just like other enterprise systems.

Risk can arise when:

* Users have excessive access.
* AI agents have excessive privileges.
* Service accounts are poorly protected.
* Authentication is weak.
* Authorization is incorrectly implemented.

The principle of least privilege remains important.

---

### 68. AI Confidentiality Risk

Confidentiality risk concerns unauthorized disclosure of:

* Business information.
* Customer information.
* Source code.
* Credentials.
* Intellectual property.
* Personal information.

Generative AI can create additional confidentiality concerns because users may unintentionally include sensitive information in prompts.

---

### 69. AI Integrity Risk

Integrity risk concerns unauthorized or unintended modification of:

* Training data.
* Reference data.
* Models.
* Prompts.
* Configurations.
* AI outputs.
* Downstream business records.

Integrity failures can result in incorrect decisions or actions.

---

### 70. AI Availability Risk

Availability risk concerns the inability to access or operate AI services when required.

Potential causes include:

* Provider outages.
* Infrastructure failures.
* API limits.
* Resource exhaustion.
* Network failures.
* Denial-of-service attacks.

Critical AI systems may require fallback or manual processes.

---

### 71. AI Input Security Risk

AI systems may receive malicious or manipulated inputs.

Examples include:

* Prompt injection.
* Malicious documents.
* Crafted queries.
* Poisoned data.
* Adversarial examples.

### 101. Core GRC Principle

The key principle of AI risk taxonomy is:

> **Classify AI risk according to its source, nature, and potential consequence so that the organization can consistently assign ownership, assess significance, select controls, and aggregate risk.**

A taxonomy should make risk easier to understand—not more complicated.

# 06.02 — AI Risk Taxonomy

## Part 3 — Advanced Classification, Risk Relationships, and Practical Taxonomy Application

### 102. Risk Taxonomy Should Describe the Complete Risk Chain

A mature taxonomy should allow an organization to describe the relationship between:

```text
Risk Source
    ↓
Vulnerability / Weakness
    ↓
Threat or Trigger
    ↓
AI Risk Event
    ↓
Impact
    ↓
Business Consequence
```

For example:

```text
Excessive AI Permissions
        ↓
Weak Authorization
        ↓
Malicious Prompt / Compromised Account
        ↓
Unauthorized AI Action
        ↓
System Modification
        ↓
Operational + Financial Impact
```

This structure prevents the taxonomy from becoming merely a list of labels.

---

### 103. Risk Source

A risk source is the underlying condition from which risk can originate.

Common AI risk sources include:

* Data.
* Model.
* Application.
* Infrastructure.
* User.
* Process.
* Vendor.
* Governance.
* External environment.

Example:

> Poor-quality training data is the risk source.

It is not itself necessarily the complete risk scenario.

---

### 104. Vulnerability or Weakness

A vulnerability is a weakness that can contribute to a risk event.

Examples:

* Excessive privileges.
* Missing validation.
* Poor data quality.
* Inadequate monitoring.
* Weak authentication.
* Missing human review.

The taxonomy can associate these weaknesses with relevant risk categories.

---

### 105. Threat or Trigger

A threat or trigger is something that can cause a risk scenario to materialize.

Examples:

* Malicious user.
* Cyberattack.
* Provider outage.
* Model update.
* Data change.
* Human error.
* Regulatory change.

The same vulnerability may produce different outcomes depending on the trigger.

---

### 106. AI Risk Event

The risk event describes what could actually happen.

For example:

> An AI agent executes an unauthorized administrative action.

This is more useful than simply recording:

> "Security risk."

The risk event should be sufficiently specific to support assessment and treatment.

---

### 107. Risk Impact

Impact describes the consequence if the event occurs.

Possible impact dimensions include:

```text
Confidentiality
Integrity
Availability
Financial
Operational
Legal
Regulatory
Human
Social
Reputational
Strategic
Environmental
```

A single event can have multiple impacts.

---

### 108. Business Consequence

The final stage is the effect on organizational objectives.

For example:

```text
AI Model Error
      ↓
Incorrect Decision
      ↓
Customer Complaint
      ↓
Regulatory Investigation
      ↓
Financial Loss + Reputation Damage
```

This connects technical AI risk to enterprise risk.

---

### 109. Taxonomy Should Support Multiple Dimensions

One AI risk may need several classifications.

Example:

> An AI recruiting system systematically produces poorer outcomes for a particular population.

Possible classifications:

* Model risk.
* Data risk.
* Fairness risk.
* Human/social risk.
* Legal/regulatory risk.
* Reputational risk.

A mature taxonomy should allow these relationships rather than forcing an artificial single-category classification.

---

### 110. Hierarchical Taxonomy

A hierarchical taxonomy provides increasing levels of specificity.

Example:

```text
Level 1
AI Risk

Level 2
Security Risk

Level 3
Access Control

Level 4
Excessive Privilege

Level 5
Unauthorized AI Action

Level 6
Specific Business Impact
```

Higher levels support executive reporting.

Lower levels support operational management.

---

### 111. Taxonomy Depth

Too little depth creates ambiguity.

Too much depth creates administrative complexity.

For example:

```text
Too Broad:
Security Risk

Better:
Security
 → Access Control
 → Excessive Privilege
```

But an organization should avoid creating hundreds of narrowly defined categories that users cannot apply consistently.

---

### 112. Taxonomy Breadth

Breadth refers to how many major risk domains are represented.

A narrow taxonomy might contain only:

```text
Security
Privacy
Model
```

A broader enterprise taxonomy may also include:

```text
Operational
Financial
Legal
Regulatory
Human
Social
Strategic
Third Party
Reputational
Environmental
Governance
```

The appropriate breadth depends on organizational objectives and risk context.

---

### 113. Risk Category Definitions

Every taxonomy category should have a clear definition.

For example:

**Model Risk**

> The possibility that an AI model performs inaccurately, unreliably, or inappropriately for its intended purpose.

**Privacy Risk**

> The possibility that AI processing results in inappropriate handling, disclosure, retention, or use of personal information.

Definitions should reduce ambiguity between teams.

---

### 114. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

A strong taxonomy can define what belongs in a category and what does not.

For example:

**Security Risk**

Includes:

* Unauthorized access.
* Model compromise.
* Prompt injection.
* Data poisoning.

Does not necessarily include:

* Poor model accuracy without a security cause.

This prevents different teams from classifying the same issue inconsistently.

---

### 115. Mutually Exclusive vs Overlapping Categories

Some taxonomies attempt to make categories mutually exclusive.

However, AI risk often does not naturally behave that way.

For example:

```text
Prompt Injection
      ↓
Security Risk
      ↓
Potential Data Exposure
      ↓
Privacy Risk
```

Therefore, overlapping categories or secondary classifications may be appropriate.

---

### 116. Risk Tags

* Cloud.
* Open Source.
* Regulated Process.

Tags provide additional analytical dimensions without creating excessive taxonomy categories.

### 117. Risk Domain vs Risk Attribute

A **risk domain** identifies what kind of risk exists.

A **risk attribute** describes characteristics of that risk.

Example:

```text
Risk Domain:
Security

Risk Attribute:
External Exposure = High

Risk Attribute:
Autonomy = High

Risk Attribute:
Data Sensitivity = Restricted
```

These should not be confused.

```text id="finaltax"
   ├── Scale
   ├── Criticality
   ├── Exposure
   └── Regulatory Relevance
   ↓
POTENTIAL IMPACT
   ├── Security
   ├── Privacy
   ├── Financial
   ├── Operational
   ├── Human
   ├── Legal
   └── Reputational
   ↓
BUSINESS CONSEQUENCE
```

This provides a detailed representation of the risk without confusing **classification** with **risk scoring**.

---

### 151. Professional GRC Principle

The most important advanced principle is:

> **A risk taxonomy should provide enough structure to consistently describe the source, nature, affected domain, and potential consequence of AI risk while remaining flexible enough to accommodate different AI technologies and emerging risks.**

The taxonomy is therefore not simply a list.

It is a **controlled classification architecture** connecting AI systems to risk scenarios, ownership, controls, evidence, reporting, and governance decisions.

## Part 4 — Enterprise Implementation, Governance, and GRC Application

### 152. From Taxonomy Design to Enterprise Implementation

Creating an AI risk taxonomy is only the beginning.

An organization must operationalize it so that business, cybersecurity, privacy, legal, compliance, data, and AI teams use the same classification approach.

A practical implementation model is:

```text
Taxonomy Design
      ↓
Taxonomy Approval
      ↓
Classification Guidance
      ↓
GRC Implementation
      ↓
Risk Identification
      ↓
Risk Classification
      ↓
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Risk Treatment
      ↓
Monitoring & Reporting
```

The taxonomy becomes useful only when it is embedded into normal governance processes.

---

### 153. Establish Taxonomy Ownership

The organization should designate an accountable owner for the taxonomy.

A typical structure may be:

```text
Executive AI Governance
        ↓
AI Governance / GRC
        ↓
Risk Taxonomy Owner
        ↓
Security | Privacy | Legal | Data | Business SMEs
```

The taxonomy owner is responsible for maintaining consistency.

---

### 154. Define Taxonomy Governance Rules

The organization should establish rules covering:

* Who can modify the taxonomy.
* Who approves changes.
* How new categories are created.
* How duplicate categories are handled.
* How obsolete categories are retired.
* How taxonomy versions are maintained.
* How changes are communicated.
* How classification disputes are resolved.

This prevents uncontrolled growth of risk categories.

---

### 155. Taxonomy Version Control

Every approved taxonomy should have a version.

Example:

```text
AI Risk Taxonomy v1.0
        ↓
Initial Enterprise Taxonomy

v1.1
        ↓
New Generative AI Subcategories

v1.2
        ↓
Agentic AI Risk Added

v2.0
        ↓
Major Structural Revision
```

Historical assessments should retain the taxonomy version used at the time of assessment.

---

### 156. Taxonomy Change Request

A formal change request can contain:

| Field              | Example           |

This creates an auditable taxonomy-change process.

---

### 157. Avoiding Taxonomy Proliferation

One common governance problem is creating a new category for every new risk.

For example:

```text
Prompt Injection
Prompt Manipulation
Prompt Attack
Malicious Prompt
Prompt Exploitation
```

These may represent the same underlying risk family.

Instead, the organization should determine whether they can be represented as:

```text
Security Risk
    ↓
AI Application Security
    ↓
Prompt Injection
```

This keeps the taxonomy manageable.

---

### 158. Avoiding Taxonomy Gaps

The opposite problem is also possible.

If the taxonomy contains:

```text
Security
Privacy
Operational
Model
```

but has no way to classify:

> AI discrimination affecting access to employment,

important risk dimensions may be lost.

A mature taxonomy should therefore be tested against realistic AI use cases.

---

### 159. Taxonomy Coverage Testing

The organization can test coverage using representative systems:

```text

AI Coding Assistant
Fraud Detection Model
Recruitment AI
Medical AI
AI Agent
Recommendation Engine
Computer Vision System
```

For each system, ask:

> Can all material risks be appropriately classified?

If not, the taxonomy may require refinement.

### 160. AI Risk Classification Workflow

A practical workflow can be:

```text

        ↓
2. Define intended purpose
        ↓
3. Identify risk scenario
        ↓
4. Identify risk source
        ↓

        ↓

        ↓
7. Add relevant attributes
        ↓
8. Identify potential impacts
        ↓

        ↓

```

Classification should occur before detailed risk scoring.

### 161. Primary Risk Category

Each risk should normally have one primary category.

For example:

> AI agent executes an unauthorized transaction because its permissions are excessive.

Primary:

**Security Risk**

Secondary:

* Operational.
* Financial.
* Governance.

This gives reporting a consistent anchor.

---

### 162. Secondary Risk Categories

Secondary classifications capture additional consequences or dimensions.

Example:

```text
Primary:
Model Risk

Secondary:
Fairness
Human Impact
Regulatory
Reputational
```

Secondary classifications should be used deliberately rather than indiscriminately.

---

### 163. Risk Scenario Naming Standard

Organizations should establish a consistent naming convention.

A useful format is:

> **[Cause/Condition] may result in [Risk Event], causing [Impact].**

Example:

> Inadequate validation of an AI model may result in inaccurate customer decisions, causing financial and reputational impact.

This is much more useful than:

> "AI Model Risk."

---

### 164. Taxonomy and Risk Statements

A strong risk statement connects the taxonomy to the actual risk.

Example:

```text
Category:
Security

Risk:
Excessive AI-agent privileges may allow unauthorized
actions against enterprise systems.
```

The category classifies the risk.

The risk statement describes the actual exposure.

### 165. Taxonomy and Risk Identification

During risk identification, assessors should not start by selecting categories randomly.

Instead:

```text

      ↓
AI Function
      ↓

      ↓

      ↓
Risk Scenario
      ↓
Taxonomy Classification
```

This keeps classification evidence-based.

### 166. Taxonomy and Risk Analysis

For example:

```text
   ↓
```

It does not determine the risk score.

### 191. Common Taxonomy Implementation Mistakes

Organizations should avoid:

**1. Creating too many categories**

This makes classification difficult.

**2. Creating categories without definitions**

Different teams interpret them differently.

**3. Treating taxonomy as static**

AI technology changes rapidly.

**4. Confusing risk categories with risk scores**

Classification and scoring are separate activities.

**5. Using only cybersecurity categories**

AI risk extends beyond cybersecurity.

**6. Ignoring business context**

The same AI capability can have different risk depending on its purpose.

---

### 192. Another Common Mistake — Technology-Centric Taxonomy

An organization may build a taxonomy entirely around technology:

```text
LLM
Computer Vision
Machine Learning
RAG
AI Agent
```

This can be useful as an **AI technology classification**, but it is not sufficient as a risk taxonomy.

The taxonomy should describe risk, not merely technology.

---

### 193. Technology Classification vs Risk Classification

These should be separate dimensions.

```text
AI Technology
     ↓
Generative AI

Risk Category
     ↓
Privacy Risk

Risk Scenario
     ↓
Sensitive information disclosure
```

This distinction makes the GRC model more flexible.

---

### 194. Taxonomy Should Remain Technology-Neutral

A strong risk category should generally remain useful even when technology changes.

For example:

> Unauthorized access

can apply to:

* Traditional ML.
* Generative AI.
* AI agents.
* Computer vision.
* Future AI architectures.

This makes the taxonomy more durable.

### 199. Interview-Level Explanation

If asked:

> **"How would you develop an AI risk taxonomy?"**

A strong answer would be:

> "I would first identify the organization's AI use cases, business processes, technologies, data types, and regulatory context. I would then establish high-level risk domains such as security, privacy, data, model, operational, legal, human, third-party, financial, and governance risk. I would define clear subcategories and classification criteria, establish primary and secondary classifications, and connect the taxonomy to risk owners, controls, assessments, and GRC reporting. Finally, I would govern the taxonomy through version control, periodic reviews, emerging-risk analysis, and quality assurance."

If you remember only one sequence from this topic, remember:
