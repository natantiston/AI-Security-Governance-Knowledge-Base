# 06.19 — ISO/IEC 23894 and AI Risk Management

## Part 1 — Introduction, Purpose, Scope, and Core Concepts

### 1. Introduction to ISO/IEC 23894

**ISO/IEC 23894** is an international guidance standard focused specifically on **risk management for artificial intelligence**.

Its purpose is to help organizations:

* Identify AI-related risks.
* Analyze and evaluate those risks.
* Treat risks appropriately.
* Integrate AI risk management into organizational processes.
* Manage risks throughout the AI system lifecycle.

The important GRC concept is:

> **ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance for applying risk-management principles specifically to AI systems and their unique characteristics.**

It should therefore be viewed as an **AI risk-management guidance standard**, rather than simply another cybersecurity control framework.

---

# 2. Why ISO/IEC 23894 Matters

Traditional enterprise risk management can be applied to AI, but AI introduces characteristics that require additional consideration.

Examples include:

* Data dependency.
* Model behavior.
* Statistical uncertainty.
* Lack of explainability.
* Model drift.
* Bias.
* Emergent behavior.
* Automation.
* Human-AI interaction.
* Third-party models.
* Changing AI capabilities.

Therefore:

```text
Traditional Risk Management
          ↓
       AI Context
          ↓
AI-Specific Risk Considerations
```

ISO/IEC 23894 helps organizations incorporate these considerations into their risk-management practices.

---

# 3. ISO/IEC 23894 in the AI Governance Landscape

ISO/IEC 23894 should not be confused with other AI standards.

A simplified view is:

| Standard      | Primary Focus                   |
| ------------- | ------------------------------- |
| ISO/IEC 23894 | AI risk management              |
| ISO/IEC 42001 | AI management system            |
| ISO/IEC 22989 | AI concepts and terminology     |
| ISO/IEC 23053 | AI system framework using ML    |
| ISO/IEC 27001 | Information security management |
| ISO/IEC 27701 | Privacy information management  |

The standards can complement one another.

For example:

```text
ISO/IEC 42001
AI Management System
        ↓
ISO/IEC 23894
AI Risk Management
        ↓
ISO/IEC 27001
Information Security
        ↓
ISO/IEC 27701
Privacy
```

The exact applicability depends on the organization's objectives and governance structure.

---

# 4. ISO/IEC 23894 Is Risk-Centered

The central question is:

> **What could go wrong because of the AI system, and how should the organization manage that risk?**

This means the standard is not simply about:

* Building AI.
* Testing AI.
* Securing infrastructure.
* Creating policies.

It is fundamentally concerned with:

> **Understanding and managing risks associated with AI.**

---

AI risk management should not exist as an isolated activity performed once before deployment.

It should be integrated into organizational risk management.

Conceptually:

```text

          ↓

          ↓
Continuous Monitoring
```

This creates a connection between AI governance and broader enterprise governance.

# 7. Establishing Context

Before identifying risks, the organization needs to understand the environment in which the AI system operates.

Consider:

* Business objective.
* AI purpose.
* Users.
* Stakeholders.
* Data.
* Technology.
* Operating environment.
* Legal obligations.
* Regulatory environment.
* Organizational risk appetite.

Example:

An AI model used internally to summarize documents has a different risk profile from an AI system making decisions affecting customers.

---

# 8. Understanding the AI System

Risk assessment requires understanding what the AI actually does.

Questions include:

> What is the AI system's purpose?

> What data does it use?

> What outputs does it generate?

> Who consumes those outputs?

> Does it make recommendations or decisions?

> Can it take autonomous actions?

> What systems does it interact with?

> What happens if the output is wrong?

These questions establish the risk context.

# 10. Planning Risks

During planning, consider:

* Is AI actually appropriate?
* Is the business objective clear?
* Are risks understood?
* Are stakeholders identified?
* Are regulatory obligations known?
* Is there sufficient governance?

A fundamental GRC question is:

> **Should this AI use case exist at all?**

Risk management should support that decision before significant investment is made.

# 11. Data Risks

AI systems frequently depend heavily on data.

Potential risks include:

* Poor data quality.
* Incomplete data.
* Inaccurate data.
* Unauthorized data.
* Biased data.
* Excessive data.
* Inappropriate data sources.
* Data leakage.

Therefore:

> **Data risk can become AI risk.**

# 12. Development Risks

During development, risks may arise from:

* Poor model design.
* Inadequate testing.
* Weak security.
* Poor documentation.
* Inappropriate assumptions.
* Insufficient validation.
* Inadequate training data.

GRC should ensure that appropriate governance requirements are embedded into the development lifecycle.

# 13. Deployment Risks

Before deployment, consider:

* Intended users.

* Integration points.

# 20. Risk Identification

Once the context is established, identify potential risk events.

A useful structure remains:

> **Cause → Event → Consequence**

Example:

> Inadequate training data may cause the model to generate systematically inaccurate outputs, resulting in poor business decisions and customer impact.

This is stronger than:

> "AI accuracy risk."

---

# 21. Risk Analysis

After identification, analyze:

* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Existing controls.
* Vulnerabilities.
* Exposure.
* Uncertainty.
* Dependencies.

The organization can then determine the level of risk.

---

# 22. Risk Evaluation

Risk evaluation compares the analyzed risk against defined criteria.

For example:

```text
Risk Level
    ↓
Risk Appetite / Criteria
    ↓
Acceptable?
  ↙       ↘
YES       NO
 ↓         ↓
Monitor   Treat /
          Escalate
```

This connects AI risk assessment to management decisions.

---

# 23. Risk Treatment

Treatment can include:

* Avoiding the AI use case.
* Reducing likelihood.
* Reducing impact.
* Sharing/transferring risk.
* Accepting risk where authorized.

The selected treatment should be proportionate to the risk.

---

# 24. Risk Treatment Example

Suppose an AI system can automatically approve customer requests.

Risk:

> Incorrect AI decisions may cause customer harm.

Possible treatment:

```text
Full Automation
       ↓
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Final Decision
```

Reducing autonomy can reduce the potential impact of erroneous AI output.

* What treatment is planned?
* What decision is required?

The communication should be appropriate for the audience.

# 28. Uncertainty in AI Risk

AI introduces a significant consideration:

> **Uncertainty.**

The organization may not be able to predict every AI behavior.

This does not mean risk management stops.

Instead:

```text
Known Risk
    +
Uncertainty
    ↓
Precaution
+
Monitoring
+

```

# 29. AI Risk and Human Oversight

# 30. AI Risk and Accountability

AI should not create an accountability gap.

Bad governance:

```text
AI made the decision
       ↓
Nobody owns the outcome
```

Better governance:

```text
AI produces output
       ↓
Responsible owner
       ↓
Defined accountability
       ↓
Decision / action
```

AI automation does not eliminate organizational responsibility.

# 32. AI Risk and Explainability

Some AI systems may be difficult to explain.

GRC should therefore ask:

> **Is the level of explainability appropriate for the risk and intended use?**

A highly consequential use case may require stronger explainability and documentation than a low-risk internal productivity tool.

# 44. ISO/IEC 23894 Is Guidance

One important interview point:

> **ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance; it is not itself a certification scheme for an AI management system.**

This is different from how organizations may approach certifiable management-system standards.

Do not describe ISO/IEC 23894 as:

> "The ISO certification for AI risk."

A better description is:

> **"ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance for managing risks related to AI systems."**

---

# 45. Relationship With ISO/IEC 42001

A particularly important distinction is:

### ISO/IEC 42001

Focuses on establishing an **Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS)**.

### ISO/IEC 23894

Focuses on **AI risk management guidance**.

Conceptually:

```text
ISO/IEC 42001
AI Management System
       ↓
Governance + Processes + Controls
       ↓
ISO/IEC 23894
AI Risk Management Guidance
       ↓
Identify + Analyze + Evaluate + Treat
```

They can therefore be used together.

# 50. Senior GRC Perspective

The most important mindset is:

> **Do not start with the framework. Start with the AI use case and its risk.**

Then determine:

```text
What are we doing?
       ↓
What could go wrong?
       ↓
What requirements apply?
       ↓
What controls do we need?
       ↓
Is residual risk acceptable?
       ↓
How do we monitor it?
```

Frameworks should support this process rather than become the objective themselves.

# 51. Practical Example

### Use Case

AI recruitment screening.

### Potential Risk

The AI may produce systematically unfair candidate rankings.

### Context

* Employment decisions.
* Personal information.
* Potentially significant impact on individuals.

### Risk

> Inadequate model validation and biased training data may result in discriminatory candidate recommendations, causing legal, regulatory, reputational and human impact.

### Treatment

* Data-quality assessment.
* Bias testing.
* Model validation.
* Human oversight.
* Documentation.
* Monitoring.

### Governance Question

> Is the residual risk acceptable for this use case?

That is the GRC decision.

# 52. Practical Example — Internal AI Assistant

### Use Case

Employees use an internal AI assistant to summarize company documents.

Potential risks:

* Confidential information exposure.
* Incorrect summaries.
* Unauthorized access.
* Data retention.

Because the AI is internal and does not directly make high-impact decisions, the risk profile may differ from an automated employment or financial decision system.

This demonstrates an important principle:

> **AI risk management should be proportionate to the context and consequences of the use case.**

---

# 53. Practical Example — Autonomous AI Agent

Consider an AI agent capable of:

* Accessing systems.
* Sending emails.
* Creating transactions.
* Modifying records.

Its autonomy increases potential impact.

Therefore, GRC should examine:

* Authorization.
* Least privilege.
* Human approval.
* Tool restrictions.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* Fail-safe mechanisms.
* Incident response.

The key risk-management principle is:

> **Greater autonomy can increase the potential risk exposure.**

When assessing an AI system, ask:

> Where and how will it operate?

### Data

> What information does it use?

### Output

> What does it produce?

### Impact

> What happens if it is wrong?

### Autonomy

> What actions can it take?

### Accountability

> Who is responsible?

### Controls

> What safeguards exist?

### Monitoring
