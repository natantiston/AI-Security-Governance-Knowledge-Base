# 06.19 — ISO/IEC 23894 and AI Risk Management

## Part 1 — Introduction, Purpose, Scope, and Core Concepts

### 1. Introduction to ISO/IEC 23894

**ISO/IEC 23894** is an international guidance standard focused specifically on **risk management for artificial intelligence**.

Its purpose is to help organizations:

* Identify AI-related risks.
* Analyze and evaluate those risks.
* Treat risks appropriately.
* Integrate AI risk management into organizational processes.
* Manage risks throughout the AI system lifecycle.

The important GRC concept is:

> **ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance for applying risk-management principles specifically to AI systems and their unique characteristics.**

It should therefore be viewed as an **AI risk-management guidance standard**, rather than simply another cybersecurity control framework.

---

# 2. Why ISO/IEC 23894 Matters

Traditional enterprise risk management can be applied to AI, but AI introduces characteristics that require additional consideration.

Examples include:

* Data dependency.
* Model behavior.
* Statistical uncertainty.
* Lack of explainability.
* Model drift.
* Bias.
* Emergent behavior.
* Automation.
* Human-AI interaction.
* Third-party models.
* Changing AI capabilities.

Therefore:

```text
Traditional Risk Management
          ↓
       AI Context
          ↓
AI-Specific Risk Considerations
```

ISO/IEC 23894 helps organizations incorporate these considerations into their risk-management practices.

---

# 3. ISO/IEC 23894 in the AI Governance Landscape

ISO/IEC 23894 should not be confused with other AI standards.

A simplified view is:

| Standard      | Primary Focus                   |
| ------------- | ------------------------------- |
| ISO/IEC 23894 | AI risk management              |
| ISO/IEC 42001 | AI management system            |
| ISO/IEC 22989 | AI concepts and terminology     |
| ISO/IEC 23053 | AI system framework using ML    |
| ISO/IEC 27001 | Information security management |
| ISO/IEC 27701 | Privacy information management  |

The standards can complement one another.

For example:

```text
ISO/IEC 42001
AI Management System
        ↓
ISO/IEC 23894
AI Risk Management
        ↓
ISO/IEC 27001
Information Security
        ↓
ISO/IEC 27701
Privacy
```

The exact applicability depends on the organization's objectives and governance structure.

---

# 4. ISO/IEC 23894 Is Risk-Centered

The central question is:

> **What could go wrong because of the AI system, and how should the organization manage that risk?**

This means the standard is not simply about:

* Building AI.
* Testing AI.
* Securing infrastructure.
* Creating policies.

It is fundamentally concerned with:

> **Understanding and managing risks associated with AI.**

---

* Operating environment.

* Organizational risk appetite.

# 8. Understanding the AI System

Risk assessment requires understanding what the AI actually does.

Questions include:

> What is the AI system's purpose?

> What data does it use?

> What outputs does it generate?

> Who consumes those outputs?

> Does it make recommendations or decisions?

> Can it take autonomous actions?

> What systems does it interact with?

> What happens if the output is wrong?

These questions establish the risk context.

# 20. Risk Identification

Once the context is established, identify potential risk events.

A useful structure remains:

> **Cause → Event → Consequence**

Example:

> Inadequate training data may cause the model to generate systematically inaccurate outputs, resulting in poor business decisions and customer impact.

This is stronger than:

> "AI accuracy risk."

# 23. Risk Treatment

Treatment can include:

* Avoiding the AI use case.
* Reducing likelihood.
* Reducing impact.
* Sharing/transferring risk.
* Accepting risk where authorized.

The selected treatment should be proportionate to the risk.

---

# 24. Risk Treatment Example

```text
       ↓
Final Decision
```

Reducing autonomy can reduce the potential impact of erroneous AI output.

# 44. ISO/IEC 23894 Is Guidance

One important interview point:

> **ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance; it is not itself a certification scheme for an AI management system.**

This is different from how organizations may approach certifiable management-system standards.

Do not describe ISO/IEC 23894 as:

> "The ISO certification for AI risk."

A better description is:

> **"ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance for managing risks related to AI systems."**

# 58. Intended Use

The intended use describes what the AI system is **supposed** to do.

Example:

> An AI assistant summarizes internal documents for employees.

This is different from:

> An AI system makes employment decisions.

The second use case creates potentially much greater consequences.

Therefore:

> **Risk cannot be assessed properly without understanding intended use.**

---

# 59. Intended vs. Actual Use

An important governance issue is that users may use AI differently from how it was originally designed.

Example:

```text id="r2r7vf"
Approved Use
AI summarizes documents
       ↓
Actual Use
AI recommends customer decisions
```

This is a material governance concern.

The organization should determine whether the new use requires:

* Reassessment.
* Additional controls.
* Approval.
* Restriction.
* New risk treatment.

---

# 60. Misuse and Unintended Use

AI systems may also be used in ways that were never intended.

Examples:

* Employees entering confidential data into public AI tools.
* Using an AI model for high-impact decisions when approved only for productivity.
* Using generated output without human review.
* Connecting an AI agent to systems beyond its approved scope.

Risk assessment should therefore consider reasonably foreseeable misuse.

The organization should use its approved risk methodology rather than inventing a scoring model for every AI system.

---

# 67. Likelihood

Likelihood asks:

> **How likely is the risk event to occur?**

Example:

| Rating | Interpretation |
| ------ | -------------- |
| 1      | Rare           |
| 2      | Unlikely       |
| 3      | Possible       |
| 4      | Likely         |
| 5      | Almost certain |

The actual scale should come from the organization's methodology.

---

# 68. Impact

Impact asks:

> **What happens if the risk occurs?**

Possible dimensions:

* Financial.
* Operational.
* Legal.
* Regulatory.
* Privacy.
* Security.
* Safety.
* Reputation.
* Customer impact.

AI risk may require multiple impact dimensions.

---

# 69. Multi-Dimensional AI Impact

Consider an AI system that exposes personal data.

The impact could include:

```text id="8shm9k"
Privacy
   +
Regulatory
   +
Financial
   +
Reputational
   +
Customer Harm
```

Therefore, simply assigning:

> "Impact = 4"

may hide important information.

A mature assessment records the rationale.

---

# 70. Uncertainty

AI risk can involve uncertainty about:

* Model behavior.
* Data quality.
* Future performance.
* Adversarial attacks.
* User behavior.
* Emerging technology.

Uncertainty should be explicitly considered rather than ignored.

---

# 71. Risk Analysis Under Uncertainty

Suppose an organization cannot confidently predict how an AI agent behaves under every condition.

Instead of saying:

> "There is no risk because no incident has happened."

A better approach is:

> **"There is uncertainty regarding behavior under abnormal conditions; therefore, additional testing, monitoring and human oversight are required."**

---

# 72. Existing Controls

Before recommending new controls, identify what already exists.

Examples:

* Access control.
* Encryption.
* Logging.
* DLP.
* Human review.
* Model validation.
* Data-quality checks.
* Vendor assessments.
* Security testing.
* Change management.

This prevents unnecessary duplication.

---

# 73. Control Effectiveness

A control can be:

* Designed but not implemented.
* Implemented but not tested.
* Tested but ineffective.
* Effective.

For example:

```text id="r7k8fk"

     ↓

     ↓

     ↓
Effective?
  ↙       ↘
YES       NO
 ↓         ↓
```

# 74. Inherent Risk

**Inherent risk** represents the level of risk before considering relevant controls.

Example:

> AI-generated incorrect financial advice.

Suppose:

* Likelihood = 4.
* Impact = 5.

Using a simple 5×5 methodology:

> 4 × 5 = 20

The organization's risk matrix may classify this as Critical.

But the actual rating must follow the approved methodology.

---

# 75. Residual Risk

Residual risk is the remaining exposure after considering controls and treatment.

Example:

```text id="qg5p5s"
Inherent Risk
Critical
     ↓
Controls
     ↓
Residual Risk
Moderate
```

This is the risk management decision point.

---

# 76. Risk Evaluation

Risk evaluation asks:

> **Is the analyzed risk acceptable under the organization's criteria?**

Compare:

```text id="1m8g3b"
Residual Risk
      ↓
Risk Appetite / Criteria
      ↓
Acceptable?
```

If yes:

> Accept and monitor.

If no:

> Treat or escalate.

---

# 77. Risk Appetite

Risk appetite represents the level and type of risk the organization is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives.

Example:

> The organization has low tolerance for unauthorized disclosure of customer information.

Therefore, even a moderate privacy risk may require stronger treatment.

---

# 78. Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance is often more specific.

Example:

> No critical customer-facing AI system may operate without validated access controls.

This creates a measurable boundary.

---

# 79. Risk Appetite vs. Risk Tolerance

Think of it as:

```text id="h3m3mz"

        ↓

        ↓
Risk Decision
Accept / Treat / Escalate
```

The exact terminology can vary between organizations.

# 80. Risk Treatment

If risk is unacceptable, treatment is required.

Common strategies:

### Avoid

Do not perform the risky activity.

### Reduce

Implement controls to lower likelihood or impact.

### Share/Transfer

Allocate some risk through contractual or insurance mechanisms where appropriate.

### Accept

Retain the risk through an authorized decision.

---

# 81. Avoidance

Example:

> An AI system would create unacceptable regulatory exposure.

The organization decides:

> Do not deploy the system.

This is legitimate risk treatment.

---

# 82. Reduction

Example:

```text id="q0r2rc"

        ↓
AI Recommendation
        ↓
Human Review
        ↓

```

Reducing AI autonomy may reduce potential impact.

# 83. Risk Sharing

* Contractual requirements.

* Security obligations.
* Indemnification.
* Insurance.
* Vendor governance.

However:

> **Transferring contractual responsibility does not necessarily eliminate the organization's underlying risk.**

# 84. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance means the organization knowingly retains the risk.

It should involve:

* Identified risk.
* Residual exposure.
* Business rationale.
* Appropriate authority.
* Defined conditions.
* Review requirements.

Risk acceptance should never mean:

> "We don't know what to do, so we'll accept it."

# 86. Treatment Priority

Treatment should consider:

* Risk severity.
* Risk appetite.
* Business criticality.
* Regulatory obligations.
* Potential harm.
* Treatment effectiveness.
* Cost.
* Time.

High risk does not automatically mean:

> "Implement every possible control."

The treatment should be proportionate.

### Option B

# 89. Risk Treatment Does Not Always Mean More Controls

Architecture and process changes can sometimes be more effective than adding another security tool.

# 90. Human Oversight as Risk Treatment

Human oversight can reduce risk where:

* AI decisions are consequential.
* Model uncertainty is significant.
* Errors could cause material harm.
* Users need an override capability.

But human review must itself be designed properly.

Simply saying:

> "A human is involved"

does not automatically make the control effective.

---

# 91. Meaningful Human Oversight

Effective oversight may require:

* Appropriate authority.
* Relevant competence.
* Sufficient information.
* Ability to challenge the AI.
* Ability to override.
* Adequate time.
* Clear escalation procedures.

A human who blindly approves AI recommendations provides weak control.

Monitor:

* Control performance.
* Incidents.
* Risk indicators.
* AI performance.
* User behavior.
* Exceptions.

Then reassess residual risk.

# 06.19 — ISO/IEC 23894 and AI Risk Management

## Part 3 — AI Risk Treatment, Controls, Lifecycle Integration, and Governance

Part 1 established **what ISO/IEC 23894 is and where it fits**.

Part 2 covered the **risk-management process**.

Part 3 moves into the practical GRC question:

> **How do we turn an identified AI risk into an effective governance and control response?**

The core sequence is:

**RISK → CONTROL → TREATMENT → VALIDATION → RESIDUAL RISK → DECISION → MONITORING**

---

# 123. Risk Treatment Is More Than Adding Controls

A common GRC mistake is to assume:

> "There is a risk, therefore we need another control."

That is not always the best response.

Risk treatment can involve:

* Changing the AI use case.
* Reducing AI functionality.
* Reducing AI autonomy.
* Changing the architecture.
* Restricting data.
* Introducing human oversight.
* Selecting another provider.
* Improving controls.
* Avoiding the activity.
* Accepting the residual risk.

The objective is:

> **Bring risk to an acceptable level, not maximize the number of controls.**

---

# 124. Start With the Risk

Suppose the risk is:

> The AI model may generate inaccurate recommendations that employees rely on, resulting in incorrect business decisions.

Do not immediately prescribe:

> "Add human review."

First understand:

* Why can the model be wrong?
* How often could it happen?
* What is the consequence?
* Who is affected?
* Can the output be validated?
* How much autonomy does the AI have?
* What controls already exist?

Then determine the appropriate treatment.

---

# 125. Treatment Options

A practical treatment decision can be represented as:

```text
                 AI RISK
                    ↓
          Can the use case be avoided?
                ↙       ↘
              YES       NO
               ↓         ↓
            AVOID     Can risk be reduced?
                         ↓
                    Controls /
                    Redesign
                         ↓
                  Can risk be shared?
                    ↙        ↘
                  YES        NO
                   ↓          ↓
                 SHARE    ACCEPT /
                           ESCALATE
```

The organization's formal risk methodology should determine the available treatment categories.

---

# 126. Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance means changing or stopping the activity that creates unacceptable exposure.

Example:

> An organization considers deploying an AI system for a highly consequential decision but determines that the risk cannot be sufficiently controlled.

Possible decision:

> Do not deploy the AI system for that purpose.

This is a valid risk-management outcome.

---

# 127. Risk Reduction Through Redesign

Instead of:

> AI automatically makes the decision.

Change the design to:

> AI provides a recommendation and a qualified employee makes the final decision.

This can reduce:

* Automation risk.
* Incorrect decision risk.
* Accountability risk.
* Potential impact.

The key lesson:

> **Architecture itself can be a risk control.**

---

# 128. Reducing AI Autonomy

Autonomy is an important AI risk consideration.

Compare:

### Level 1

AI generates information.

### Level 2

AI recommends an action.

### Level 3

AI initiates an action.

### Level 4

AI independently executes an action.

As autonomy increases, potential consequences may increase.

A GRC professional should therefore ask:

> **"What is the minimum level of autonomy required to achieve the business objective?"**

---

# 129. Least Privilege for AI

The principle of least privilege applies to AI agents as well.

An AI agent should have only the access required to perform its approved function.

Example:

```text
AI Agent
   ↓
Needs customer lookup
   ↓
READ access
```

Do not automatically provide:

```text
READ + WRITE + DELETE + ADMIN
```

if those privileges are unnecessary.

* Databases.
* CRM.
* Ticketing.
* Payment systems.
* File repositories.
* APIs.

Therefore:

> **Tool access should be explicitly governed.**

# 132. Human-in-the-Loop

Human involvement can be an important control.

But there is a difference between:

### Human-in-the-loop

Human actively reviews or approves an AI action.

### Human-on-the-loop

Human supervises the system and can intervene.

### Human-out-of-the-loop

AI operates without meaningful human intervention.

The appropriate model depends on the risk and use case.

---

# 133. Human Oversight Must Be Effective

A weak control:

> "Employee reviews AI output."

Questions should follow:

* Is the employee qualified?
* Do they have enough information?
* Can they challenge the output?
* Can they reject it?
* Can they override the AI?
* Is there sufficient time?
* Is the review actually performed?

Otherwise:

> Human oversight may exist on paper but not in practice.

# 143. Control Effectiveness

The organization should distinguish between:

### Design Effectiveness

> Is the control appropriately designed to address the risk?

### Operating Effectiveness

> Does the control actually operate as intended?

Example:

A policy requiring human approval may be well designed.

But if employees routinely approve AI recommendations without reviewing them:

> Operating effectiveness is weak.

# 145. Control Failure

* Was it a design problem?
* Was it an operating problem?

# 178. Avoiding Control Overload

More controls do not automatically mean less risk.

* Operational burden.
* Control conflicts.
* Poor adoption.
* False assurance.

# 182. Interview Master Answer

If asked:

> **"How would you treat an AI risk under ISO/IEC 23894?"**

Use:

> **"I would first understand the risk and determine whether the exposure can be avoided or reduced through changes to the AI use case, architecture or level of autonomy. If controls are required, I would select proportionate preventive, detective and corrective controls and clearly map them to the risk. I would assign control ownership, define evidence and validate both design and operating effectiveness.**
>
> **After implementation, I would reassess the residual risk against the organization's risk appetite. If it is within appetite, I would obtain or document the appropriate risk decision and establish monitoring. If it remains above appetite, I would recommend further treatment, redesign, scope reduction or escalation to the authorized decision-maker. I would also establish reassessment triggers for incidents, model changes, data changes, vendor changes and other material changes."**
