# 06.14 — Privacy and Environmental Impact Assessment

## Part 1 — Foundations, Purpose, Scope, and Core Concepts

### 01. Introduction

AI systems can create impacts that extend beyond cybersecurity and business operations.

An AI system may:

* Process personal information.
* Influence how individuals are treated.
* Collect or infer sensitive information.
* Transfer information across jurisdictions.
* Consume significant computational resources.
* Require substantial energy and infrastructure.
* Generate environmental consequences throughout its lifecycle.

Therefore, an AI risk assessment should consider both **privacy impact** and **environmental impact** where relevant.

The fundamental principle is:

> **An AI system should be assessed not only for what it can do, but also for what information it processes, how individuals may be affected, and what environmental resources its operation requires.**

---

# 02. Purpose of Privacy and Environmental Impact Assessment

The purpose is to identify, understand, evaluate, and appropriately address potential:

### Privacy impacts

* Unauthorized collection.
* Excessive processing.
* Inappropriate use.
* Disclosure.
* Re-identification.
* Inference.
* Retention.
* Cross-border transfer.
* Loss of individual control.

### Environmental impacts

* Energy consumption.
* Computing resource consumption.
* Carbon emissions.
* Water consumption.
* Hardware requirements.
* Electronic waste.
* Infrastructure lifecycle impacts.

The assessment should provide decision-makers with sufficient information to determine whether the AI system can be deployed responsibly.

---

# 03. Why Privacy Is Different for AI

Traditional information systems generally process data according to defined rules.

AI can introduce additional complexity because systems may:

* Infer information that was not explicitly provided.
* Combine information from multiple sources.
* Generate predictions about individuals.
* Process unstructured information.
* Learn statistical relationships.
* Produce outputs that reveal sensitive characteristics.

For example:

```text
Known Data
   ↓
AI Processing
   ↓
Statistical Relationship
   ↓
Inference
   ↓
Potentially Sensitive Conclusion
```

The resulting privacy concern may therefore arise from the **inference**, not merely from the original dataset.

---

# 04. Why Environmental Impact Is Relevant to AI

AI systems can require substantial computational infrastructure.

Environmental considerations may arise from:

* Model training.
* Fine-tuning.
* Inference.
* Data storage.
* Networking.
* Cooling.
* Hardware manufacturing.
* Hardware replacement.
* Data-center operations.

The assessment should therefore consider the AI lifecycle rather than only the software itself.

---

# 05. AI Lifecycle Perspective

A useful conceptual model is:

```text
Design
  ↓
Development
  ↓
Training
  ↓
Testing
  ↓
Deployment
  ↓
Inference / Operation
  ↓
Maintenance
  ↓
Retirement
```

Privacy and environmental impacts may occur at every stage.

---

# 06. Privacy Impact Assessment

A Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA) is a structured process for identifying and addressing privacy risks associated with the processing of personal information.

Depending on the jurisdiction and circumstances, organizations may also use a **Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)** or equivalent assessment.

The terminology and legal requirements depend on the applicable regulatory environment.

---

# 07. Core Privacy Question

The fundamental question is:

> **What happens to individuals because of the way the AI system collects, uses, analyzes, stores, shares, or generates information about them?**

This shifts the assessment from simply asking:

> "Is the database secure?"

to:

> "How does the AI processing affect individuals' privacy?"

---

# 08. Privacy Is More Than Confidentiality

Privacy and security overlap, but they are not identical.

### Security asks:

> How do we protect information?

### Privacy asks:

> Should the information be collected or processed in this way, and what rights and interests of individuals are affected?

For example:

```text
Strong Encryption
       ↓
Information is well protected
```

But:

```text
Information was collected
without an appropriate purpose
       ↓
Privacy concern remains
```

Strong security does not automatically mean compliant or responsible processing.

---

# 09. Privacy Impact Dimensions

A privacy assessment can examine:

* Collection.
* Purpose.
* Necessity.
* Proportionality.
* Data minimization.
* Accuracy.
* Transparency.
* Access.
* Retention.
* Sharing.
* International transfers.
* Individual rights.
* Security.
* Accountability.

---

# 10. Data Collection

Determine:

* What information is collected?
* Why is it collected?
* Who collects it?
* From whom?
* When is it collected?
* Is collection necessary?
* Is the amount of information proportionate?

The principle is:

> **Do not collect more information than is reasonably necessary for the defined purpose.**

---

# 11. AI Data Sources

AI systems may receive information from multiple sources:

```text
Customer Data
      +
Employee Data
      +
Public Data
      +
Operational Data
      +
Third-Party Data
      ↓
AI System
```

The combination itself may create additional privacy considerations.

---

# 12. Data Aggregation

Information that appears harmless individually may become sensitive when combined.

Example:

```text
Age
+
Location
+
Job
+
Transaction History
+
Online Activity
       ↓
Combined Profile
```

The resulting profile may reveal considerably more about an individual than any single data element.

---

# 13. Data Inference

AI can generate inferred information.

For example:

```text
Behavioral Data
      ↓
AI Analysis
      ↓
Predicted Preference
      ↓
Individual Profile
```

The individual may never have explicitly provided the inferred characteristic.

This creates an important privacy consideration.

---

# 14. Sensitive Inference

Potentially sensitive inferences may concern:

* Health.
* Financial circumstances.
* Personal preferences.
* Behavioral characteristics.
* Identity-related characteristics.
* Location patterns.
* Other sensitive attributes.

The assessment should determine whether the AI is capable of generating such inferences.

---

# 15. Purpose Specification

The organization should define:

> **What is the AI processing personal information for?**

Examples:

* Customer support.
* Fraud prevention.
* Service personalization.
* Document classification.
* Internal productivity.
* Security monitoring.

The purpose should be sufficiently clear to evaluate whether the processing is appropriate.

---

# 16. Purpose Limitation

Information collected for one purpose should not automatically be reused for an unrelated purpose.

Example:

```text
Customer Support Data
       ↓
Original Purpose
Customer service
```

Later:

```text
Customer Support Data
       ↓
Unrelated AI profiling
```

The second use may require separate privacy analysis.

---

# 17. Data Minimization

Ask:

> **Does the AI actually need all of this information?**

For example, if an AI assistant only needs:

* Customer issue.
* Product type.
* Support history.

it may not need:

* Full payment information.
* Complete identity records.
* Unrelated personal information.

Reducing unnecessary data reduces privacy exposure.

---

# 18. Necessity

The assessment should distinguish between:

> **Useful data**

and

> **Necessary data**

An AI model may benefit from more information statistically, but that does not automatically mean the organization should process it.

---

# 19. Proportionality

Consider whether the benefits of processing are proportionate to the privacy impact.

Conceptually:

```text
Business Benefit
       ↕
Privacy Intrusion
```

A highly intrusive processing activity requires stronger justification and safeguards.

---

# 20. Transparency

Individuals should generally be able to understand relevant aspects of how their information is processed, subject to applicable law.

The assessment should consider:

* What information is provided to individuals.
* When information is provided.
* Whether AI processing is disclosed where appropriate.
* Whether explanations are understandable.
* Whether privacy notices remain accurate.

---

# 21. AI Transparency Challenges

AI can complicate transparency because individuals may not know:

* That AI is being used.
* What data is being processed.
* How their information influences outputs.
* Whether their information is used for training.
* Who receives the information.

Therefore:

> **AI transparency should be considered as part of the privacy assessment.**

---

# 22. Individual Control

The assessment should consider whether individuals have appropriate mechanisms relating to their information.

Depending on applicable law, these may include rights concerning:

* Access.
* Correction.
* Deletion.
* Restriction.
* Objection.
* Data portability.
* Other legally applicable rights.

The exact rights depend on the jurisdiction and processing context.

---

# 23. Data Accuracy

Incorrect personal information can produce incorrect AI outputs.

Example:

```text
Incorrect Personal Data
       ↓
AI Processing
       ↓
Incorrect Profile
       ↓
Incorrect Recommendation
       ↓
Individual Impact
```

Therefore, data quality can become a privacy and AI governance issue.

---

# 24. Data Retention

The assessment should determine:

* How long information is retained.
* Why it is retained.
* Where it is retained.
* Who controls retention.
* How deletion occurs.
* Whether backups retain information.
* Whether AI systems or providers retain prompts and outputs.

---

# 25. AI-Specific Retention Questions

Ask:

> Are prompts stored?

> Are outputs stored?

> Is conversation history retained?

> Is data used for model improvement?

> Are logs retained?

> Can data be deleted from downstream systems?

These questions should be answered before deployment where relevant.

---

# 26. Third-Party AI Providers

External AI providers create additional privacy considerations.

The assessment should examine:

* Provider role.
* Data processing responsibilities.
* Data location.
* Data retention.
* Subprocessors.
* Security controls.
* Contractual obligations.
* International transfers.
* Data-use restrictions.

---

# 27. Data Processing Chain

An AI service may involve:

```text
Organization
     ↓
AI Application
     ↓
AI Provider
     ↓
Cloud Infrastructure
     ↓
Subprocessor
```

Privacy assessment should consider the relevant processing chain rather than stopping at the primary vendor.

---

# 28. Cross-Border Data Transfer

AI services may process information in multiple countries.

The assessment should identify:

* Where data originates.
* Where it is transmitted.
* Where it is processed.
* Where it is stored.
* Which providers have access.

Cross-border processing may introduce additional legal and governance requirements.

---

# 29. Privacy by Design

Privacy should be incorporated into the system from the beginning.

Rather than:

```text
Build AI
   ↓
Deploy AI
   ↓
Discover Privacy Issues
```

prefer:

```text
Business Requirement
       ↓
Privacy Requirements
       ↓
Architecture
       ↓
Implementation
       ↓
Testing
       ↓
Deployment
```

Privacy becomes an engineering and governance requirement rather than a late-stage compliance exercise.

---

# 30. Privacy Controls

Potential controls include:

* Data minimization.
* Access controls.
* Encryption.
* Pseudonymization.
* Anonymization where appropriate.
* Retention controls.
* Data-loss prevention.
* Privacy notices.
* Consent mechanisms where legally applicable.
* Data-subject request processes.
* Vendor controls.
* Audit logging.

The appropriate control depends on the identified privacy risk.

---

# 31. Pseudonymization

Pseudonymization replaces direct identifiers with another identifier.

Conceptually:

```text
Jonathan Morales
       ↓
Customer ID 78452
```

The information may still be considered personal data under applicable privacy laws if re-identification remains possible.

Therefore:

> **Pseudonymization reduces exposure but does not necessarily eliminate privacy obligations.**

---

# 32. Anonymization

Anonymization aims to prevent information from being linked back to an identifiable individual in a sufficiently robust manner.

The assessment should not assume that simply removing names makes data anonymous.

For AI systems, re-identification risk should be carefully considered.

---

# 33. Privacy Threats to Consider

Potential AI privacy threats include:

* Unauthorized data access.
* Prompt leakage.
* Sensitive information appearing in outputs.
* Membership inference.
* Model inversion.
* Re-identification.
* Excessive retention.
* Unauthorized secondary use.
* Data poisoning involving personal information.
* Inappropriate profiling.

These threats should be assessed according to the actual architecture and use case.

---

# 34. Environmental Impact Assessment

Environmental assessment considers the ecological consequences associated with the AI system.

Potential dimensions include:

* Energy use.
* Carbon emissions.
* Water use.
* Hardware.
* Data-center infrastructure.
* Resource consumption.
* Electronic waste.

---

# 35. Energy Consumption

AI workloads require computing resources.

Energy consumption can occur during:

### Training

Large-scale computation used to develop or adapt models.

### Inference

Computational resources used whenever the model generates an output.

### Supporting infrastructure

Storage, networking, cooling, and other infrastructure also consume resources.

---

# 36. Training Versus Inference

Conceptually:

```text
Training
High computational workload
       ↓
Model creation / adaptation

Inference
Repeated computational workload
       ↓
Ongoing AI operation
```

A model with relatively modest training requirements can still create substantial operational resource consumption if it is used at very large scale.

---

# 37. AI Usage Volume

Environmental impact can be influenced by:

* Number of users.
* Number of requests.
* Request complexity.
* Model size.
* Context size.
* Frequency of inference.
* Processing duration.
* Infrastructure efficiency.

Therefore, environmental assessment should consider actual usage patterns.

---

# 38. Model Selection

A larger model is not automatically the most appropriate model.

Organizations may evaluate:

```text
Business Requirement
       ↓
Required AI Capability
       ↓
Appropriate Model
       ↓
Computational Requirement
```

Using a model substantially larger than necessary may increase resource consumption without proportionate business benefit.

---

# 39. AI Efficiency

Potential efficiency approaches include:

* Appropriate model selection.
* Efficient inference.
* Caching.
* Request optimization.
* Batch processing where appropriate.
* Reducing unnecessary computation.
* Hardware optimization.
* Efficient infrastructure.

The goal is not simply to minimize computing.

The goal is to achieve the required business outcome efficiently.

---

# 40. Data-Center Impact

AI infrastructure may require:

* Servers.
* GPUs or other accelerators.
* Storage.
* Networking.
* Cooling.
* Power infrastructure.

Environmental impact can therefore extend beyond the model itself.

---

# 41. Water Consumption

Data-center operations may involve water consumption, particularly through cooling systems.

The relevance and magnitude depend on:

* Data-center design.
* Cooling technology.
* Geographic location.
* Climate.
* Workload.
* Infrastructure efficiency.

Where material, this can be considered within environmental impact assessment.

---

# 42. Hardware Lifecycle

AI requires physical infrastructure.

Consider:

```text
Manufacturing
     ↓
Transportation
     ↓
Deployment
     ↓
Operation
     ↓
Maintenance
     ↓
Replacement
     ↓
End-of-Life
```

Environmental impact can occur throughout this lifecycle.

---

# 43. Electronic Waste

Rapid hardware evolution may lead to replacement of:

* GPUs.
* Servers.
* Networking equipment.
* Storage systems.

The organization should consider appropriate asset lifecycle and disposal practices.

---

# 44. Renewable Energy Considerations

Where environmental reporting is relevant, organizations may consider:

* Energy source.
* Renewable-energy procurement.
* Data-center energy efficiency.
* Provider environmental reporting.
* Emissions factors.

The assessment should rely on credible provider or organizational data rather than unsupported assumptions.

---

# 45. Environmental Data Quality

Environmental metrics can be difficult to calculate precisely.

Potential data sources include:

* Cloud provider sustainability information.
* Data-center metrics.
* Internal energy measurements.
* Hardware specifications.
* AI workload measurements.
* Organizational sustainability reporting.

Where precise information is unavailable, assumptions should be clearly documented.

---

# 46. Privacy and Environmental Impact Are Different Dimensions

They should not be combined into one generic score without justification.

For example:

```text
Privacy Impact       = High
Environmental Impact = Moderate
```

A system may have significant privacy implications but relatively low environmental impact.

Another may have low privacy exposure but substantial computational requirements.

---

A broader assessment can therefore be structured as:

```text
 Transparency                Hardware
 Retention                   E-Waste
       ↓                           ↓
       └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                     ↓
             Overall AI Assessment
```

The dimensions remain analytically distinct.

### Core GRC Anchor

> **"Privacy and environmental impact assessment expands AI governance beyond traditional cybersecurity. For privacy, I examine what personal information the AI collects, processes, infers, stores, and shares, why that processing is necessary, how individuals may be affected, and whether appropriate controls exist around minimization, transparency, retention, access, rights, security, and third-party processing. For environmental impact, I consider the AI lifecycle, including training, inference, infrastructure, energy consumption, water where material, hardware requirements, and end-of-life considerations. I keep privacy and environmental impacts analytically distinct, use evidence wherever possible, document assumptions where data is uncertain, and use the results to support proportionate and accountable AI governance decisions."**

## Part 2 — Privacy Assessment Methodology and AI-Specific Privacy Risks

### 52. Establishing the Privacy Assessment Scope

A privacy assessment should begin by defining exactly what processing activity is being examined.

The scope should identify:

* AI system.
* Business purpose.
* Processing activities.
* Categories of personal data.
* Categories of individuals.
* Data sources.
* Data recipients.
* AI providers.
* Storage locations.
* Processing locations.
* Retention periods.
* Connected systems.
* Applicable organizational requirements.

A useful starting structure is:

```text id="8n7v3c"
AI USE CASE
    ↓
PROCESSING ACTIVITY
    ↓
PERSONAL DATA
    ↓
DATA SUBJECTS
    ↓
PROCESSING PURPOSE
    ↓
RECIPIENTS / PROVIDERS
    ↓
LOCATIONS
    ↓
RETENTION
```

The assessment should be specific enough that another reviewer can understand exactly what processing is being evaluated.

---

# 53. Identify the Data Subjects

Determine who may be affected.

Potential categories include:

* Customers.
* Employees.
* Applicants.
* Suppliers.
* Contractors.
* Partners.
* Website visitors.
* Members of the public.

Different groups may have different expectations, vulnerabilities, and applicable protections.

---

# 54. Identify the Categories of Personal Data

Do not simply record:

> "Personal data."

Break the information down.

Examples:

### Identity information

* Name.
* Identification number.
* Account identifier.

### Contact information

* Email.
* Telephone number.
* Address.

### Transaction information

* Purchases.
* Payments.
* Service history.

### Behavioral information

* Usage patterns.
* Search activity.
* Interaction history.

### Technical information

* IP address.
* Device information.
* System logs.

### Potentially sensitive information

Depending on the jurisdiction and context:

* Health-related information.
* Biometric information.
* Financial information.
* Precise location.
* Other specially protected categories.

---

# 55. Data Classification

Map privacy information to the organization's data-classification scheme.

For example:

```text id="z7a3qk"
Public
  ↓
Internal
  ↓
Confidential
  ↓
Restricted
```

The exact classification levels should follow organizational policy.

AI systems processing higher-classification information generally require stronger controls.

---

# 56. Identify the Data Source

Ask where the information originates.

Possible sources:

* Individual directly.
* Internal business system.
* Public website.
* Mobile application.
* IoT device.
* Third-party provider.
* Data broker.
* Employee system.
* Customer interaction.

The source affects transparency, provenance, and governance considerations.

---

# 57. Direct Versus Indirect Collection

### Direct collection

The individual provides the information.

```text id="v8q6gx"
Customer
   ↓
Customer Form
   ↓
AI System
```

### Indirect collection

The organization obtains information from another source.

```text id="7u5q0b"
Third Party
   ↓
Organization
   ↓
AI System
```

Indirect collection can create additional transparency and data-quality questions.

---

# 58. Identify the Processing Activity

Describe what the AI actually does with the information.

Examples:

* Classifies.
* Summarizes.
* Predicts.
* Recommends.
* Generates.
* Matches.
* Profiles.
* Detects anomalies.
* Translates.
* Extracts information.

The assessment should avoid vague statements such as:

> "AI processes customer data."

Instead:

> "The AI analyzes customer support conversations to classify the reported issue and recommend an appropriate support response."

---

# 59. Purpose of Processing

Document the specific purpose.

A good purpose statement answers:

> **What business objective requires this processing?**

Example:

> "The system processes customer support conversations to assist agents in identifying issues and generating draft responses."

This creates a baseline against which additional uses can be evaluated.

---

# 60. Secondary Use

Determine whether the information is used for purposes beyond the original objective.

Examples:

```text id="1f0z2f"
Customer Support
      ↓
Original Purpose
      ↓
AI Assistance
```

Potential secondary use:

```text id="p5q8rm"
Customer Support Data
      ↓
Model Training
      ↓
Unrelated AI Development
```

Any secondary use should be evaluated under applicable privacy requirements and organizational policy.

---

# 61. Data Lifecycle

Map the complete information lifecycle:

```text id="6z1p0c"
Collect
  ↓
Transmit
  ↓
Store
  ↓
Process
  ↓
Generate Output
  ↓
Share
  ↓
Retain
  ↓
Delete
```

Privacy risks can occur at any point in this lifecycle.

---

# 62. AI Input Data

Identify everything that can enter the AI system.

Examples:

* User prompts.
* Documents.
* Customer records.
* Emails.
* Tickets.
* Images.
* Audio.
* Video.
* Metadata.
* API payloads.

This is especially important for generative AI systems because users may unintentionally submit information that was not anticipated during design.

---

# 63. AI Output Data

Assess what the AI can produce.

Outputs may contain:

* Personal information.
* Sensitive information.
* Confidential business information.
* Inferred characteristics.
* Information from source documents.
* Information about other individuals.

The assessment must therefore examine **outputs**, not only inputs.

---

# 64. Prompt Privacy Risk

Generative AI introduces a specific concern:

> **Users may voluntarily place personal information into prompts.**

Example:

```text id="4z2x7b"
Employee
   ↓
Copies HR case
   ↓
Pastes into AI tool
   ↓
Personal information enters AI system
```

This may occur even when the original architecture did not intend the system to process such information.

---

# 65. Shadow AI

Employees may use unauthorized AI tools for business activities.

Example:

```text id="0n8t7x"
Employee
   ↓
Business Document
   ↓
Unapproved AI Service
   ↓
External Processing
```

This creates governance and privacy exposure.

A mature privacy program should therefore consider both:

* Approved AI.
* Unapproved AI usage.

---

# 66. AI Data Discovery

Organizations may use discovery mechanisms to identify:

* AI applications.
* AI API usage.
* Data transfers.
* Sensitive prompts.
* AI-related SaaS services.
* Unauthorized AI tools.

The objective is to understand the actual AI processing environment rather than relying only on officially documented systems.

---

# 67. Lawful Basis and Legal Requirements

Where applicable, the privacy assessment should identify the legal basis or other applicable legal justification for processing.

For organizations operating under privacy regimes such as the EU GDPR, possible legal bases can include:

* Consent.
* Contract.
* Legal obligation.
* Vital interests.
* Public task.
* Legitimate interests.

The appropriate basis depends on the specific processing activity.

A GRC professional should not assume that consent is always required.

---

# 68. Consent

Where consent is used, the assessment should consider whether it is:

* Appropriate.
* Specific.
* Informed.
* Freely given.
* Properly documented.
* Withdrawable where applicable.

Consent should not be treated as a universal solution to privacy risk.

---

# 69. Legitimate Interests

Where legitimate interests are relied upon under an applicable framework, the organization may need to evaluate:

```text id="f5u0r7"
Legitimate Interest
       ↓
Necessity
       ↓
Balancing / Impact
       ↓
Safeguards
```

The specific legal test depends on the applicable law.

---

# 70. Necessity and Proportionality Test

A useful GRC approach is:

### Question 1

Is the processing necessary for the stated purpose?

### Question 2

Is there a less intrusive way to achieve the same objective?

### Question 3

Are the privacy impacts proportionate to the expected benefit?

This prevents organizations from treating technical capability as sufficient justification.

---

# 71. Privacy Risk Scenario

Example:

> The AI analyzes employee communications to identify potential insider threats.

Potential benefits:

* Security monitoring.
* Threat detection.

Potential privacy concerns:

* Employee surveillance.
* Excessive monitoring.
* False positives.
* Sensitive information exposure.
* Secondary use.
* Lack of transparency.

The assessment must consider both organizational security objectives and individual privacy interests.

---

# 72. Profiling

AI may create profiles based on observed or inferred characteristics.

Conceptually:

```text id="1y1gqp"
Multiple Data Points
       ↓
AI Analysis
       ↓
Individual Profile
       ↓
Prediction / Recommendation
```

The assessment should determine whether profiling occurs and what consequences may result.

---

# 73. Automated Decision-Making

Some AI systems influence or make decisions affecting individuals.

Examples:

* Candidate screening.
* Fraud detection.
* Customer eligibility.
* Credit decisions.
* Service prioritization.
* Risk scoring.

Where automated decision-making is involved, the privacy and legal analysis may become significantly more important.

---

# 74. Human Review of Automated Decisions

The assessment should determine:

* Is a human involved?
* At what stage?
* Does the human have genuine authority?
* Can the human override the AI?
* Does the human understand the AI output?
* Is the review meaningful?

Simply placing a human at the end of an automated workflow does not automatically eliminate risk.

---

# 75. Meaningful Human Intervention

A meaningful reviewer should generally have:

* Sufficient information.
* Appropriate expertise.
* Authority to challenge the result.
* Adequate time.
* Ability to override the decision.

This creates a stronger governance mechanism.

---

# 76. Privacy Risk From Incorrect AI Output

Incorrect outputs can affect individuals.

Example:

```text id="e4c8xq"
Incorrect Input
     ↓
AI Analysis
     ↓
Incorrect Risk Score
     ↓
Incorrect Customer Treatment
     ↓
```

### Senior GRC Anchor

> **"For the privacy component of an AI impact assessment, I first establish the processing boundary, identify the affected individuals and categories of personal information, understand the source and purpose of the data, and map the complete lifecycle from collection through processing, sharing, retention and deletion. I specifically assess AI-related risks such as inference, profiling, automated decision-making, prompt leakage, model-training use, retrieval authorization, re-identification and third-party processing. I then evaluate necessity, proportionality, transparency, data minimization, individual rights, security controls, retention, vendor controls and cross-border processing where applicable. Finally, I validate the assessment through evidence, document assumptions and uncertainties, determine the remaining privacy exposure, and ensure that appropriate ownership and governance decisions are established."**

## Part 3 — Environmental Impact Assessment, Measurement, Controls, and GRC Integration

### 102. Environmental Impact Assessment Foundations

Environmental impact assessment for AI examines the resources and environmental consequences associated with developing, deploying, operating, maintaining, and retiring AI capabilities.

The central question is:

> **What environmental resources does this AI capability consume, what environmental effects may result, and can the organization achieve the required business outcome more efficiently?**

A GRC professional should avoid treating environmental impact as simply an "energy consumption" question.

It is broader:

```text id="7s2j4m"
AI SYSTEM
   ↓
COMPUTE
   ↓
ENERGY
   ↓
WATER / COOLING
   ↓
INFRASTRUCTURE
   ↓
HARDWARE
   ↓
LIFECYCLE
   ↓
ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACT
```

---

# 103. Why AI Environmental Assessment Matters

AI systems can create environmental impacts through:

* Model training.
* Fine-tuning.
* Inference.
* Data storage.
* Networking.
* Cooling.
* Hardware manufacturing.
* Hardware replacement.
* Electronic waste.

The magnitude varies considerably by:

* Model architecture.
* Workload.
* Hardware.
* Data-center efficiency.
* Utilization.
* Geographic location.
* Energy source.
* Number of users.
* Request frequency.

Therefore, environmental impact should be assessed in context.

---

# 104. AI Lifecycle Environmental Assessment

A lifecycle approach is preferable:

```text id="l1x5c8"
Research
   ↓
Development
   ↓
Training
   ↓
Testing
   ↓
Deployment
   ↓
Inference
   ↓
Maintenance
   ↓
Scaling
   ↓
Retirement
```

Each stage can create different resource requirements.

---

# 105. Training Energy

Training can require significant computational resources.

The assessment may consider:

* Training duration.
* Hardware used.
* Number of accelerators.
* Utilization.
* Number of training runs.
* Retraining frequency.
* Data-center efficiency.

However, avoid making unsupported claims about exact environmental impact when reliable measurements are unavailable.

---

# 106. Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning may require less computational effort than training a large model from scratch, but repeated fine-tuning can accumulate resource consumption.

For example:

```text id="v6p2f1"

    ↓
Fine-Tuning
    ↓
Deployment
    ↓
New Data
    ↓
Fine-Tuning
    ↓
New Version
    ↓
Repeated Cycle
```

The assessment should consider the cumulative lifecycle rather than one isolated training event.

---

# 107. Inference

Inference is the computation required to produce outputs after deployment.

For enterprise AI, inference may become particularly important because it occurs repeatedly.

Conceptually:

```text id="k9z4qs"
1 request
     ↓
1 computation

10,000 requests
     ↓
10,000 computations

Millions of requests
     ↓
Large cumulative workload
```

Therefore, operational scale can materially influence environmental impact.

---

# 108. Model Size

Larger models may require greater computational resources.

However:

> **Model size alone does not determine environmental impact.**

Other factors include:

* Hardware efficiency.
* Number of requests.
* Context length.
* Quantization.
* Utilization.
* Data-center efficiency.
* Model architecture.

A proper assessment considers the complete workload.

---

# 109. Context Size

Generative AI systems may process large amounts of contextual information.

For example:

```text id="f2h8q7"
Short Prompt
     ↓
Lower Processing Requirement

Large Document
     ↓
Larger Context
     ↓
Greater Processing Requirement
```

Repeatedly sending unnecessary information may increase computational demand.

---

# 110. AI Request Optimization

Environmental efficiency can therefore begin at the application layer.

Potential approaches:

* Reduce unnecessary prompts.
* Avoid redundant processing.
* Cache reusable results.
* Limit unnecessary context.
* Use appropriate models.
* Batch workloads where appropriate.
* Avoid repeated computations.

These controls may simultaneously improve:

* Cost.
* Performance.
* Environmental efficiency.

---

# 111. Model Selection as a GRC Decision

The question should not simply be:

> "Which model is most powerful?"

Instead:

> **"Which model provides sufficient capability for the business requirement at an appropriate level of cost, risk, performance, and resource consumption?"**

This is a governance decision.

---

# 112. Model Right-Sizing

```text id="2m4g7x"
       ↓

       ↓

       ↓

```

The objective is proportionality.

# 113. Cloud Infrastructure

# 116. Data-Center Efficiency

One commonly discussed infrastructure metric is **Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE)**.

Conceptually:

> PUE = Total Data Center Energy ÷ IT Equipment Energy

A lower PUE generally indicates more efficient supporting infrastructure.

However, PUE alone does not describe the complete environmental impact of an AI workload.

---

# 117. Why PUE Is Not Enough

Two data centers may have similar PUE values but different:

* Energy sources.
* Water requirements.
* Hardware efficiency.
* Workload characteristics.
* Geographic conditions.

Therefore:

> **A single infrastructure metric should not be treated as a complete environmental assessment.**

# 157. Privacy Screening

Before conducting a detailed privacy assessment, a screening process can determine whether one is necessary.

1. Does the system process personal information?

6. Does it involve vulnerable individuals?
7. Does it transfer data externally?
8. Does it involve cross-border processing?
9. Does it introduce significant new technology or processing?

# 159. Proportionality of Assessment

The assessment itself should be proportional.

Do not spend months conducting a detailed environmental analysis for a small internal AI tool with minimal usage.

Likewise, do not perform a five-question privacy checklist for an AI system processing millions of customer records.

The principle is:

> **Assessment depth should be proportional to potential impact.**

# 183. Interview Scenario — Privacy Assessment

**Interviewer:**

> "How would you conduct a privacy impact assessment for an AI system?"

### Strong answer

> "I would begin by defining the processing activity, business purpose, data subjects, categories of personal information, sources, recipients, processing locations and retention. I would map the complete data lifecycle and identify AI-specific concerns such as inference, profiling, automated decision-making, prompt leakage, model-training use and third-party processing. I would then assess necessity, proportionality, transparency, individual rights, data minimization and security safeguards. Finally, I would validate the relevant controls through evidence, document residual privacy exposure, assign ownership and ensure that the appropriate governance authority makes the deployment decision."

---

# 184. Interview Scenario — Environmental Assessment

**Interviewer:**

> "How would you assess the environmental impact of an AI system?"

### Strong answer

> "I would assess the AI lifecycle, including training, fine-tuning, inference, infrastructure, cooling, energy consumption, hardware utilization and end-of-life. I would consider workload volume, model size, request complexity and infrastructure efficiency rather than assuming that model size alone determines environmental impact. I would use measured or provider-reported data where available and clearly document estimates and assumptions. I would then identify optimization opportunities such as right-sizing the model, reducing unnecessary inference, improving utilization and consolidating duplicate workloads, and report the remaining environmental impact proportionately."

---

# 185. Interview Scenario — Privacy and Security Conflict

**Interviewer:**

> "What if security wants more data but privacy wants less data?"

### Strong answer

> "I would not treat this as simply a conflict between security and privacy teams. I would define the specific security objective, identify the minimum information necessary to achieve it, assess the privacy impact of collecting additional information, and evaluate alternative controls that could achieve the security objective with less privacy exposure. The decision should be based on necessity, proportionality, risk and evidence, with the appropriate governance authority making the final decision."

---

# 186. Interview Scenario — AI Provider

**Interviewer:**

> "The AI provider says it is secure. Is that enough?"

### Strong answer

> "No. I would treat the provider's statement as an input, not as sufficient evidence. I would validate contractual terms, data-processing arrangements, retention, data-use restrictions, subprocessors, processing locations, security controls and relevant assurance evidence. I would also assess the provider's environmental reporting where that is material to the organization's requirements."

---

# 187. Interview Scenario — Missing Environmental Data

**Interviewer:**

> "What if the AI provider cannot tell you the exact environmental impact?"

### Strong answer

> "I would not invent a number. I would document the limitation, identify what provider or workload data is available, use a defensible estimation methodology if appropriate, clearly label assumptions and evaluate whether the uncertainty itself affects the governance decision. For material AI workloads, I would also consider whether contractual reporting requirements or alternative providers should be evaluated."

---

# 188. Interview Scenario — Business Wants Immediate Deployment

**Interviewer:**

> "The business wants to deploy tomorrow. What do you do?"

### Strong answer

> "I would first determine whether the AI system falls within a mandatory assessment or approval requirement and identify any material privacy, security or environmental exposure. I would prioritize the assessment based on risk rather than delaying low-risk use cases unnecessarily. If material issues remain unresolved, I would clearly document the exposure, available compensating controls, business rationale and decision authority. The goal is not to block innovation; it is to ensure that deployment is informed, proportionate and accountable."

Assuming the AI provider owns all responsibility.

Treating environmental impact as only electricity.

Inventing precise environmental numbers.

Treating every AI system equally.

**Correction:**

Doing the assessment once and never revisiting it.
