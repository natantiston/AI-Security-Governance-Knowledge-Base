# 06.14 — Privacy and Environmental Impact Assessment

## Part 1 — Foundations, Purpose, Scope, and Core Concepts

### 01. Introduction

AI systems can create impacts that extend beyond cybersecurity and business operations.

An AI system may:

* Process personal information.
* Influence how individuals are treated.
* Collect or infer sensitive information.
* Transfer information across jurisdictions.
* Consume significant computational resources.
* Require substantial energy and infrastructure.
* Generate environmental consequences throughout its lifecycle.

Therefore, an AI risk assessment should consider both **privacy impact** and **environmental impact** where relevant.

The fundamental principle is:

> **An AI system should be assessed not only for what it can do, but also for what information it processes, how individuals may be affected, and what environmental resources its operation requires.**

---

# 02. Purpose of Privacy and Environmental Impact Assessment

The purpose is to identify, understand, evaluate, and appropriately address potential:

### Privacy impacts

* Unauthorized collection.
* Excessive processing.
* Inappropriate use.
* Disclosure.
* Re-identification.
* Inference.
* Retention.
* Cross-border transfer.
* Loss of individual control.

### Environmental impacts

* Energy consumption.
* Computing resource consumption.
* Carbon emissions.
* Water consumption.
* Hardware requirements.
* Electronic waste.
* Infrastructure lifecycle impacts.

The assessment should provide decision-makers with sufficient information to determine whether the AI system can be deployed responsibly.

---

# 03. Why Privacy Is Different for AI

Traditional information systems generally process data according to defined rules.

AI can introduce additional complexity because systems may:

* Infer information that was not explicitly provided.
* Combine information from multiple sources.
* Generate predictions about individuals.
* Process unstructured information.
* Learn statistical relationships.
* Produce outputs that reveal sensitive characteristics.

For example:

```text
Known Data
   ↓
AI Processing
   ↓
Statistical Relationship
   ↓
Inference
   ↓
Potentially Sensitive Conclusion
```

The resulting privacy concern may therefore arise from the **inference**, not merely from the original dataset.

---

# 04. Why Environmental Impact Is Relevant to AI

AI systems can require substantial computational infrastructure.

Environmental considerations may arise from:

* Model training.
* Fine-tuning.
* Inference.
* Data storage.
* Networking.
* Cooling.
* Hardware manufacturing.
* Hardware replacement.
* Data-center operations.

The assessment should therefore consider the AI lifecycle rather than only the software itself.

---

# 05. AI Lifecycle Perspective

A useful conceptual model is:

```text
Design
  ↓
Development
  ↓
Training
  ↓
Testing
  ↓
Deployment
  ↓
Inference / Operation
  ↓
Maintenance
  ↓
Retirement
```

Privacy and environmental impacts may occur at every stage.

---

# 06. Privacy Impact Assessment

A Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA) is a structured process for identifying and addressing privacy risks associated with the processing of personal information.

Depending on the jurisdiction and circumstances, organizations may also use a **Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)** or equivalent assessment.

The terminology and legal requirements depend on the applicable regulatory environment.

---

# 07. Core Privacy Question

The fundamental question is:

> **What happens to individuals because of the way the AI system collects, uses, analyzes, stores, shares, or generates information about them?**

This shifts the assessment from simply asking:

> "Is the database secure?"

to:

> "How does the AI processing affect individuals' privacy?"

---

# 08. Privacy Is More Than Confidentiality

Privacy and security overlap, but they are not identical.

### Security asks:

> How do we protect information?

### Privacy asks:

> Should the information be collected or processed in this way, and what rights and interests of individuals are affected?

For example:

```text
Strong Encryption
       ↓
Information is well protected
```

But:

```text
Information was collected
without an appropriate purpose
       ↓
Privacy concern remains
```

Strong security does not automatically mean compliant or responsible processing.

---

# 09. Privacy Impact Dimensions

A privacy assessment can examine:

* Collection.
* Purpose.
* Necessity.
* Proportionality.
* Data minimization.
* Accuracy.
* Transparency.
* Access.
* Retention.
* Sharing.
* International transfers.
* Individual rights.
* Security.
* Accountability.

---

# 10. Data Collection

Determine:

* What information is collected?
* Why is it collected?
* Who collects it?
* From whom?
* When is it collected?
* Is collection necessary?
* Is the amount of information proportionate?

The principle is:

> **Do not collect more information than is reasonably necessary for the defined purpose.**

---

# 11. AI Data Sources

AI systems may receive information from multiple sources:

```text
Customer Data
      +
Employee Data
      +
Public Data
      +
Operational Data
      +
Third-Party Data
      ↓
AI System
```

The combination itself may create additional privacy considerations.

---

# 12. Data Aggregation

Information that appears harmless individually may become sensitive when combined.

Example:

```text
Age
+
Location
+
Job
+
Transaction History
+
Online Activity
       ↓
Combined Profile
```

The resulting profile may reveal considerably more about an individual than any single data element.

---

# 13. Data Inference

AI can generate inferred information.

For example:

```text
Behavioral Data
      ↓
AI Analysis
      ↓
Predicted Preference
      ↓
Individual Profile
```

The individual may never have explicitly provided the inferred characteristic.

This creates an important privacy consideration.

---

# 14. Sensitive Inference

Potentially sensitive inferences may concern:

* Health.
* Financial circumstances.
* Personal preferences.
* Behavioral characteristics.
* Identity-related characteristics.
* Location patterns.
* Other sensitive attributes.

The assessment should determine whether the AI is capable of generating such inferences.

---

# 15. Purpose Specification

The organization should define:

> **What is the AI processing personal information for?**

Examples:

* Customer support.
* Fraud prevention.
* Service personalization.
* Document classification.
* Internal productivity.
* Security monitoring.

The purpose should be sufficiently clear to evaluate whether the processing is appropriate.

---

# 16. Purpose Limitation

Information collected for one purpose should not automatically be reused for an unrelated purpose.

Example:

```text
Customer Support Data
       ↓
Original Purpose
Customer service
```

Later:

```text
Customer Support Data
       ↓
Unrelated AI profiling
```

The second use may require separate privacy analysis.

---

# 17. Data Minimization

Ask:

> **Does the AI actually need all of this information?**

For example, if an AI assistant only needs:

* Customer issue.
* Product type.
* Support history.

it may not need:

* Full payment information.
* Complete identity records.
* Unrelated personal information.

Reducing unnecessary data reduces privacy exposure.

---

# 18. Necessity

The assessment should distinguish between:

> **Useful data**

and

> **Necessary data**

An AI model may benefit from more information statistically, but that does not automatically mean the organization should process it.

---

# 19. Proportionality

Consider whether the benefits of processing are proportionate to the privacy impact.

Conceptually:

```text
Business Benefit
       ↕
Privacy Intrusion
```

A highly intrusive processing activity requires stronger justification and safeguards.

---

# 20. Transparency

Individuals should generally be able to understand relevant aspects of how their information is processed, subject to applicable law.

The assessment should consider:

* What information is provided to individuals.
* When information is provided.
* Whether AI processing is disclosed where appropriate.
* Whether explanations are understandable.
* Whether privacy notices remain accurate.

---

# 21. AI Transparency Challenges

AI can complicate transparency because individuals may not know:

* That AI is being used.
* What data is being processed.
* How their information influences outputs.
* Whether their information is used for training.
* Who receives the information.

Therefore:

> **AI transparency should be considered as part of the privacy assessment.**

---

# 22. Individual Control

The assessment should consider whether individuals have appropriate mechanisms relating to their information.

Depending on applicable law, these may include rights concerning:

* Access.
* Correction.
* Deletion.
* Restriction.
* Objection.
* Data portability.
* Other legally applicable rights.

The exact rights depend on the jurisdiction and processing context.

---

# 23. Data Accuracy

Incorrect personal information can produce incorrect AI outputs.

Example:

```text
Incorrect Personal Data
       ↓
AI Processing
       ↓
Incorrect Profile
       ↓
Incorrect Recommendation
       ↓
Individual Impact
```

Therefore, data quality can become a privacy and AI governance issue.

---

# 24. Data Retention

The assessment should determine:

* How long information is retained.
* Why it is retained.
* Where it is retained.
* Who controls retention.
* How deletion occurs.
* Whether backups retain information.
* Whether AI systems or providers retain prompts and outputs.

---

# 25. AI-Specific Retention Questions

Ask:

> Are prompts stored?

> Are outputs stored?

> Is conversation history retained?

> Is data used for model improvement?

> Are logs retained?

> Can data be deleted from downstream systems?

These questions should be answered before deployment where relevant.

---

# 26. Third-Party AI Providers

External AI providers create additional privacy considerations.

The assessment should examine:

* Provider role.
* Data processing responsibilities.
* Data location.
* Data retention.
* Subprocessors.
* Security controls.
* Contractual obligations.
* International transfers.
* Data-use restrictions.

---

# 27. Data Processing Chain

An AI service may involve:

```text
Organization
     ↓
AI Application
     ↓
AI Provider
     ↓
Cloud Infrastructure
     ↓
Subprocessor
```

Privacy assessment should consider the relevant processing chain rather than stopping at the primary vendor.

---

# 28. Cross-Border Data Transfer

AI services may process information in multiple countries.

The assessment should identify:

* Where data originates.
* Where it is transmitted.
* Where it is processed.
* Where it is stored.
* Which providers have access.

Cross-border processing may introduce additional legal and governance requirements.

---

# 29. Privacy by Design

Privacy should be incorporated into the system from the beginning.

Rather than:

```text
Build AI
   ↓
Deploy AI
   ↓
Discover Privacy Issues
```

prefer:

```text
Business Requirement
       ↓
Privacy Requirements
       ↓
Architecture
       ↓
Implementation
       ↓
Testing
       ↓
Deployment
```

Privacy becomes an engineering and governance requirement rather than a late-stage compliance exercise.

---

# 30. Privacy Controls

Potential controls include:

* Data minimization.
* Access controls.
* Encryption.
* Pseudonymization.
* Anonymization where appropriate.
* Retention controls.
* Data-loss prevention.
* Privacy notices.
* Consent mechanisms where legally applicable.
* Data-subject request processes.
* Vendor controls.
* Audit logging.

The appropriate control depends on the identified privacy risk.

---

# 31. Pseudonymization

Pseudonymization replaces direct identifiers with another identifier.

Conceptually:

```text
Jonathan Morales
       ↓
Customer ID 78452
```

The information may still be considered personal data under applicable privacy laws if re-identification remains possible.

Therefore:

> **Pseudonymization reduces exposure but does not necessarily eliminate privacy obligations.**

---

# 32. Anonymization

Anonymization aims to prevent information from being linked back to an identifiable individual in a sufficiently robust manner.

The assessment should not assume that simply removing names makes data anonymous.

For AI systems, re-identification risk should be carefully considered.

---

# 33. Privacy Threats to Consider

Potential AI privacy threats include:

* Unauthorized data access.
* Prompt leakage.
* Sensitive information appearing in outputs.
* Membership inference.
* Model inversion.
* Re-identification.
* Excessive retention.
* Unauthorized secondary use.
* Data poisoning involving personal information.
* Inappropriate profiling.

These threats should be assessed according to the actual architecture and use case.

---

# 34. Environmental Impact Assessment

Environmental assessment considers the ecological consequences associated with the AI system.

Potential dimensions include:

* Energy use.
* Carbon emissions.
* Water use.
* Hardware.
* Data-center infrastructure.
* Resource consumption.
* Electronic waste.

---

# 35. Energy Consumption

AI workloads require computing resources.

Energy consumption can occur during:

### Training

Large-scale computation used to develop or adapt models.

### Inference

Computational resources used whenever the model generates an output.

### Supporting infrastructure

Storage, networking, cooling, and other infrastructure also consume resources.

---

# 36. Training Versus Inference

Conceptually:

```text
Training
High computational workload
       ↓
Model creation / adaptation

Inference
Repeated computational workload
       ↓
Ongoing AI operation
```

A model with relatively modest training requirements can still create substantial operational resource consumption if it is used at very large scale.

---

# 37. AI Usage Volume

Environmental impact can be influenced by:

* Number of users.
* Number of requests.
* Request complexity.
* Model size.
* Context size.
* Frequency of inference.
* Processing duration.
* Infrastructure efficiency.

Therefore, environmental assessment should consider actual usage patterns.

---

# 38. Model Selection

A larger model is not automatically the most appropriate model.

Organizations may evaluate:

```text
Business Requirement
       ↓
Required AI Capability
       ↓
Appropriate Model
       ↓
Computational Requirement
```

Using a model substantially larger than necessary may increase resource consumption without proportionate business benefit.

---

# 39. AI Efficiency

Potential efficiency approaches include:

* Appropriate model selection.
* Efficient inference.
* Caching.
* Request optimization.
* Batch processing where appropriate.
* Reducing unnecessary computation.
* Hardware optimization.
* Efficient infrastructure.

The goal is not simply to minimize computing.

The goal is to achieve the required business outcome efficiently.

---

# 40. Data-Center Impact

AI infrastructure may require:

* Servers.
* GPUs or other accelerators.
* Storage.
* Networking.
* Cooling.
* Power infrastructure.

Environmental impact can therefore extend beyond the model itself.

---

# 41. Water Consumption

Data-center operations may involve water consumption, particularly through cooling systems.

The relevance and magnitude depend on:

* Data-center design.
* Cooling technology.
* Geographic location.
* Climate.
* Workload.
* Infrastructure efficiency.

Where material, this can be considered within environmental impact assessment.

---

# 42. Hardware Lifecycle

AI requires physical infrastructure.

Consider:

```text
Manufacturing
     ↓
Transportation
     ↓
Deployment
     ↓
Operation
     ↓
Maintenance
     ↓
Replacement
     ↓
End-of-Life
```

Environmental impact can occur throughout this lifecycle.

---

# 43. Electronic Waste

Rapid hardware evolution may lead to replacement of:

* GPUs.
* Servers.
* Networking equipment.
* Storage systems.

The organization should consider appropriate asset lifecycle and disposal practices.

---

# 44. Renewable Energy Considerations

Where environmental reporting is relevant, organizations may consider:

* Energy source.
* Renewable-energy procurement.
* Data-center energy efficiency.
* Provider environmental reporting.
* Emissions factors.

The assessment should rely on credible provider or organizational data rather than unsupported assumptions.

---

# 45. Environmental Data Quality

Environmental metrics can be difficult to calculate precisely.

Potential data sources include:

* Cloud provider sustainability information.
* Data-center metrics.
* Internal energy measurements.
* Hardware specifications.
* AI workload measurements.
* Organizational sustainability reporting.

Where precise information is unavailable, assumptions should be clearly documented.

---

# 46. Privacy and Environmental Impact Are Different Dimensions

They should not be combined into one generic score without justification.

For example:

```text
Privacy Impact       = High
Environmental Impact = Moderate
```

A system may have significant privacy implications but relatively low environmental impact.

Another may have low privacy exposure but substantial computational requirements.

---

# 47. Combined Assessment Model

A broader assessment can therefore be structured as:

```text
                 AI SYSTEM
                     ↓
       ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
       ↓                           ↓
    PRIVACY                    ENVIRONMENT
       ↓                           ↓
 Personal Data                Energy
 Individuals                 Emissions
 Rights                      Water
 Transparency                Hardware
 Retention                   E-Waste
       ↓                           ↓
       └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                     ↓
             Overall AI Assessment
```

The dimensions remain analytically distinct.

---

# 48. Stakeholder Perspective

Privacy impact primarily concerns:

* Individuals.
* Customers.
* Employees.
* Data subjects.
* Organizations.
* Regulators.

Environmental impact may concern:

* Communities.
* Society.
* Ecosystems.
* Future resource availability.
* Organizational sustainability objectives.

The assessment therefore has a broader stakeholder perspective.

---

# 49. Governance Perspective

From a GRC perspective, the assessment should connect:

```text
AI Use Case
   ↓
Privacy Exposure
   +
Environmental Exposure
   ↓
Controls / Safeguards
   ↓
Evidence
   ↓
Residual Impact
   ↓
Governance Decision
```

The objective is responsible decision-making.

---

# 50. Key Principles to Remember

### Privacy

1. Know what personal information the AI processes.
2. Understand why it is processed.
3. Minimize unnecessary information.
4. Consider inference as well as explicit data.
5. Assess transparency.
6. Consider individual rights.
7. Evaluate retention.
8. Assess third-party processing.
9. Identify cross-border transfers.
10. Apply privacy by design.

### Environment

11. Consider the entire AI lifecycle.
12. Assess computational requirements.
13. Consider training and inference.
14. Consider workload volume.
15. Consider infrastructure.
16. Consider energy.
17. Consider water where material.
18. Consider hardware lifecycle.
19. Consider electronic waste.
20. Use credible environmental data and document assumptions.

---


### Core GRC Anchor

> **"Privacy and environmental impact assessment expands AI governance beyond traditional cybersecurity. For privacy, I examine what personal information the AI collects, processes, infers, stores, and shares, why that processing is necessary, how individuals may be affected, and whether appropriate controls exist around minimization, transparency, retention, access, rights, security, and third-party processing. For environmental impact, I consider the AI lifecycle, including training, inference, infrastructure, energy consumption, water where material, hardware requirements, and end-of-life considerations. I keep privacy and environmental impacts analytically distinct, use evidence wherever possible, document assumptions where data is uncertain, and use the results to support proportionate and accountable AI governance decisions."**
