# 06.01 — AI Risk Management Foundations

## Part 1 — Foundations and Core Concepts

### 1. Definition of AI Risk Management

AI risk management is the systematic process of identifying, analyzing, evaluating, treating, monitoring, and communicating risks arising from the development, deployment, operation, use, modification, and retirement of artificial intelligence systems.

It applies traditional enterprise risk-management principles while accounting for characteristics that are specific to AI.

A simplified model is:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Potential Risk
    ↓
Risk Assessment
    ↓
Risk Treatment
    ↓
Residual Risk
    ↓
Monitoring
    ↺
```

The objective is not to eliminate every AI risk.

The objective is to ensure that AI risks are **understood, controlled, monitored, and managed within the organization's defined risk appetite**.

---

### 2. Why AI Risk Management Is Different

Traditional information-security risk management often focuses on assets, vulnerabilities, threats, and impacts.

AI introduces additional dimensions.

For example:

```text
Traditional System
    ↓
Threat
    ↓
Vulnerability
    ↓
Exploit
    ↓
Impact
```

AI systems may additionally involve:

```text
Data
 ↓
Model
 ↓
Training
 ↓
Inference
 ↓
Human Interaction
 ↓
Autonomous Behavior
 ↓
Decision / Action
```

Consequently, AI risk management must consider both **technical security risks** and broader risks associated with how AI behaves, makes recommendations, influences decisions, and interacts with people and organizations.

---

### 3. AI Risk Is Broader Than AI Security Risk

One of the most important distinctions is:

> **AI security risk is a subset of the broader AI risk landscape.**

AI risk can include:

* Cybersecurity risk.
* Privacy risk.
* Data risk.
* Model risk.
* Operational risk.
* Legal risk.
* Regulatory risk.
* Financial risk.
* Reputational risk.
* Human-impact risk.
* Social-impact risk.
* Environmental risk.

Therefore:

```text
AI Risk
├── Security Risk
├── Privacy Risk
├── Data Risk
├── Model Risk
├── Operational Risk
├── Legal / Regulatory Risk
├── Financial Risk
├── Human Risk
└── Environmental Risk
```

A cybersecurity team may manage some AI risks, but it cannot independently own the entire AI risk-management function.

---

### 4. AI Risk Management and Enterprise Risk Management

AI risk management should be integrated with the organization's existing enterprise risk-management structure.

Rather than creating an isolated AI risk universe:

```text
Enterprise Risk Management
          │
    ┌─────┼─────┐
    ↓     ↓     ↓
 Cyber  Privacy  AI
 Risk    Risk   Risk
```

AI risks should connect to the organization's broader risk taxonomy, risk appetite, risk registers, controls, reporting, and escalation processes.

This allows management to view AI risk alongside other material enterprise risks.

---

### 5. AI Risk Management Objectives

A mature AI risk-management program should aim to:

1. Identify AI systems and their intended purposes.
2. Understand potential harms and consequences.
3. Identify threats, vulnerabilities, and failure modes.
4. Evaluate the likelihood and impact of risks.
5. Implement proportionate controls.
6. Establish accountability.
7. Monitor AI systems throughout their lifecycle.
8. Detect changes in risk.
9. Communicate material risks to decision-makers.
10. Continuously improve risk-management practices.

---

### 6. The AI Risk Management Lifecycle

A basic lifecycle can be represented as:

```text
Identify
   ↓
Analyze
   ↓
Evaluate
   ↓
Treat
   ↓
Monitor
   ↓
Communicate
   ↺
```

The process is iterative.

A risk that was acceptable during initial deployment may become unacceptable after:

* A model change.
* A new data source.
* A new vulnerability.
* A new regulatory requirement.
* A change in business use.
* Increased system autonomy.

Therefore, AI risk assessment should not be considered a one-time activity.

---

### 7. Risk Identification

Risk identification asks:

> **What could go wrong?**

For an AI system, this may include:

* Incorrect outputs.
* Hallucinated information.
* Data leakage.
* Unauthorized access.
* Prompt injection.
* Model manipulation.
* Biased outcomes.
* Unsafe recommendations.
* Excessive autonomous actions.
* Vendor failure.
* Regulatory non-compliance.

The objective is to establish a sufficiently comprehensive risk picture before determining how risks should be treated.

---

### 8. Risk Analysis

Risk analysis asks:

> **How serious is the risk and how likely is it to occur?**

A simple model is:

```text
Risk = Likelihood × Impact
```

For example:

```text
Likelihood = Medium
Impact     = High

Overall Risk = High
```

Actual enterprise methodologies may use more sophisticated scoring models.

Risk analysis should consider both the probability of an event and the consequences if it occurs.

---

### 9. Risk Evaluation

Risk evaluation asks:

> **Is the identified risk acceptable?**

The result of risk analysis is compared against:

* Risk appetite.
* Risk tolerance.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Business objectives.
* Security requirements.
* Management thresholds.

For example:

```text
Calculated Risk: High
       ↓
Risk Appetite: Medium
       ↓
Decision:
Additional Treatment Required
```

Risk evaluation therefore connects technical assessment with management decision-making.

---

### 10. Risk Treatment

Risk treatment determines what the organization should do about the risk.

Common approaches include:

```text
Avoid
Reduce
Transfer
Accept
```

Treatment may involve:

* Technical controls.
* Process controls.
* Contractual controls.
* Governance requirements.
* Monitoring.
* Human oversight.
* Restricting the AI use case.
* Discontinuing the system.

Treatment should be proportionate to the risk.

---

### 11. Residual Risk

Risk does not normally disappear after controls are implemented.

For example:

```text
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Security Controls
     ↓
Human Oversight
     ↓
Monitoring
     ↓
Residual Risk
```

Residual risk represents the remaining exposure after controls and other treatments are considered.

Management must determine whether that residual risk is acceptable.

---

### 12. Inherent Risk

Inherent risk is the level of risk before considering the effect of controls or other risk treatments.

Example:

> An AI assistant processes confidential customer information through an external service.

Before considering controls, the organization may determine that the inherent confidentiality risk is high.

This establishes the baseline for evaluating treatment effectiveness.

---

### 13. Control Effectiveness

Controls should not simply exist on paper.

The organization should determine whether controls:

* Are properly designed.
* Are implemented.
* Operate consistently.
* Produce the intended outcome.

For example:

```text
Control Exists
     ↓
Control Implemented
     ↓
Control Operating
     ↓
Control Effective
```

This distinction is essential for meaningful AI risk management.

---

### 14. Residual Risk and Management Decision

After controls are evaluated:

```text
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Control Effectiveness
     ↓
Residual Risk
     ↓
Management Decision
```

Management may decide to:

* Accept the risk.
* Require additional controls.
* Restrict the AI system.
* Transfer some risk.
* Avoid the use case.

The decision should be documented and attributable to an appropriate risk owner.

---

### 15. AI Risk Appetite

Risk appetite defines the amount and type of risk an organization is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives.

For AI, risk appetite might address:

* Confidentiality.
* Accuracy.
* Availability.
* Privacy.
* Regulatory compliance.
* Human impact.
* Autonomous decision-making.

For example, an organization may have a very low tolerance for AI systems making autonomous decisions that could materially affect individuals.

---

### 16. AI Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance provides more specific boundaries around acceptable variation.

For example:

```text
Risk Appetite:
Low tolerance for sensitive-data exposure.

Risk Tolerance:
No unauthorized external disclosure of restricted customer data.
```

Risk appetite provides the strategic boundary.

Risk tolerance translates that boundary into more operational terms.

---

### 17. AI Risk Owner

Every material AI risk should have an accountable risk owner.

The risk owner is responsible for ensuring that the risk is:

* Understood.
* Assessed.
* Treated.
* Monitored.
* Reported.
* Escalated when necessary.

The AI system owner and risk owner may be the same person, but they do not have to be.

---

### 18. AI System Owner

The AI system owner is accountable for the system itself.

Responsibilities may include:

* Business purpose.
* System lifecycle.
* User population.
* Operational performance.
* Required assessments.
* Control implementation.
* Change management.
* Retirement.

The owner should understand both the business value and risk profile of the system.

---

### 19. Separation of Responsibilities

AI governance should avoid excessive concentration of responsibilities.

For example:

```text
AI System Owner
       ↓
Operates / Manages AI

Security
       ↓
Assesses Security

Risk / GRC
       ↓
Challenges / Oversees Risk

Internal Audit
       ↓
Provides Independent Assurance
```

This separation supports stronger governance and independent challenge.

---

### 20. AI Risk Communication

Risk management is not complete unless relevant information reaches decision-makers.

Risk communication should explain:

* What the AI system does.
* What could go wrong.
* How serious the risk is.
* What controls exist.
* What residual risk remains.
* Who owns the risk.
* What decision is required.

Technical findings should be translated into business consequences.

---

### 21. Technical Risk vs Business Risk

Consider:

> "The AI agent has excessive permissions."

This is a technical security finding.

A GRC translation would be:

> "The AI agent's excessive privileges could allow unauthorized access to restricted enterprise resources if the agent or its control interface is compromised."

An executive translation could be:

> "The current design creates a material risk of unauthorized access to sensitive business information and requires additional access-control safeguards."

The underlying issue is the same, but the communication is adapted to the audience.

---

### 22. AI Risk Communication Flow

A useful model is:

```text
Technical Finding
       ↓
Risk Scenario
       ↓
Business Impact
       ↓
Residual Risk
       ↓
Management Decision
```

This allows technical teams and executives to operate from the same risk picture.

---

### 23. AI Risk Context

Risk cannot be evaluated in isolation.

The organization should understand the context in which AI operates.

Important context includes:

* Intended purpose.
* Business process.
* Users.
* Data.
* Geographic scope.
* Regulatory environment.
* System dependencies.
* Level of autonomy.
* Human oversight.
* Potential affected individuals.

The same model may have very different risk levels depending on how it is used.

---

### 24. AI Use Case Matters

Consider two uses of the same generative AI model.

**Use Case A:**

> Drafting internal meeting notes.

**Use Case B:**

> Supporting decisions that materially affect customers.

The underlying model may be identical.

The risk profiles are not.

Therefore:

> **AI risk should be assessed in the context of intended use, not solely based on the model itself.**

---

### 25. AI Lifecycle Risk

Risk can change throughout the AI lifecycle.

```text
Development
     ↓
Testing
     ↓
Deployment
     ↓
Operation
     ↓
Modification
     ↓
Retirement
```

Different lifecycle stages introduce different risks.

For example:

**Development:** Data and model risks.

**Deployment:** Configuration and access risks.

**Operation:** Abuse and performance risks.

**Modification:** Change-related risks.

**Retirement:** Data, access, and dependency risks.

---

### 26. AI Risk Is Dynamic

AI risk should be treated as dynamic rather than static.

Risk may change because:

* Threat actors develop new techniques.
* Models become more capable.
* New integrations are added.
* Data changes.
* Users change.
* Regulations change.
* Vendors change their services.

Therefore:

```text
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Deployment
      ↓
Monitoring
      ↓
Change
      ↓
Reassessment
```

---

### 27. Human Oversight as a Risk Control

Human oversight can reduce certain AI risks.

For example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Business Decision
```

However, human oversight is not automatically effective.

The organization should determine:

* Who reviews the output?
* What are they expected to verify?
* Do they have sufficient expertise?
* Can they override the AI?
* Is the review documented?
* Are reviewers likely to over-trust the AI?

Effective human oversight must be designed, not merely declared.

---

### 28. Automation and Risk

Increasing AI autonomy can increase the potential consequences of failure.

A simple progression is:

```text
AI Provides Information
        ↓
AI Provides Recommendation
        ↓
AI Initiates Action
        ↓
AI Executes Autonomous Action
```

As autonomy increases, organizations may require stronger:

* Authorization.
* Monitoring.
* Human oversight.
* Testing.
* Fail-safe mechanisms.
* Approval requirements.

---

### 29. AI Risk Controls

Controls can be categorized broadly as:

**Preventive**

Designed to prevent an undesirable event.

**Detective**

Designed to identify an event.

**Corrective**

Designed to restore or correct conditions.

Example:

```text
Prevent:
Least Privilege

Detect:
AI Security Monitoring

Correct:
Incident Response
```

A mature AI risk-management program uses appropriate combinations of these controls.

---

### 30. Proportionality Principle

AI governance should be proportionate to risk.

A low-risk internal productivity tool should not necessarily receive the same governance burden as a critical autonomous AI system.

A practical model is:

```text
Low Risk
→ Standard Controls

Medium Risk
→ Enhanced Controls

High Risk
→ Formal Assessment + Approval

Critical Risk
→ Enhanced Assurance + Executive Oversight
```

This allows organizations to manage risk without unnecessarily obstructing AI adoption.

---

### 31. Risk-Based AI Governance

The principle can be expressed as:

> **Higher potential impact and uncertainty generally require stronger governance, controls, monitoring, and assurance.**

This principle helps organizations prioritize limited security and GRC resources.

---

### 32. AI Risk Management as a Shared Responsibility

AI risk cannot be owned by a single department.

Relevant stakeholders may include:

* Executive management.
* Business owners.
* AI developers.
* Cybersecurity.
* GRC.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Compliance.
* Data governance.
* Procurement.
* IT operations.
* Internal audit.

Each stakeholder contributes a different perspective.

---

### 33. Three Lines Perspective

AI risk management can be aligned with the Three Lines Model.

```text
First Line
Business / Technology
→ Own and manage risks

Second Line
Risk / Compliance / Security
→ Oversight and challenge

Third Line
Internal Audit
→ Independent assurance
```

This model helps clarify accountability and independence.

---

### 34. AI Risk Governance Committee

A governance committee may oversee significant AI risks.

Responsibilities can include:

* Reviewing high-risk AI systems.
* Reviewing material risk assessments.
* Reviewing exceptions.
* Reviewing significant incidents.
* Monitoring risk trends.
* Escalating material risks.
* Approving strategic risk decisions.

The committee should have appropriate representation from business, technology, security, risk, privacy, and legal functions.

---

### 35. AI Risk Decision-Making

A mature risk-management process connects assessment to action:

```text
Risk Identified
      ↓
Risk Analyzed
      ↓
Risk Evaluated
      ↓
Treatment Selected
      ↓
Controls Implemented
      ↓
Residual Risk Determined
      ↓
Risk Decision
```

The process should produce a clear decision rather than simply a risk score.

---

### 36. Risk Assessment Evidence

Risk decisions should be supported by evidence.

Evidence may include:

* Architecture diagrams.
* Data-flow diagrams.
* Threat models.
* Security assessments.
* Privacy assessments.
* Model documentation.
* Vendor assessments.
* Testing results.
* Control evidence.
* Approval records.

Evidence allows the organization to demonstrate how a risk decision was reached.

---

### 37. Risk Documentation

A useful AI risk record should answer:

```text
What is the AI system?
What is its purpose?
What could go wrong?
Who could be affected?
What is the likelihood?
What is the impact?
What controls exist?
What residual risk remains?
Who owns the risk?
What decision was made?
```

Good documentation supports governance, audit, and future reassessment.

---

### 38. AI Risk Monitoring

Risk monitoring should determine whether assumptions remain valid.

Monitoring may include:

* Security events.
* AI incidents.
* Control failures.
* Model changes.
* Data changes.
* Vendor changes.
* Risk indicators.
* Regulatory changes.

Monitoring allows organizations to detect when reassessment is necessary.

---

### 39. Risk Escalation

Material AI risks should be escalated when they exceed established thresholds.

Examples include:

```text
Risk > Appetite
Critical Control Failure
Material AI Incident
Significant Data Exposure
Major Model Change
Regulatory Breach
```

Escalation should follow predefined governance procedures.

---

### 40. Foundations Mental Model

The foundation of AI risk management can be remembered as:

```text
KNOW THE AI
     ↓
UNDERSTAND THE CONTEXT
     ↓
IDENTIFY WHAT CAN GO WRONG
     ↓
ANALYZE LIKELIHOOD & IMPACT
     ↓
COMPARE WITH RISK APPETITE
     ↓
TREAT THE RISK
     ↓
MEASURE RESIDUAL RISK
     ↓
MONITOR & COMMUNICATE
     ↺
```

The central principle is:

> **AI risk management is the disciplined process of making informed decisions about uncertainty and potential harm throughout the AI lifecycle.**

A strong AI risk professional does not simply ask, **"Is the AI secure?"**

They ask:

> **"What are we trying to achieve with this AI system, what could go wrong, who could be affected, how significant could the consequences be, what controls are available, what residual risk remains, and is that risk acceptable to the organization?"**


# 06.01 — AI Risk Management Foundations

## Part 2 — AI Risk Management Structure, Governance, and Enterprise Application

### 41. AI Risk Management as a Management Discipline

AI risk management should be understood as a **management discipline**, not merely a technical security activity.

Its purpose is to help the organization make informed decisions when AI introduces uncertainty.

The fundamental relationship is:

```text
Business Objective
       ↓
AI Use Case
       ↓
Uncertainty
       ↓
Potential Consequence
       ↓
Risk Decision
       ↓
Control / Treatment
```

The organization accepts some uncertainty in exchange for business value.

The role of AI risk management is to ensure that this uncertainty remains understood and controlled.

---

### 42. AI Risk Exists Because AI Introduces Uncertainty

Risk exists because future outcomes are uncertain.

For AI, uncertainty can arise from:

* Data quality.
* Model behavior.
* Training limitations.
* Unexpected inputs.
* Human interaction.
* System integrations.
* Autonomous actions.
* Third-party dependencies.
* Changing threat conditions.

Therefore:

> **AI risk management is fundamentally the management of uncertainty associated with AI-enabled outcomes.**

---

### 43. AI Risk Has Multiple Dimensions

AI risk should not be reduced to a single security score.

A useful conceptual model is:

```text
                 AI RISK
                    │
     ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
     ↓              ↓              ↓
 Technical       Business        Human
     │              │              │
     ↓              ↓              ↓
 Security       Financial       Social
 Reliability    Regulatory      Individual
 Data           Operational     Rights
```

A complete assessment considers the dimensions relevant to the particular AI use case.

---

### 44. Technical AI Risk

Technical AI risk concerns the technology itself.

Examples:

* Model failure.
* Software vulnerabilities.
* API weaknesses.
* Infrastructure compromise.
* Unauthorized access.
* Model manipulation.
* Availability failures.
* Integration failures.

These risks are usually familiar to cybersecurity and technology teams.

---

### 45. Business AI Risk

Business AI risk concerns the consequences for organizational objectives.

Examples:

* Incorrect business decisions.
* Financial loss.
* Operational disruption.
* Customer dissatisfaction.
* Loss of competitive advantage.
* Business-process failure.

A technically secure AI system can still create significant business risk if it produces unreliable decisions.

---

### 46. Human AI Risk

AI can directly or indirectly affect people.

Potential consequences include:

* Incorrect decisions.
* Unfair treatment.
* Discrimination.
* Loss of opportunity.
* Privacy intrusion.
* Excessive surveillance.
* Inappropriate automation.

This is why AI risk management extends beyond traditional information security.

---

### 47. AI Risk Context

Before assessing risk, the organization should establish the context.

Important questions include:

1. What is the AI system?
2. What is its purpose?
3. Who uses it?
4. Who is affected?
5. What decisions does it influence?
6. What data does it process?
7. What external systems does it access?
8. How autonomous is it?
9. What happens if it fails?

Without context, risk scoring can be misleading.

---

### 48. Intended Use

The intended use of AI is one of the most important risk factors.

For example:

```text
AI Model
   │
   ├── Internal brainstorming
   │
   ├── Customer-support assistance
   │
   ├── Financial analysis
   │
   └── Automated decision-making
```

The same underlying technology may have dramatically different risk depending on its application.

Therefore, AI risk should be assessed at the **use-case level**, not exclusively at the model level.

---

### 49. Reasonably Foreseeable Misuse

Risk management should consider not only intended use but also reasonably foreseeable misuse.

Examples:

* Employees entering confidential information into public AI tools.
* Users bypassing safety restrictions.
* AI outputs being used outside their intended purpose.
* Attackers manipulating inputs.
* Agents being granted excessive privileges.

This is important because real-world AI usage may differ from the original design assumptions.

---

### 50. AI System Boundaries

Risk assessment should establish what is inside and outside the AI system boundary.

For example:

```text
User
 ↓
Application
 ↓
AI Agent
 ↓
Model API
 ↓
External Provider
 ↓
Database
 ↓
Enterprise Systems
```

Each connection may introduce additional risk.

A narrow assessment that examines only the model may miss important risks elsewhere in the system.

---

### 51. AI Dependencies

AI systems commonly depend on other technologies.

Dependencies may include:

* Cloud infrastructure.
* External APIs.
* Model providers.
* Data providers.
* Identity systems.
* Databases.
* Plugins.
* Software libraries.
* Monitoring platforms.

Risk management should account for these dependencies.

---

### 52. AI Risk Ownership

Ownership should be explicit.

A simple responsibility structure may be:

| Role             | Primary Responsibility         |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Business Owner   | Business outcome and use case  |
| AI/System Owner  | System lifecycle               |
| Security         | Security risk                  |
| Privacy          | Privacy risk                   |
| Legal/Compliance | Legal and regulatory risk      |
| GRC/Risk         | Risk methodology and oversight |
| Internal Audit   | Independent assurance          |

The exact structure depends on organizational size and complexity.

---

### 53. Accountability vs Responsibility

These concepts should not be confused.

**Responsibility** means performing an activity.

**Accountability** means being ultimately answerable for the outcome.

For example:

> Security may be responsible for conducting an AI security assessment, while the business owner may remain accountable for the AI system's overall risk.

Clear accountability prevents risk from becoming "everyone's responsibility but nobody's ownership."

---

### 54. Risk Management Governance

An enterprise AI risk-management structure may look like:

```text
Board / Executive Management
          ↓
AI Governance Committee
          ↓
AI Risk / GRC Function
          ↓
Business + Technology + Security
          ↓
AI System Owners
```

Governance should provide escalation paths for material risks.

---

### 55. AI Risk Policy

An enterprise AI risk policy should establish high-level requirements such as:

* Scope.
* Definitions.
* Roles.
* Risk classification.
* Assessment requirements.
* Approval requirements.
* Risk acceptance.
* Monitoring.
* Reporting.
* Exceptions.
* Review frequency.

The policy establishes the organizational expectation.

Detailed procedures and standards operationalize it.

---

### 56. Policy, Standard, Procedure, and Guideline

These should be distinguished.

**Policy**

> What the organization requires.

**Standard**

> The mandatory level or condition that must be met.

**Procedure**

> How an activity is performed.

**Guideline**

> Recommended practice.

Example:

```text
Policy
→ All material AI systems must undergo risk assessment.

Standard
→ High-risk AI requires documented security assessment.

Procedure
→ Steps for conducting the assessment.

Guideline
→ Recommended threat-modeling techniques.
```

This hierarchy makes governance easier to operate and audit.

---

### 57. AI Risk Criteria

The organization should establish criteria for determining when formal AI risk assessment is required.

Potential triggers include:

* Sensitive data.
* External users.
* Critical business processes.
* High-impact decisions.
* Autonomous actions.
* Regulatory significance.
* High privilege.
* Large-scale deployment.
* Significant third-party dependency.

These criteria help standardize decisions.

---

### 58. Risk Classification

Risk classification allows governance to become proportional.

For example:

| Classification | Typical Governance  |
| -------------- | ------------------- |
| Low            | Standard review     |
| Moderate       | Formal assessment   |
| High           | Enhanced assessment |
| Critical       | Executive oversight |

Classification should be based on meaningful risk characteristics rather than arbitrary labels.

### 59. Risk Assessment Trigger

An AI risk assessment may be required when:

       OR

       OR
New Data
       OR
New Integration
       OR
New Business Purpose
       OR

This creates a trigger-based governance process.

### 60. Change Management and AI Risk

AI risk management should integrate with change management.

A significant change can invalidate the original risk assessment.

For example:

```text
Approved AI
     ↓
New Model Version
     ↓
New Capabilities
     ↓
New Risk Profile
```

Therefore, significant changes should trigger reassessment.

### 61. AI Risk Assessment Frequency

Periodic reviews may be appropriate, but fixed schedules alone are insufficient.

A mature approach combines:

```text
Periodic Review
       +
Event-Driven Review
```

Event-driven reassessment may occur following:

* Incidents.
* Major changes.
* New vulnerabilities.
* Regulatory changes.
* Vendor changes.
* Significant performance degradation.

---

### 62. AI Risk Assessment Inputs

A risk assessment should draw from multiple sources.

Potential inputs include:

* Business requirements.
* Architecture.
* Data-flow diagrams.
* Threat intelligence.
* Security assessments.
* Privacy assessments.
* Vendor documentation.
* Model documentation.
* Testing results.
* Historical incidents.

This produces a more complete risk picture.

---

### 63. Risk Scenario

A risk should ideally be expressed as a scenario.

A useful structure is:

```text
Threat / Cause
      +
Vulnerability / Weakness
      ↓
Risk Event
      ↓
Consequence
```

Example:

> An attacker exploits excessive permissions assigned to an AI agent, allowing unauthorized access to confidential enterprise information.

This is more actionable than simply recording:

> "AI security risk — High."

---

### 64. Cause, Event, and Consequence

A strong risk statement separates three elements.

**Cause**

What creates the possibility?

**Event**

What could happen?

**Consequence**

What happens if it occurs?

Example:

```text
Cause:
Excessive AI-agent permissions

Event:
Unauthorized agent action

Consequence:
Sensitive data exposure
```

This structure improves risk treatment.

---

### 65. Risk vs Issue

A **risk** is a potential future event or condition.

An **issue** is an existing problem.

Example:

**Risk:**

> The AI system may expose sensitive data.

**Issue:**

> Sensitive customer data has already been discovered in AI prompts.

The distinction matters because the management response differs.

---

### 66. Risk vs Incident

A risk describes potential harm.

An incident is an actual event requiring response.

```text
Risk
 ↓
Incident occurs
 ↓
Response
 ↓
Investigation
 ↓
Risk reassessment
```

AI governance should connect these processes.

---

### 67. Risk vs Vulnerability

A vulnerability is a weakness.

A risk describes the potential consequence of that weakness under particular circumstances.

Example:

**Vulnerability:**

> AI API lacks strong authorization controls.

**Risk:**

> Unauthorized users could invoke privileged AI functions, potentially causing data exposure or unauthorized system actions.

This distinction is particularly important when communicating with executives.

---

### 68. Risk vs Control

A risk is what could cause harm.

A control is what the organization does to reduce the risk.

```text
Risk
 ↓
Control
 ↓
Reduced Likelihood / Impact
 ↓
Residual Risk
```

Controls should therefore be traceable to risks.

### 69. Risk-Control Mapping

A useful GRC relationship is:

| ------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------- |

| Output errors       | Human review       | Reduce decision risk            |

This mapping allows management to understand why controls exist.

+

+

### 71. Risk Treatment Is Not Always a Technical Control

Instead:

```text

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

```

The risk is treated partly through process design.

  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

A combination may be appropriate.

For example:

* Why it is being accepted.

* Acceptance authority.
* Expiration/review date.

Risk acceptance should not simply mean:

> "We don't have time to fix it."

Therefore, risk appetite should reflect both **business ambition and organizational constraints**.

## Part 3 — Advanced AI Risk Management Concepts and Practical Application

### 81. AI Risk Assessment Should Begin With the Business Objective

AI risk cannot be properly understood without first understanding why the organization is using AI.

The assessment should establish:

* Business objective.
* Expected benefit.
* AI-enabled process.
* Intended users.
* Affected stakeholders.
* Criticality of the process.
* Consequences of failure.

A useful sequence is:

```text
Business Objective
       ↓
AI Use Case
       ↓
Business Process
       ↓
Potential Failure
       ↓
Business Impact
       ↓
AI Risk
```

This prevents the risk assessment from becoming disconnected from business reality.

---

### 82. AI Use-Case Risk Profile

The use case determines much of the AI system's risk profile.

Important characteristics include:

* Purpose.
* Decision authority.
* Level of autonomy.
* Data sensitivity.
* Number of affected individuals.
* Business criticality.
* External exposure.
* Regulatory significance.
* Potential consequences of incorrect output.

For example, an AI system used for internal document summarization generally has a different risk profile from an AI system that influences employment, financial, healthcare, or customer decisions.

---

### 83. AI Risk Drivers

Risk drivers are characteristics that increase or decrease AI risk.

Common drivers include:

```text id="4k7q4s"
Data Sensitivity
      +
Autonomy
      +
Decision Impact
      +
Scale
      +
External Exposure
      +
System Privileges
      +
Model Uncertainty
      +
Third-Party Dependency
```

These drivers should influence the depth of assessment and control requirements.

---

### 84. AI Autonomy as a Risk Driver

Autonomy deserves particular attention.

Consider:

```text id="p9z6ob"
Level 1
AI provides information

Level 2
AI provides recommendations

Level 3
AI prepares actions

Level 4
AI executes approved actions

Level 5
AI executes autonomous actions
```

As autonomy increases, the potential impact of an incorrect or manipulated AI behavior can increase.

This may justify stronger:

* Authorization.
* Human oversight.
* Monitoring.
* Testing.
* Approval.
* Recovery mechanisms.

---

### 85. AI Privilege as a Risk Driver

An AI system with access to sensitive or critical resources has a larger potential impact if compromised or misused.

For example:

```text id="xqfr7h"
AI Chatbot
→ Read-only public information
→ Lower potential impact

AI Agent
→ Customer database
→ Higher potential impact

AI Agent
→ Financial transaction system
→ Very high potential impact
```

Therefore, AI permissions should be considered during risk assessment.

---

### 86. AI Scale as a Risk Driver

Scale can amplify AI risk.

An error affecting one internal user may be relatively limited.

The same error affecting:

* 10,000 customers,
* millions of transactions,
* or a critical enterprise process

can create materially greater consequences.

A useful principle is:

> **Potential impact increases when AI decisions or outputs are distributed at scale.**

---

### 87. AI Exposure

External exposure should also be considered.

Examples include:

* Public-facing AI chatbot.
* Internet-accessible API.
* Customer-facing AI assistant.
* Internal-only AI application.
* Restricted administrative AI agent.

A public-facing AI system may have greater exposure to malicious inputs and abuse.

---

### 88. AI Data Sensitivity

Data sensitivity is another major risk driver.

A simplified classification could be:

```text id="f1v5i4"
Public
 ↓
Internal
 ↓
Confidential
 ↓
Restricted / Highly Sensitive
```

The more sensitive the data processed by AI, the stronger the expected controls may need to be.

---

### 89. AI Decision Impact

AI risk increases when AI influences decisions with significant consequences.

Examples include decisions involving:

* Employment.
* Credit.
* Insurance.
* Healthcare.
* Access to services.
* Security.
* Financial transactions.

The assessment should therefore ask:

> **What happens to a person or the organization if the AI output is wrong?**

---

### 90. AI Risk Interdependencies

AI risks rarely exist independently.

For example:

```text id="n4bqyf"
Poor Data Quality
      ↓
Incorrect Model Output
      ↓
Incorrect Business Decision
      ↓
Customer Impact
      ↓
Regulatory / Reputational Risk
```

A single underlying weakness can therefore produce multiple categories of risk.

---

### 91. Risk Cascades

AI failures can propagate across interconnected systems.

Example:

```text id="cascade01"
Compromised AI Agent
        ↓
Unauthorized Tool Invocation
        ↓
Enterprise System Access
        ↓
Data Modification
        ↓
Business Process Disruption
        ↓
Financial / Regulatory Impact
```

Risk assessments should consider these cascading effects.

---

### 92. Concentration Risk

Organizations may become heavily dependent on a single:

* AI provider.
* Cloud provider.
* Model.
* API.
* Data source.

This creates concentration risk.

For example:

```text id="concentration01"
Multiple Business Applications
            ↓
       One AI Provider
            ↓
      Provider Outage
            ↓
Multiple Business Processes Affected
```

The organization should determine whether alternative providers or fallback processes are necessary.

---

### 93. Dependency Risk

AI applications may depend on multiple components.

```text id="dependency02"
Application
 ↓
AI Framework
 ↓
Model
 ↓
API
 ↓
Cloud
 ↓
External Data
```

A failure in one dependency can affect the entire AI service.

Risk management should therefore consider the complete dependency chain.

---

### 94. Uncertainty in AI Systems

AI systems can introduce uncertainty that differs from deterministic software.

Potential sources include:

* Probabilistic outputs.
* Incomplete training data.
* Distribution changes.
* Unexpected prompts.
* Emergent behavior.
* Model updates.
* Context limitations.

Risk assessment should explicitly document material uncertainty.

---

### 95. Model Performance Risk

An AI model can operate as designed but still perform poorly for a particular context.

For example:

> A model trained on one population may perform differently when applied to another population.

Potential consequences include:

* Incorrect predictions.
* Increased error rates.
* Unequal outcomes.
* Business losses.

Model performance should therefore be evaluated against the intended operating environment.

---

### 96. Distribution Shift

The environment in which AI operates may change.

For example:

```text id="distshift"
Training Environment
        ↓
Deployment
        ↓
New Data / New Users / New Conditions
        ↓
Model Performance Changes
```

This is one reason why AI risk management must continue after deployment.

---

### 97. AI Change Risk

Changes to an AI system can alter its risk profile.

Changes may include:

* New model.
* New model version.
* New prompt architecture.
* New data.
* New tools.
* New users.
* New permissions.
* New geographic deployment.
* New business purpose.

A change-management process should determine whether reassessment is required.

---

### 98. AI Configuration Risk

AI risk is not always caused by the model.

Configuration can create significant exposure.

Examples:

* Excessive permissions.
* Insecure API configuration.
* Incorrect access settings.
* Inadequate logging.
* Unsafe tool permissions.
* Incorrect data routing.

This reinforces the importance of assessing the **AI system**, rather than only the model.

---

### 99. AI Integration Risk

Integrating AI with enterprise systems can significantly change risk.

For example:

```text id="intg01"
Standalone AI
      ↓
Limited Impact

AI + CRM
      ↓
Customer Data Access

AI + ERP
      ↓
Financial / Operational Access

AI + Privileged Infrastructure
      ↓
Potential Critical Impact
```

The risk assessment should therefore consider the systems to which AI is connected.

---

### 100. Human-AI Interaction Risk

Human users can introduce additional risk.

Examples include:

* Over-reliance on AI.
* Automation bias.
* Misinterpretation.
* Inadequate review.
* Excessive trust.
* Improper use.
* Failure to challenge AI output.

A technically well-designed system can still create risk if users misunderstand its limitations.

---

### 101. Automation Bias

Automation bias occurs when people place excessive trust in automated recommendations.

Example:

```text id="autobias"
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Assumes AI Is Correct
       ↓
No Meaningful Verification
       ↓
Incorrect Decision
```

Human oversight should therefore require meaningful evaluation rather than merely clicking an approval button.

---

### 102. Human Oversight Effectiveness

The existence of a human reviewer does not automatically reduce risk.

The organization should assess:

* Reviewer competence.
* Reviewer independence.
* Available information.
* Time available for review.
* Ability to override AI.
* Escalation mechanisms.
* Evidence of actual review.

The control should be evaluated for effectiveness, not merely existence.

---

### 103. AI Risk Control Layers

AI risk management should generally use multiple control layers.

```text id="layers01"

       ↓

       ↓
Technical Controls
       ↓

       ↓
Detective Controls
       ↓
Response Controls
       ↓

```

Layered controls reduce dependence on any single safeguard.

---

### 104. Defense in Depth for AI

Defense in depth means that failure of one control does not automatically result in catastrophic impact.

Example:

```text id="defense01"
Least Privilege
      +
Input Validation
      +
Output Filtering
      +
Human Approval
      +
Monitoring
      +
Incident Response
```

If one layer fails, other layers may reduce the resulting impact.

### 105. Preventive AI Controls

Preventive controls attempt to stop undesirable outcomes before they occur.

Examples:

* Access control.
* Least privilege.
* Approved AI tools.
* Data restrictions.
* Secure configuration.
* Input validation.
* Model restrictions.
* Network segmentation.

---

### 106. Detective AI Controls

Detective controls identify suspicious or undesirable behavior.

Examples:

* AI activity monitoring.
* Logging.
* SIEM alerts.
* Anomaly detection.
* Data-loss monitoring.
* Model performance monitoring.
* Abuse detection.

Detection is particularly important because not every AI risk can be prevented.

---

### 107. Corrective AI Controls

Corrective controls respond after an undesirable event.

Examples:

* Incident response.
* Access revocation.
* Model rollback.
* Account suspension.
* Data recovery.
* System isolation.
* Vendor escalation.

Corrective capabilities should be defined before a major incident occurs.

---

### 108. Recovery Controls

Recovery controls restore acceptable operation.

Examples:

* Backup models.
* Alternate providers.
* Manual processes.
* Model rollback.
* Data restoration.
* Disaster recovery.
* Business continuity procedures.

Critical AI systems should have appropriately tested recovery capabilities.

---

### 109. AI Risk Treatment Selection

Treatment should be based on the risk scenario.

For example:

**Risk:**

AI agent can execute unauthorized financial transactions.

Potential treatments:

```text

      +
Transaction Limits
      +
Human Approval
      +
Transaction Monitoring
      +
Emergency Disablement
```

Treatment should address the actual risk mechanism.

---

### 110. Avoiding Control Theater

A common governance weakness is implementing controls merely to demonstrate compliance.

Examples:

* Policies nobody follows.
* Reviews nobody performs.
* Risk assessments copied from templates.
* Human approval without meaningful review.
* Monitoring that generates no actionable information.

Effective AI governance should focus on **risk reduction and control effectiveness**, not paperwork volume.

The objective is to select controls that provide meaningful risk reduction.

Risk treatment should consider proportionality.

A control may reduce risk significantly but have substantial operational cost.

Management should consider:

* Risk reduction.
* Implementation cost.
* Operational impact.
* Business benefit.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Residual risk.

This supports rational risk decisions.

### 140. Practical GRC Principle

The strongest foundational principle is:

> **AI risk management should connect business objectives, AI capabilities, potential harm, controls, evidence, residual exposure, and management decisions into one traceable process.**

The risk professional's role is not simply to assign a score.

The role is to help the organization understand **what the risk means, whether it matters, what can be done about it, who owns the decision, and whether the remaining exposure is acceptable**.
